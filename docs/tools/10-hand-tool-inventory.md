# Essential Hand Tool Inventory

## Summary

This is a buying-and-keeping guide to the hand tools worth owning for repair,
small construction, and improvisation when there is no power and no supply
chain to fall back on. It is deliberately not exhaustive — a hardware store
aisle has hundreds of specialty tools that earn their keep on a job site but
not in a household kit. The list below is what actually gets reached for,
organized by task rather than by trade.

Power tools are out of scope: they depend on electricity or fuel and on
replacement parts (blades, brushes, batteries) that a hand tool does not need.
A hand tool bought once, kept sharp, and stored dry outlives the person who
bought it.

Two adjacent pages go deeper on tools this page only introduces:
[Sharpening and Tool Maintenance](20-sharpening-and-maintenance.md) covers
keeping an edge and preventing rust, and
[Basic Blacksmithing and Metal Work](30-blacksmithing-and-metalwork.md)
covers making or repairing tools from raw stock. Timber-framing-specific
tools — framing chisels, slicks, froes, boring machines — are covered in
[Joinery and Fastening Without Power Tools](../building/structure/60-joinery.md#the-hand-tool-kit)
rather than repeated here.

## Buying philosophy

- **One good tool beats three cheap ones.** A cheap tool that bends, chips, or
  strips a fastener head costs more in ruined work and lost time than the
  price difference. Buy the best example of each tool you can afford, once,
  and stop looking.
- **Forged beats cast for anything that's struck, levered, or torqued.**
  Forging aligns the metal's grain flow along the tool's shape, which is what
  gives a wrench or a pry bar its resistance to bending and cracking under
  load; a casting has no directional grain and fails without the warning a
  forging gives by bending first. Check a tool's markings or the manufacturer's
  spec sheet for "drop forged" or "forged and heat treated."
- **Know what alloy matters for.** Chrome-vanadium steel is the standard for
  wrenches, sockets, and pliers because vanadium refines the grain and adds
  toughness under repeated torque and impact — most quality mechanic's tools
  are marked "Cr-V." Edge tools (chisels, plane irons, knives, axes) are a
  different question: what matters there is carbon content and heat treatment,
  covered on the sharpening page.
- **Handle material is a real trade-off.** Wood (hickory or ash) absorbs shock
  well and can be re-handled in the field with nothing but the same species of
  wood and a rasp. Fiberglass resists breaking but cannot be repaired, only
  replaced. Steel one-piece handles are nearly indestructible but transmit
  every bit of shock to the hand. For tools meant to last decades in a kit
  with no replacement supply, wood is usually the right default — buy a spare
  handle or two along with the tool.
- **Prefer common, non-proprietary fastener drives and sizes.** A tool kit
  built entirely around one obscure standard is a liability if a single tool
  is lost. Standard sizes (SAE and metric, common screwdriver tips) mean any
  future scavenged tool is likely to be usable.

## Measuring and layout

| Tool | Notes |
|---|---|
| Tape measure, 25 ft | A locking blade tape covers most work; keep a long tape (100 ft) for site layout. |
| Folding rule or shrinking rule | Stays accurate and rigid over its length in a way a tape does not; useful for interior measurements a tape fights. |
| Combination square | Squares, measures depth, marks 45°, and its ruler detaches for use alone. The most-used layout tool in a shop. |
| Framing square | Large right-angle layout, roof and stair calculations; stamped with rafter tables on quality models. |
| Torpedo level and a 24–48 in level | Torpedo for tight spots, the longer level for anything structural. |
| Chalk line | Doubles as a plumb bob if the reel has a point, and marks long straight lines faster than any other method. |
| Marking gauge or scratch awl | For scoring a line that a pencil will not hold, especially across grain or on metal. |
| Compass/dividers | Scribing circles, stepping off equal distances, transferring irregular shapes (scribing a board to an uneven wall). |

## Cutting and sawing

| Tool | Notes |
|---|---|
| Handsaw, crosscut | General carpentry; a modern hardpoint saw stays sharp longer but cannot be resharpened — pair with a traditional taper-ground saw if long-term self-sufficiency matters. |
| Backsaw / tenon saw | Fine, straight joinery cuts; the stiffening spine controls the cut. |
| Coping saw | Curves and fine detail work, cheap thin blades that are worth stocking spares of. |
| Hacksaw | Metal cutting; buy a frame that takes standard 12 in blades in a range of tooth counts. |
| Utility knife | Fixed or retractable, with spare blades. The single most-reached-for cutting tool in most kits. |
| Sloyd or fixed-blade sheath knife | Fine wood carving, general cutting, food and rope work. High-carbon steel holds an edge well and is field-sharpenable. |
| Tin snips (straight and offset) | Cutting sheet metal, roofing, flashing. |
| Bolt cutters | Cutting rod, wire, chain, and padlocks; a long-handled pair multiplies force. |

## Striking and prying

| Tool | Notes |
|---|---|
| Claw hammer, 16–20 oz | General carpentry and demolition; a milled face grips nail heads better, a smooth face is kinder to finish work. |
| Ball-peen hammer | Metalworking — striking punches and chisels, riveting, shaping. Never use a claw hammer on metal punches; the hardened claw-hammer face can chip. |
| Small sledge (3–4 lb) | Driving stakes, breaking masonry, heavier demolition. |
| Wrecking/pry bar | Demolition, nail pulling, and leverage generally. A 24–36 in bar with both a flat chisel end and a nail-pulling claw covers most needs. |
| Flat bar (Wonder Bar type) | Finer prying — trim, flooring, shingles — where a full wrecking bar is too coarse. |
| Cold chisel set | Cutting bolts, rivets, and sheet metal, and chipping masonry or rusted fasteners free. |
| Center punch and pin punch set | Starting drill holes accurately and driving out pins and roll pins. |

Striking tools take the most abuse in a kit and are exactly where ANSI/ASME
performance and safety standards (the B107.400 series, covering hammers,
hatchets, and axes) matter in practice: they specify head hardness and
handle-retention testing so a struck face does not chip or a head does not
fly off. A tool marked to that standard, or from a manufacturer that
publishes compliance, is a meaningful quality signal.

## Fastening and driving

| Tool | Notes |
|---|---|
| Screwdriver set, flat and Phillips | Several sizes of each; a hollow-ground flat tip fits a screw slot without camming out. |
| Multi-bit / ratcheting screwdriver | Convenience for mixed fastener types, but keep dedicated full-size drivers too — bit shanks twist off under real torque. |
| Adjustable wrench, 8–10 in and 12 in | Covers odd sizes a fixed wrench set misses. |
| Combination wrench set, SAE and metric | Open end for speed, box end for grip on a stubborn or rounding fastener. |
| Socket set with ratchet, 3/8 in drive, SAE and metric | The single highest-leverage-per-pound tool investment for mechanical work. |
| Pipe wrench, 10–14 in | Threaded pipe and rounded-off fasteners generally. |
| Vise-grip / locking pliers | Clamps and holds with adjustable, repeatable pressure — doubles as a makeshift wrench on a damaged fastener. |
| Nail set | Setting finish nails below the surface without marring the wood. |

## Holding and clamping

| Tool | Notes |
|---|---|
| Bench vise, 4–6 in jaw | Bolted to a solid bench, this is the base for most metal and wood work; buy the largest one the bench can support. |
| Bar or pipe clamps, several lengths | Glue-ups and holding work under pressure. |
| C-clamps, assorted sizes | General-purpose clamping where a bar clamp is too long or too coarse. |
| Spring clamps | Quick, light-duty holding — the ones reached for most often precisely because they need no adjustment. |
| Slip-joint and needle-nose pliers | General gripping and wire work; needle-nose for tight spaces and small parts. |
| Locking chain clamp / strap wrench | Round or irregular stock a vise jaw slips on. |

## Boring

| Tool | Notes |
|---|---|
| Brace and auger bit set | Bores large-diameter holes with no electricity; auger bits self-feed via a lead screw and clear their own chips. |
| Hand drill (eggbeater style) | Small-diameter holes faster than a brace, still no power required. |
| Hand or push awl | Starting pilot holes, piercing leather and canvas. |
| Countersink | Seating screw heads flush by hand or in a brace. |

## Filing and shaping

| Tool | Notes |
|---|---|
| File set (flat, half-round, round, triangular) | Metal shaping and deburring, plus saw and blade maintenance — see the sharpening page. |
| Rasp (wood) | Fast, coarse wood shaping where a plane or file is too slow or too fine. |
| Block plane and jack plane | Flattening, fitting, and smoothing wood by hand; a block plane alone covers most household needs. |
| Wire brush | Rust, scale, and paint removal; a necessary companion to files and sharpening stones. |

## Basic metalworking hand tools

Full blacksmithing tooling — anvil, forge, tongs, hardy tools — is covered on
its own page. These are the hand tools worth keeping even without a forge, for
cold metal work and repair:

| Tool | Notes |
|---|---|
| Ball-peen hammers, small and medium | See Striking, above. |
| Bench vise | See Holding, above — a metalworking vise with hardened jaws is preferable if only one vise is owned. |
| Hacksaw and spare blades | See Cutting, above. |
| Files, cut on all faces | See Filing, above. |
| Tap and die set, SAE and metric | Repairing or cutting threads — restores a stripped bolt hole or salvaged bolt to usable condition. |
| Metal-cutting tin snips | See Cutting, above. |
| Sheet metal hand seamer / bending brake (small) | Clean bends in flashing and light sheet stock without a machine. |

## Safety

- **A dull tool is more dangerous than a sharp one.** It requires more force,
  and force is what makes an edge or a point skid off the work and into a
  hand. Keep edge tools maintained — see
  [Sharpening and Tool Maintenance](20-sharpening-and-maintenance.md).
- **Inspect before use.** OSHA's general hand tool standard (29 CFR
  1910.242(a)) puts the responsibility on the user to keep tools in safe
  condition; in practice that means checking for a mushroomed striking face,
  a cracked or loose handle, or a chipped edge before every use, and pulling
  the tool from service rather than using it "just this once."
- **Match the tool to the job.** A screwdriver is not a chisel, a wrench is
  not a hammer, and a wood chisel is not a pry bar. Misused tools are the most
  common source of hand-tool injuries — the tool slips or fails in a way it
  was never designed to resist.
- **Eye protection** for anything struck, cut, ground, or filed — chips and
  filings travel farther and faster than expected.
- **Never strike a hardened steel face against another hardened steel face**
  (for example, a hammer against a cold chisel's poll rated for a softer
  striking face). Chips fly off at high velocity.
- **Store edges sheathed or blade-down**, and keep striking tools on a rack
  rather than loose in a drawer, where a claw or a blade is the first thing a
  hand finds.
