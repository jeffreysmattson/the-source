# Salvage and Material Reuse

## Summary

When new material isn't coming, the building next door, the barn falling into
its own foundation, and the pile of "junk" in the shed are your supply chain.
Salvage is a skill, not just scavenging: it means recognizing what's worth the
labor to recover, screening it for hazards before you touch it, taking it apart
without destroying it, and storing it so it's actually usable six months later
instead of becoming an unsorted heap.

The hazard screening in this page is not optional reading. Older buildings and
equipment concentrate exactly the materials — lead, asbestos, treated wood,
old wiring insulation, rodent-contaminated enclosed spaces — that cause
irreversible harm, and salvage work is the activity most likely to disturb
them. Read that section before you pry anything loose.

## Before you touch anything: hazard screening

### Lead paint

Any structure built **before 1978** should be assumed to have lead-based paint
somewhere, and it was used more heavily the older the building is. Lead paint
itself is not the main hazard when intact; the danger is the dust and chips
created by sanding, scraping, cutting, or demolishing painted material.

- Do not dry-sand, dry-scrape, or burn painted material from a pre-1978
  structure without assuming lead until tested otherwise. EPA-recognized test
  kits give a fast field screen; lab testing is more reliable.
- If you must disturb painted surfaces, wet the surface first to keep dust
  down, use a respirator rated for lead particulate (a NIOSH N100/P100
  cartridge or filter, not a paper dust mask), and contain and dispose of
  debris rather than letting it scatter.
- Lead exposure is cumulative and especially dangerous to children and
  pregnant women — do not do this work with them nearby, and change clothes
  and wash up before contact with them afterward.

### Asbestos

Common in materials made or installed before the early 1980s:

- Vermiculite attic insulation (see
  [insulating an existing structure](../shelter/20-insulating-existing-structure.md)
  for the specific Zonolite/Libby, Montana history — treat all vermiculite as
  contaminated until proven otherwise)
- 9"×9" and 12"×12" floor tile and its black mastic
- Pipe and boiler insulation ("pipe wrap"), often a white or gray wrap or
  corrugated paper
- Cement siding and roofing shingles ("transite")
- Some old ductwork joint compound and furnace/door gaskets

Intact, undisturbed asbestos-containing material is low risk. The danger is
airborne fiber from cutting, sanding, breaking, or crumbling it — exactly what
salvage and demolition do. Asbestos fibers cause mesothelioma and other
cancers decades after exposure, with no safe threshold and no way to reverse
exposure once it happens.

- **Do not cut, sand, break, or dry-sweep suspect material.** If you must move
  it, keep it wet, keep it intact, and treat any broken or crumbled piece
  ("friable" asbestos) as an emergency stop, not something to clean up with a
  broom.
- When in doubt, don't guess — a lab test of a small sample is cheap insurance
  against a disease with no cure.
- In a true no-services situation, the fallback is containment (don't disturb
  it, seal the area) rather than removal, since safe abatement normally
  requires specialized respirators, negative-pressure containment, and
  regulated disposal.

### Treated lumber

- **Older wood (pre-2004 in the U.S. residential market)** may be CCA
  (chromated copper arsenate) treated — it contains arsenic and chromium.
  It typically shows a greenish tint that fades with age and weathering, which
  makes it easy to mistake for untreated wood once it's silvered from UV
  exposure.
- **Newer treated lumber** uses copper-based preservatives (ACQ, copper
  azole) without arsenic, but is roughly twice as corrosive to fasteners as
  CCA was — see
  [materials and spans](../building/70-materials-and-spans.md#preservative-treated-wood)
  for reuse and fastener compatibility.
- **Never burn treated wood of any kind**, indoors or out — combustion
  concentrates and releases the preservative metals in the ash and smoke. This
  applies with particular force to any wood salvaged from an unknown source: if
  you can't confirm it's untreated, don't put it in a stove, fire pit, or
  forge.
- Wear gloves when handling treated wood and a dust mask when cutting it; wash
  hands before eating.
- Treated lumber is legitimate salvage for outdoor, ground-contact, or
  structural reuse — the concern is combustion and cutting exposure, not
  handling.

### Old electrical wiring and equipment

- **Knob-and-tube wiring** (visible ceramic knobs and tubes, rubber-and-cloth
  insulated conductors run separately rather than as a sheathed cable) is
  common in construction before about 1950. The insulation is often brittle
  and cracks when flexed. Salvage the copper, but treat any wiring still
  energized or of unknown status as live until you've confirmed otherwise at
  the panel.
- **Old fluorescent light ballasts and some capacitors** manufactured before
  1979 may contain PCBs (polychlorinated biphenyls). A ballast that is
  leaking, bulging, or labeled "contains no PCBs" (a post-1979 label) tells you
  where you stand; an unlabeled, unleaking one from before that date should be
  treated as PCB-containing for disposal purposes and not opened or drained.
- Old cloth- or rubber-insulated cable is fine to strip for scrap copper once
  de-energized and confirmed dead; it is not safe to reuse as-is for new wiring
  runs.

### Biological hazards in long-closed spaces

- **Rodent droppings, nests, and urine** in barns, sheds, and closed-up
  buildings can carry hantavirus, which is transmitted by inhaling aerosolized
  particles — sweeping or vacuuming dry droppings is the dangerous action.
  CDC guidance: ventilate the space for at least 30 minutes before entering,
  avoid sweeping or vacuuming dry debris, wet everything down with a
  disinfectant solution before cleanup, and wear gloves and a respirator.
- **Mold** in water-damaged salvage material (soaked lumber, wallboard,
  insulation) is a respiratory hazard and, structurally, a sign the material's
  strength may already be compromised. Porous material that's been wet for
  more than 48 hours is generally not worth salvaging for anything but scrap
  metal fasteners.

## Salvaging fasteners

- Pull nails straight rather than bending them further; a cat's paw or flat
  bar gets under the head without chewing up the surrounding wood.
- Sort as you go, not after — a bucket of mixed nails is nearly worthless
  labor to sort later, but 30 seconds per handful into labeled bins keeps them
  usable.
- Sort by fastener type and size: common nail lengths (by penny size),
  screw length and drive type, and bolts by diameter and thread pitch (coarse
  vs. fine matters — a bolt that doesn't match the nut's pitch will cross-thread
  and gall).
- Straighten reusable nails on an anvil or hard flat steel surface; badly bent
  or rusted nails go to the scrap-metal pile instead, not back into the
  fastener bins.
- Clean rust and old paint off reusable fasteners with a wire wheel before
  storage — light surface rust left untreated will progress and can weld a
  fastener into wood or lock a nut to a bolt.

## Salvaging dimensional lumber

- Deconstruct in reverse of how it was built: trim, then finish surfaces,
  then structural members, working from the last thing installed to the
  first.
- De-nail before stacking, not after — a board with nails left in it is a hand
  and saw-blade hazard and a real risk once it's stacked with others.
- Check for rot, insect damage, and old fastener holes before deciding a board
  is worth keeping. Probe suspect areas with an awl or screwdriver tip: sound
  wood resists; rotten wood is soft or crumbles.
- **Rot and insect damage disqualify a board from structural reuse** even if
  it looks fine elsewhere — the compromised section can be well beyond what's
  visible. Rot-free, insect-free lumber can be reused structurally; anything
  else is fine for non-structural use (blocking, sheathing, forms, firewood if
  untreated).
- Re-milling: a hand plane or jointer can true a salvaged board's edges, but
  remember old lumber may carry embedded grit, old fastener fragments, or paint
  that will dull or chip a blade fast — inspect and probe with a nail-free
  sweep of a magnet before running it through anything.
- Old-growth dimensional lumber (pre-1950s framing, especially) is often
  denser and straighter-grained than anything available new, and is worth
  extra effort to salvage intact for that reason alone.

## Salvaging metal

Two quick field tests separate scrap worth keeping for smithing or fabrication
from scrap that mostly isn't:

- **Magnet test.** A strong magnet sticks firmly to plain carbon steel and
  most low-alloy steel — the bulk of what's forge-workable. It does not stick,
  or sticks only weakly, to austenitic stainless steel (most kitchen and
  fastener-grade stainless), aluminum, copper, brass, and bronze. This is a
  quick sort, not an identification of exact alloy.
- **Spark test.** Touching the metal to a grinding wheel and reading the spark
  stream gives a rough read on carbon content: long, sparse, dull red-orange
  sparks with a few forks suggest low-carbon (mild) steel; a shorter, brighter,
  more branching, star-bursting spark stream suggests higher carbon content —
  more suited to tools, edges, and springs, but also more prone to cracking if
  worked or quenched carelessly. This takes practice to read reliably and is
  not a substitute for a known-source steel when the application matters.

Full identification and heat-treatment behavior for smithing purposes belongs
on the [blacksmithing and metalwork](30-blacksmithing-and-metalwork.md) page —
this is only the field sort for "is this worth hauling home and setting aside."

Good sources of forge- and fabrication-worthy scrap: mild steel from angle
iron, rebar (usable but often inconsistent alloy — treat as unknown carbon
content), leaf springs and coil springs (high-carbon, good for edge tools but
harder to work and needs care in heat treatment), old files and machine parts
(high-carbon or tool steel, but check for chrome plating, which releases toxic
fumes when forge-heated and should be ground off first), and mild-steel plate
and pipe from appliances and furniture frames.

Non-ferrous metal — copper wire and pipe, brass fittings, aluminum stock — is
worth its own separate, sorted pile: it isn't forge stock in the blacksmithing
sense, but it's valuable for wiring, plumbing repair, and fittings, and it's
significantly more valuable as clean sorted scrap than mixed with steel.

## Salvaging glass, wire, and fixtures

- **Window glass**: worth pulling intact from a demolition if the sash or
  frame is being removed anyway. Store flat or in a rack on edge, never
  leaning at a shallow angle where it can bow and crack. Old single-pane glass
  is often a slightly different thickness than modern stock — measure before
  planning a replacement use.
- **Wire**: strip insulated copper wire for scrap value or, if the insulation
  is intact and the gauge is known, coil and label it for reuse as-is. Bare
  wire (fencing, mechanical wire) is worth keeping by gauge.
- **Plumbing fixtures and fittings**: valves, unions, and fittings salvaged
  from a shutoff system are often reusable if not corroded through — inspect
  threads carefully, since a damaged thread on a fitting will leak no matter
  how well the rest of the joint is made. Galvanized pipe fittings corrode from
  the inside and can look fine externally while being nearly closed off with
  scale — this matters for reuse in a water line, less for structural use.
- **Hardware**: hinges, latches, door and cabinet hardware are usually worth
  the few minutes to remove intact rather than pry off and damage, especially
  older hardware, which is often better made than current low-cost equivalents.

## Deconstruction technique

- **Work top-down and in reverse construction order.** Remove finish
  materials, then the layer under them, then structure — the same order the
  building went up, run backward. This keeps loads supported until you
  deliberately release them and avoids surprises.
- **Match the tool to the goal.** A flat bar, cat's paw, and reciprocating saw
  (or hand saw, hatchet, and wrecking bar without power) favor careful removal
  of intact material. A sledgehammer and demolition are for material you've
  already decided isn't worth saving, or for taking down what's structurally
  unsafe to work on piece by piece.
- **Decide "salvage vs. demolish" by labor economics, not sentiment.**
  Deconstruction takes several times longer than demolition. It's worth it for
  old-growth or hard-to-source lumber, good hardware, intact glass, and
  anything expensive or slow to replace without a supply chain. It is not
  worth it for water-damaged, rotten, or already-broken material — that goes
  straight to the scrap or burn pile (checking first for treated wood, as
  above).
- **Support what you're not ready to release.** Cutting a load-bearing member
  before it's shored, or pulling a fastener that's the only thing holding
  something up, is how deconstruction injures people. Think through what each
  member is doing before you remove it, not after it moves unexpectedly.

## Storage and organization

Salvage that isn't sorted and protected is a liability, not an asset — it
takes up space, and by the time you need it you won't remember what's in the
pile or trust its condition.

- **Sort at the point of collection**, not later. Fasteners by type and size,
  lumber by dimension and species if known, metal by ferrous/non-ferrous and
  rough alloy class, glass by size.
- **Label everything** — a bin of unlabeled mixed bolts becomes unusable
  within a season. A sharpie and masking tape is sufficient; durability matters
  more than neatness.
- **Protect from weather.** Steel stock rusts, lumber warps and rots, and
  fasteners seize if left outside uncovered. A dry, ventilated shed or covered
  rack beats a tarp, which traps condensation against the material it's
  supposedly protecting.
- **Keep lumber stickered and off the ground** — spacers between stacked
  boards and a base that lifts the stack off damp ground prevent both warping
  and rot, the same principles as green lumber storage.
- **Revisit the pile periodically.** Salvage that sits ignored for years tends
  to degrade (rust, rot, mislabeled bins) faster than it gets used — an
  annual pass to re-sort, re-oil bare metal, and discard what's degraded past
  usefulness keeps the stock actually usable.
