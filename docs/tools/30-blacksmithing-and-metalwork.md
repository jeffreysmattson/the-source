# Basic Blacksmithing and Metal Work

## Summary

Blacksmithing is hot forging: heating steel until it deforms easily, then
shaping it with hammer blows before it cools. When supply chains for hardware
are gone, it is the only practical way to fabricate new tool parts, repair
broken ones, or turn salvaged scrap into something usable — hooks, hinges,
brackets, nails, replacement axe eyes, and simple blades.

This page is an introduction, not a substitute for practice. Skill comes from
hours at the anvil, ideally with an experienced smith watching your first
sessions. What follows is the minimum to start safely and productively: the
equipment, the fire, the handful of moves that everything else is built from,
and how to harden and temper what you make.

Blacksmithing is also one of the more dangerous activities in this wiki — open
fire, red-hot metal flying off under hammer blows, and heavy tools swung
one-handed. Read [Safety](#safety) before lighting a forge for the first time.

## Materials

**Minimum kit** — forging is possible with just these four:

| Item | Notes |
|---|---|
| Forge | A solid-fuel (coal or charcoal) forge is the most achievable off-grid, since it needs no purchased gas. A simple brake-drum or firepot forge with a hand-crank or bellows blower works; propane forges are faster to light and easier to control but depend on a purchased, storable fuel. |
| Anvil | A true anvil (flat face, horn, hardy and pritchel holes) is ideal. A section of railroad rail set on end, or any large chunk of dense steel set on a stable stand, is a workable substitute for basic work. |
| Hammer(s) | A 2–3 lb cross-peen or ball-peen hammer for most work; a lighter hammer for fine work. Weight should match what you can control accurately for an hour, not the heaviest one available. |
| Tongs | At least one pair sized to the stock you work most often. Loose-fitting tongs drop hot steel; this is worth getting right. |

**Also needed:**

- Vise (post vise or heavy bench vise) for bending and filing cold work
- Quenching container: water and/or oil, in a metal container well clear of the forge
- Wire brush, for scale
- Hardy tools as you acquire them: hardy cutoff, punches, drifts — improvised at
  first from scrap tool steel
- A stable, non-combustible floor and clear space around the anvil

**Personal protective equipment:**

- Safety glasses or goggles at all times at the forge — flying scale is a
  constant, low-grade hazard
- Leather gloves for handling stock, removed for the actual hammer swing by
  many smiths for better hammer control (a matter of trained judgment, not a
  rule to start with)
- Natural-fiber clothing only (cotton, wool, leather) — no synthetics, which
  melt onto skin
- Closed-toe leather boots; long pants with no cuffs to catch sparks
- Hair tied back, no loose sleeves or jewelry

## Procedure

### 1. Build and manage the fire (solid-fuel forge)

- Start with a small bed of kindling or charcoal in the firepot, air blast low.
- Build a compact, deep fire — a "beehive" mound of coke (coal that has already
  coked, or charcoal) around and above the air inlet (the tuyere). A deep,
  concentrated fire heats evenly; a shallow, spread-out fire scorches the
  outside of the stock while the core stays cold.
- Bury the workpiece in the heart of the fire, not on top of it, and rotate it
  for even heating.
- Increase air only as needed to reach working heat — excess air wastes fuel
  and burns the steel (see temperature note below).
- Keep a bucket of water at hand to bank down flare-ups and quench small burns.

### 2. Read the color

Steel's glow color is a rough, reliable temperature gauge in ordinary shop
light (dim the shop if you can — bright daylight washes out the low end of the
scale). Approximate colors for plain carbon steel:

| Color | Approx. temperature | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Black turning dull red | ~900–1000°F (480–540°C) | Below general forging range for most steel |
| Dark cherry / cherry red | ~1300–1500°F (700–815°C) | Lower forging range; workable but stiffer |
| Bright cherry / orange | ~1500–1800°F (815–980°C) | Good general forging range for plain carbon steel |
| Bright orange / yellow | ~1800–2000°F (980–1090°C) | Upper forging range — steel moves easily |
| White, with sparks | above ~2200°F (1200°C) | **Burning.** Grain structure is being destroyed; the steel is ruined and cannot be recovered by more forging. Stop well before this. |

Work the steel while it is at orange to bright-orange heat, and return it to
the fire as soon as it dulls toward dark red rather than trying to force cold
work — this is what "strike while the iron is hot" means literally, and
fighting cold steel is how tools break and technique gets worse, not better.

### 3. Core techniques

Practice these on mild steel scrap before attempting a project.

- **Drawing out** — lengthening and thinning stock by hammering it, working
  from the center out, turning the piece a quarter turn between blows to keep
  it roughly square or round rather than flat and wide.
- **Upsetting** — the reverse: thickening a section by hammering on the end of
  a heated bar, driving material back into itself. Used to build up mass for a
  bolt head or tool shoulder.
- **Bending** — over the anvil horn for curves, over the edge or in the vise
  for sharp angles. Heat the bend area fully through; a cold spot will kink
  instead of curving smoothly.
- **Punching and drifting** — driving a punch through hot stock to start a
  hole, then enlarging it to final shape and size with a tapered drift. Punch
  most of the way from one face, then finish from the other to avoid a ragged
  "cabbage" exit hole.
- **Splitting (slitting)** — driving a chisel-like slitter through hot stock to
  open a slot, commonly opened further with a drift — the basis of forge-punched
  eyes (axe heads, hammer eyes).
- **Riveting** — the basic mechanical joint for two pieces of thin stock: a hot
  rivet is set through aligned holes and its protruding ends are hammered flat
  over washers or directly against the material, drawing the joint tight as it
  cools and shrinks.

### 4. Hardening and tempering plain carbon steel

Heat treatment only works on **known steel with enough carbon to harden** —
roughly 0.4% carbon and up (medium- and high-carbon steel, most files, leaf
springs, and old woodworking chisels). Mild steel (most construction stock,
rebar, most mystery scrap) will not harden by quenching, no matter how it is
treated, because it lacks the carbon content martensite hardening requires.
This is the main reason to know or test what steel a salvaged piece is before
building an edge tool from it — see
[Salvage and Material Reuse](40-salvage-and-material-reuse.md).

1. **Harden.** Heat the piece uniformly to non-magnetic (a magnet no longer
   sticks — this is a practical field proxy for the steel's critical
   temperature, roughly bright cherry red), then quench immediately.
   - Water quenches faster and produces harder, more brittle results, with a
     higher risk of cracking, especially in higher-carbon or thick sections.
   - Oil quenches more gently, giving somewhat lower hardness but better
     toughness and lower cracking risk — the safer default for a first attempt
     or an unfamiliar steel.
2. **Test.** A properly hardened piece should skate a file rather than let it
   bite. If the file bites, it did not harden — check temperature and quench
   speed.
3. **Temper.** Full hardness is too brittle to use directly; tempering trades
   some hardness back for toughness. Clean one face bright, then heat gently
   (in an oven, on a hot plate, or held near — not in — the fire) and watch the
   oxide color spread across the bright metal:

   | Temper color | Approx. temperature |
   |---|---|
   | Light straw | ~410–430°F (210–220°C) |
   | Straw / dark straw | ~430–450°F (220–230°C) |
   | Yellow-brown | ~460–480°F (240–250°C) |
   | Purple | ~520–530°F (270–280°C) |
   | Dark blue | ~550–570°F (290–300°C) |
   | Grey-blue | ~590–610°F (310–320°C) |

   Lower temper colors (straw) leave the steel harder and more brittle,
   appropriate for edges that must hold a keen bite; higher colors (blue) leave
   it softer and tougher, appropriate for parts that flex or take shock rather
   than cut. Quench again from the temper color to stop the process at the
   color you want.

!!! warning "Mystery steel"
    Scrap of unknown origin may be mild steel, medium-carbon steel, an alloy
    tool steel, or something with additives that make it dangerous to quench
    (spalling or even minor explosive fragmentation is possible with some
    alloy and case-hardened stock). Spark-test on a grinder against a known
    sample if you need to guess composition, treat unknown pieces cautiously,
    and never assume scrap behaves like construction-grade mild steel.

### 5. Beginner projects, roughly in order of difficulty

1. **S-hook** — draws out a taper on each end of a short bar and curls each end
   around the horn. Teaches drawing out and scrolling.
2. **Simple hook or hanger** — a longer draw with a decorative or functional
   bend and a mounting hole punched at one end.
3. **Drift punch** — forging a tool you will use for later projects; teaches
   upsetting and shaping a working point.
4. **Leaf key fob** — a flattened, textured leaf shape on a twisted stem;
   teaches spreading (fullering/flattening) and controlled thinning.
5. **Small chisel or froe from known tool steel** — the first project that
   exercises the full hardening-and-tempering sequence end to end.

## Safety

- **Burns** are the most common injury. Hot steel looks identical to cold
  steel from a distance — treat every piece near the fire as hot until you
  have confirmed otherwise, and never touch the anvil or tongs to check.
- **Eye protection is non-negotiable.** Scale (flaking oxide) is thrown off by
  every hammer blow and can travel farther than expected.
- **Fire and carbon monoxide.** A solid-fuel forge burning indoors or in an
  enclosed space produces carbon monoxide. OSHA's general industry permissible
  exposure limit for CO is 50 ppm as an 8-hour time-weighted average (29 CFR
  1910.1000, Table Z-1) — a limit meant for monitored industrial settings, not
  a safe target for an unventilated shed. Forge only with generous, moving
  fresh air (open on at least two sides, or under a hood with a flue to
  outside), and treat headache, dizziness, or nausea while forging as a sign to
  get outside immediately, not to push through.
- **Keep a fire extinguisher and a full water bucket at the forge**, and keep
  the floor and surrounding area clear of combustibles, sawdust, and fuel
  containers.
- **Never grab, catch, or improvise a stop for falling hot stock** — let it
  fall and deal with it once it has stopped moving.
- **Hammer control.** A glancing blow off the anvil edge or the workpiece can
  send the hammer flying. Keep the striking face square to the work and keep
  bystanders out of the swing radius.
- **Quenching produces steam and spatter.** Keep your face back from the
  quench tank, and keep the tank's lid or cover nearby to smother an oil
  flare-up if one occurs.
