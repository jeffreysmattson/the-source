# Sharpening and Tool Maintenance

## Summary

A tool that cannot be replaced has to be maintained instead. None of the work
on this page needs electricity — a sharp edge and a tight handle come from
stones, files, oil, and correct technique, all of which keep working after the
grid does not. Treat this page alongside
[Essential Hand Tool Inventory](10-hand-tool-inventory.md): the tools listed
there are only as good as their edges and handles.

Three failure modes account for nearly every hand tool going out of service:
a dull edge, a rusted-through edge or surface, and a loose or broken handle.
All three are preventable and, short of the metal being gone, repairable.

## Edge geometry

The angle ground onto an edge trades sharpness against durability. A shallow
angle cuts more easily but chips or rolls under lateral force; a steeper angle
survives abuse but cuts less cleanly. Match the angle to the work, not to a
single "correct" number.

| Tool | Edge angle | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Axe, felling/general | 20–25° | Ground flat or gently convex for a clean, deep bite |
| Axe, splitting | 30–40°, convex | Follow the curve of the existing bevel rather than holding one fixed angle; geometry and weight split wood, not sharpness |
| Knife, general/utility | 17–22° per side (34–44° inclusive) | Lower for fine slicing, higher for a camp or chopping knife |
| Knife, fine/slicing | 12–17° per side | Fragile edge — food prep and delicate work only |
| Bench chisel | 25° primary bevel, often honed with a 30° secondary (micro-bevel) | The secondary bevel is what actually meets the wood and is quick to renew |
| Paring chisel | 20° | No mallet use, so it can run thinner |
| Mortise chisel | 20° primary, with the last ~3/16 in. at 35° | Takes mallet blows and prying loads; the steeper secondary survives it |
| Plane iron, bevel-down | 25° primary, honed to 30–35° | Bed angle (commonly 45°) sets the effective cutting angle, not the bevel alone |
| Plane iron, bevel-up | 25° primary | Effective cutting angle is bed angle plus bevel angle — check the tool |
| Scissors/shears, household or fabric | 30–45° | Ground on one face only (single bevel); never grind the flat back |
| Hand saw, crosscut | Rake 12–15°, fleam 15–25° | Fleam files each tooth at an angle across the blade, producing a knife-point tooth that severs fibers |
| Hand saw, rip | Rake 0–8°, fleam 0° (filed straight across) | Rip teeth work like a row of tiny chisels; some filers add a few degrees of fleam only to reduce tear-out |

## Materials

- Sharpening stones covering a coarse-to-fine range (see below)
- Honing oil (for oilstones) or clean water (for waterstones) — never mix the
  two on the same stone
- A strop (leather, charged with fine abrasive compound) for edge tools and
  knives
- A flat reference surface (float glass, a machined plate, or a coarse
  diamond plate) for flattening stones
- Files: mill bastard files for axes and mowers, triangular saw files sized
  to the saw's tooth pitch, needle files for small work
- A saw set, for hand saws
- Angle guide or simple shop-made jig (a block cut to the target angle is
  enough)
- Fine abrasive pad or 0000 steel wool, penetrating oil, and a wire brush for
  rust and grime
- Boiled linseed oil, paste wax, or plain machine oil for long-term
  protection
- Wooden handle stock (hickory or ash) and steel or wood handle wedges

## Sharpening methods

Pick one primary system and learn it well rather than owning a little of
everything. All of the following work without power.

### Oilstones (novaculite, silicon carbide, aluminum oxide)

Traditional and forgiving. Lubricate with honing oil, not water — oil keeps
swarf suspended so it does not glaze the stone. Slower-cutting than
waterstones but does not need soaking and does not dish as quickly.

### Waterstones

Cut faster than oilstones because the abrasive releases fresh grit as it
wears, but they dish rapidly and most need soaking (check the specific
stone — some are splash-and-go). A practical progression for tool steel is
coarse (220–320) for repair and reprofiling, medium (1000) for general
resharpening, and fine (4000–8000) for a polished edge; add a step in between
if the jump in scratch pattern is hard to close. Flatten before and after
each use on a dedicated flattening stone or plate — a dished stone rounds
the edge it is meant to make flat.

### Diamond plates

Do not dish, cut aggressively even on very hard steel, and work dry or with
water. The most durable option for field use and the best choice for flattening
other stones. More expensive per plate, but a single coarse/fine combination
plate covers most sharpening and stone-flattening needs indefinitely.

### Files

The only practical option for axes, mower blades, and hand saws, and a
reasonable field substitute for anything else. Push-cut only (files cut in one
direction); lift on the return stroke or the teeth dull fast. A mill bastard
file handles axes and drawknives; saws need a triangular file sized to the
tooth spacing (roughly matched so the file's corners just clear the gullet).

### Stropping

The finishing step for knives and edge tools: a few light, trailing-edge
passes on leather charged with honing compound removes the microscopic wire
edge (burr) left by the stone and can restore working sharpness between full
sharpenings for weeks.

### General procedure (stones)

1. Fix the target angle — by feel, by a jig, or by resting the tool's spine
   on a stack of coins or a block cut to that angle, then lift to the bevel.
2. Work the coarsest grit needed to remove damage or reach the previous
   scratch pattern, using full-length strokes and consistent pressure.
3. Feel for a **burr** (wire edge) along the full length of the opposite face
   before moving to the next grit — this confirms the bevel has met the edge.
4. Progress through finer grits, alternating faces lightly to remove the
   prior burr, until the final stone.
5. Remove the last, finest burr with a few strokes on each face, then strop.

### Saw sharpening (added steps)

1. Clamp the saw in a saw vise, teeth up.
2. **Joint** first: run a flat file lightly along the tooth line to bring all
   points to one height — skip this only if the teeth are already even.
3. **Set** the teeth (bend alternate teeth outward slightly with a saw set)
   before filing, since setting can deform a freshly sharpened edge.
4. **File**, maintaining rake and fleam angle from the table above, working
   every other gullet from one side, then flipping the saw and filing the
   remaining gullets from the other side.

## Testing for sharpness

- **Paper test:** a sharp knife or chisel slices printer paper cleanly with
  light pressure; a dull one tears or catches.
- **Arm-hair test:** a sharp edge shaves arm hair cleanly at a light touch —
  useful but do not force it against resistance.
- **Fingernail test:** drawn lightly across a nail at a shallow angle, a sharp
  edge bites and holds; a dull one slides.
- **Sighting:** tilt the edge under light — a truly sharp edge shows no
  reflective line; any bright glint marks a remaining flat (unsharpened) spot.

## Rust prevention and removal

**Prevention** is far less work than removal:

- Wipe tools dry after use and before storage; damp is the enemy, not rain
  itself.
- Keep a light film of oil (plain machine oil, camellia oil, or boiled
  linseed oil on wood-and-metal tools) on bare steel between uses.
- Store in a dry space; a bucket of sand mixed with a cup of used motor oil
  or boiled linseed oil makes a serviceable dip-and-wipe station for
  garden and digging tools.
- Silica gel packets or a box of chalk in a closed tool chest absorb ambient
  moisture.

**Removing light surface rust:**

1. Soak in white vinegar (add table salt for stronger action) for several
   hours to overnight.
2. Scrub with a wire brush, 0000 steel wool, or a fine abrasive pad.
3. Rinse, dry completely, and oil immediately — vinegar strips any
   protective film along with the rust, and bare steel flashes to new rust
   within hours if left wet.

**Removing heavy rust — electrolysis:** effective on cast and wrought iron,
axe heads, and other heavily corroded steel, and it does not touch sound
metal.

1. Submerge the rusted piece and a sacrificial steel anode (rebar or scrap
   steel) in a plastic tub of water with washing soda (sodium carbonate)
   dissolved in it — **not table salt**, which generates toxic and corrosive
   chlorine gas at the anode.
2. Connect a battery charger's negative lead to the rusted piece and the
   positive lead to the sacrificial anode, keeping the two pieces apart so
   they cannot touch.
3. Run the charger for several hours to overnight; rust lifts as a loose black
   scale that wipes or brushes off.
4. Rinse, dry, and oil immediately.

!!! warning "Electrolysis produces hydrogen gas"
    The process evolves hydrogen at the workpiece and oxygen at the anode —
    both flammable, and explosive in combination if allowed to concentrate.
    Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated space, never under a sealed lid,
    keep open flame and sparks away, and do not lean over the tank.

## Handle maintenance

Wood handles fail by loosening, splitting, or checking (drying cracks). All
three are field-repairable as long as the handle is not broken outright.

**Loose head, minor:**

1. Soak the head end of the handle in boiled linseed oil overnight to swell
   the wood — a temporary fix, useful in the field.
2. If a wood wedge is present and there is room, drive it deeper.
3. Add a steel wedge (barrel or step pattern) driven perpendicular to the
   existing wood wedge, oiled first to ease driving. It bites into the wood
   and spreads the kerf in the opposite direction, locking both wedges in.

**Loose head, full correction:** drive out the old wedge, remove the handle
(driving the eye down onto a solid surface, or splitting a doomed handle out
if it will be replaced), fit a new handle by shaving the tenon to match the
eye, seat it fully, saw a wedge kerf if one is not already present, and drive
a wood wedge coated in glue, then trim it flush and add a steel wedge across it.

**Checked (cracked) handles:** a shallow surface check that has not reached
the eye can often be stabilized with thin cyanoacrylate (super glue) worked
into the crack, or simply monitored. A check that runs into the eye, or any
crack you can open with hand pressure, means the handle is not safe to use for
striking — replace it.

**When to replace outright:** any crack through the grain near the eye or
waist, a handle that has taken a permanent set (bent) from overstrike, or wood
that has gone soft, spongy, or insect-damaged. A failed handle under a
swinging load can throw the head.

## Storage for the long term

- Hang or rack tools rather than piling them — edges and points survive
  contact damage better than storage abrasion.
- Keep a light oil film on all bare steel, reapplied seasonally at minimum.
- Store wood-handled tools out of direct sun and away from heat sources
  (near a wood stove, for instance) that accelerate drying and checking.
- Separate galvanic pairs (e.g., don't leave dissimilar bare metals clamped
  together wet) to avoid accelerated corrosion at the contact point.

## Safety

- **Discard mushroomed striking tools.** A chisel, wedge, or drift pin with a
  flared, cracked striking face can shed metal fragments on impact. Grind the
  mushroom back to a clean radius, or retire the tool.
- **Wear eye protection (ANSI Z87.1-rated) for all sharpening, filing, and
  striking work** — grinding and hammering both throw debris, and stone or
  file work throws fine grit.
- **Never grind a hardened edge dry against a fast wheel** without cooling —
  frictional heat can draw the temper (soften the steel) at the very edge
  without any visible discoloration warning you in time. Hand stones and
  files run cool by comparison and are the safer default for field
  maintenance.
- **Ventilate electrolysis work** as described above; treat the tank as a
  source of flammable gas, not just a cleaning bath.
- **Inspect handles before every use of a striking tool.** A head that comes
  loose mid-swing is a projectile.
