# Masonry, Concrete, and Mortar Mixes

## Summary

Concrete and masonry are the same idea at two scales: a binder that hardens by
chemical reaction, holding aggregate or units in place. Neither is forgiving.
Both are strong in compression and weak in tension, both are effectively
permanent once set, and both fail in this climate for the same reason — water
that gets in, freezes, and expands.

The three numbers that decide whether the work lasts are the **water-cement
ratio**, the **air content**, and the **curing**. None of them is visible in the
finished product, and all three are routinely sacrificed to make the day go
faster.

One idea underlies the whole page, and it is counterintuitive: **stronger is
usually worse.** A mortar stronger than the brick it joins destroys the brick. A
concrete mix made soupy for easy placement is much weaker than the same mix
placed stiff. Resist the instinct to add water and to add cement.

## Binders

| Binder | Sets by | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Portland cement | Hydration — reacts with water, sets under water | The modern default. Strong, fast, rigid, industrially produced. |
| Hydraulic lime (NHL) | Hydration, more slowly | Weaker and more flexible than portland; tolerant of movement; used for historic masonry. |
| Non-hydraulic (fat) lime | Carbonation — reabsorbs CO₂ from air | Very slow, very weak, very forgiving. Self-healing of hairline cracks. Cannot set under water. |
| Clay / earth | Drying | No chemical set. Adequate for protected, low-stress work only. |

Portland cement is a manufactured product requiring roughly 1,450 °C kilns. Lime
is not — which makes it the binder that remains available when industry does
not. See *Lime without industry* below.

## Concrete

### What is in it

Concrete is portland cement, water, fine aggregate (sand), and coarse aggregate
(stone), plus air. It is not "cement" — cement is one ingredient, roughly 10–15%
by volume.

A serviceable general-purpose mix by volume is **1 cement : 2 sand : 3 coarse
aggregate**, with water added to reach a workable but stiff consistency. A
leaner **1 : 2.5 : 4** is adequate for non-structural work. These volumetric
recipes are how concrete gets batched by hand; a plant batches by weight with a
designed water-cement ratio, which is more accurate and worth using whenever a
truck can reach the site.

### Water-cement ratio

The single most important variable. Strength and durability fall as the
water-cement ratio rises — the excess water leaves voids behind when it
evaporates.

- **0.40–0.45** for concrete exposed to freeze-thaw and de-icing salts.
- **0.45–0.50** for general structural work.
- **Above 0.60** the concrete is significantly weaker and much more permeable.

Site water added to the truck to make the pour easier is the most common way a
3,000 psi mix becomes a 2,000 psi mix. If it is too stiff to place, the answer
is a plasticizer or a different mix, not a hose.

### Specified strength

The code sets minimum 28-day compressive strength by exposure. New Hampshire is
in the **severe** weathering category.

| Type or location of construction | Negligible | Moderate | Severe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basement walls, foundations, and other concrete not exposed to the weather | 2,500 | 2,500 | 2,500 |
| Basement slabs and interior slabs on grade, except garage floor slabs | 2,500 | 2,500 | 2,500 |
| Basement walls, foundation walls, exterior walls, and other vertical concrete work exposed to the weather | 2,500 | 3,000 | 3,000 |
| Porches, carport slabs, and steps exposed to the weather, and garage floor slabs | 2,500 | 3,000 | 3,500 |

Values are psi at 28 days. Everywhere the table calls for more than 2,500 psi —
that is, all weather-exposed concrete in moderate and severe climates — the
concrete **must be air-entrained**, 5% to 7% total air by volume. Concrete in
the first two rows that may freeze *during construction* must be air-entrained
as well.

!!! note "Jurisdictions amend this table"
    Minnesota, for example, requires 5,000 psi footings where the base IRC sets
    no separate footing value. Confirm the adopted numbers with your building
    department rather than working from the model code.

### Air entrainment

Deliberately introduced microscopic air bubbles give freezing water somewhere to
expand into. Without them, water in the capillary pores expands roughly 9% on
freezing and progressively destroys the surface — the familiar scaling and
spalling of driveways and steps.

**In this climate, air entrainment on anything exposed is not optional.** It is
ordered with the concrete; it cannot be added later, and it is largely destroyed
by over-troweling the surface.

One exception worth knowing: for a steel-troweled garage floor, the code permits
reducing total air to not less than 3% if the specified strength is raised to at
least 4,000 psi — because hard troweling and high air content together cause the
surface to delaminate.

### Slump, placing, and consolidation

- **Slump** measures consistency, not quality. A 4 in slump mix placed and
  vibrated properly beats a 7 in slump mix every time.
- **Do not let concrete free-fall** more than about 4–5 ft, or the aggregate
  segregates from the paste.
- **Consolidate** — vibrate or rod — to drive out entrapped air and get paste
  against the forms and around the rebar. Honeycombing at the base of a wall is
  a consolidation failure. Over-vibration, on the other hand, drives out the
  *entrained* air you paid for.
- **Do not overwork the surface**, and never sprinkle dry cement on bleed water
  to dry it. That makes a weak, dusty skin.
- **Wait for bleed water to disappear** before finishing. Troweling water back
  into the surface is the standard way to ruin a slab.

### Jointing

Concrete will crack. Control joints decide where.

- Cut or tool joints to a depth of **at least one quarter of the slab
  thickness** — shallower joints do not induce the crack and the slab cracks
  somewhere else.
- Space joints, in feet, at roughly **24 to 36 times the slab thickness in
  inches**. A 4 in slab wants joints at 8–12 ft.
- Cut them early — within hours for a saw cut, before the slab cracks on its
  own.
- **Isolation joints** where a slab meets a wall, column, or footing, so the two
  can move independently.

### Curing

Curing is keeping the concrete moist and warm enough for hydration to continue.
It is not "drying."

- Keep it moist for **at least 7 days** at normal temperatures — wet burlap,
  plastic sheeting, ponding, or a curing compound.
- Concrete reaches roughly 70% of design strength at 7 days and the specified
  strength at 28 days, and continues to gain slowly for years.
- Concrete allowed to dry early stops gaining strength permanently. The surface
  suffers most, which is why poorly cured slabs dust and scale.

### Cold weather

The controlling facts: hydration virtually stops at about 40 °F, and **concrete
that freezes before reaching 500 psi is permanently compromised** — losing up to
half its potential strength.

ACI 306 minimum concrete temperature as placed and maintained through the
protection period:

| Section minimum dimension | Minimum concrete temperature | Max. gradual surface temperature drop in 24 h after protection ends |
|---|---|---|
| Less than 12 in | 55 °F | 50 °F |
| 12–36 in | 50 °F | 40 °F |
| 36–72 in | 45 °F | 30 °F |
| Greater than 72 in | 40 °F | 20 °F |

Practical rules for a New England fall pour:

- **Never place on frozen ground.** It thaws later and settles. See
  [Site Preparation](../site-prep/10-site-preparation.md).
- Heat the mix water, and the aggregate if necessary. Place at the lowest
  practical slump.
- Cover with insulating blankets immediately and keep them on through the
  protection period. Insulated form blankets for walls.
- Use an accelerator. **Non-chloride accelerators for anything containing steel**
  — calcium chloride promotes corrosion of reinforcement.
- Type III (high early strength) cement reaches 500 psi sooner.
- Vent any fuel-fired heater used in an enclosure. Unvented combustion both
  poisons the crew and carbonates the fresh surface, producing a soft, dusting
  slab.
- Below about 20 °F for any sustained period, do not pour.

A drop of 20 °F in concrete temperature roughly doubles setting time. Plan the
day around that, not around the calendar.

## Reinforcement

Concrete has roughly a tenth of its compressive strength in tension. Steel
carries the tension.

- **Cover protects the steel.** Minimum concrete cover per ACI 318: **3 in**
  where concrete is cast against and permanently exposed to earth; **2 in** for
  formed surfaces exposed to earth or weather with #6 bars and larger, **1½ in**
  for #5 and smaller; **¾ in** for slabs and walls not exposed to weather or
  ground.
- Rebar lying on the ground at the bottom of a footing trench does nothing. Use
  chairs or dobies.
- **Rusty rebar is fine** — light surface rust actually improves bond. Flaking,
  pitted, or scaling rebar is not.
- Splice laps are specified by the design; a common residential rule of thumb is
  40 bar diameters, but confirm against the drawings.
- Welded wire mesh in a slab only works if it is held up in the slab's middle or
  upper third. Mesh stepped down to the subgrade is decoration.

## Mortar

### The types

ASTM C270 defines four types. The mnemonic runs **M, S, N, O** — in decreasing
strength, from the phrase "MaSoN wOrK."

| Type | Min. 28-day compressive strength | Character |
|---|---|---|
| M | 2,500 psi | High strength, poor workability |
| S | 1,800 psi | Strong, good flexural bond |
| N | 750 psi | General purpose; good bond and workability |
| O | 350 psi | Low strength; interior and restoration |

### Proportion specification

ASTM C270 can be specified by *property* (laboratory-tested) or by *proportion*
(a recipe). The proportion specification, parts by volume:

| Type | Portland cement | Hydrated lime or lime putty |
|---|---|---|
| M | 1 | ¼ |
| S | 1 | over ¼ to ½ |
| N | 1 | over ½ to 1¼ |
| O | 1 | over 1¼ to 2½ |

Aggregate in every case: **not less than 2¼ and not more than 3 times the sum of
the separate volumes of the cementitious materials**, measured damp and loose.

The familiar field shorthands — 1:1:6 for Type N, 1:½:4½ for Type S, 1:¼:3¾ for
Type M — are single points inside those ranges. The range exists for a reason:
more lime means more water retention and better workability, which suits a
high-absorption brick; less lime suits a dense, low-absorption unit.

### Choosing a type

The Brick Industry Association's guidance is explicit and is the opposite of
most people's instinct: **select the mortar type with the lowest compressive
strength that meets the requirements.** Type N is recommended for normal use,
including most veneer.

| Location | Building segment | Recommended | Alternate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exterior, above grade | Reinforced or loadbearing walls | S | N |
| | Veneer or non-loadbearing walls | N | S |
| | Parapets, chimneys | N | S |
| Exterior, at or below grade | Foundation walls, retaining walls | M | S |
| | Sewers, manholes | M | S |
| Interior | Loadbearing walls | N | S |
| | Partitions | N | O or S |

The reason to stay low is **bond, not strength**. Mortar's job is to make
continuous, intimate contact with the unit and to resist water penetration.
Compressive strength is the property most overemphasized in mortar selection and
the one least related to how the wall performs.

### Mortar must be weaker than the unit

This is the rule that saves old buildings. Masonry moves — thermally,
seasonally, structurally. Something has to accommodate that movement, and it
should be the mortar joint, which can be raked out and repointed, not the unit,
which cannot.

Repointing soft, historic, hand-made brick with a hard portland mortar is the
classic destructive repair. The mortar is stronger and less permeable than the
brick, so movement and salt crystallization are forced into the brick face
instead, and the faces spall off over a few freeze-thaw seasons. The mortar
survives; the wall does not.

For historic soft brick and lime-mortared stone, use **lime mortar or a natural
hydraulic lime**, matched to the original. ASTM C1713 covers mortars for repair
of historic masonry.

### Repointing old brick

Repointing replaces failed joint mortar without disturbing the brick. The
brick is supposed to outlast several rounds of repointing; done with the wrong
mortar or technique, repointing instead destroys the brick faster than leaving
a failing joint alone.

**Diagnose before mixing.** Determine what is already there before choosing a
replacement.

- Pre-1900s New England brick and stone is generally soft, low-fired, and
  originally laid in straight lime mortar. Mid-20th-century-and-later brick is
  typically laid in a portland-lime mortar and is considerably harder.
- **Scratch test**: if the existing mortar can be scored with a fingernail or a
  knife point and is visibly softer than the brick, it is a lime-based system.
  Match it with lime or a natural hydraulic lime — never portland, Type N, or
  anything stronger. See *Mortar must be weaker than the unit*, above.
- When in doubt, test a proposed mix in a small, inconspicuous area and let it
  cure fully before committing the whole wall. A mortar-analysis lab can
  identify the original binder and sand precisely if the building warrants it.

**Remove the old mortar properly.**

- Rake out to a depth of at least 2 to 2½ times the joint width, or until sound
  mortar is reached — commonly ¾ to 1 in for a standard ⅜ in joint.
- Remove with a joint chisel and mash hammer or a hand raking tool.
  **Do not grind out joints on soft historic brick.** A grinder cuts a wide,
  straight-sided kerf that oversizes the joint and routinely nicks the brick
  arrises, which then spall under freeze-thaw. Grinding is acceptable only on
  hard modern brick in hard mortar, and even then the corners should be
  finished by hand so the chisel, not the wheel, defines the joint edge.
- Brush and flush the raked joint clean. Mortar will not bond to a dust-coated
  surface.

**Match the mortar to what is there.**

- Soft historic brick or lime-mortared stone: straight lime putty mortar, or a
  natural hydraulic lime (NHL 2 or NHL 3.5). Never straight portland cement.
  ASTM C1713 covers mortars for repair of historic masonry.
- Sound 20th-century brick laid in portland-lime mortar: Type O or Type N,
  matched to the original — repointing should never introduce a mortar harder
  than the one it replaces, even where the original is already portland-based.
- Match sand color and gradation as closely as the original allows; the sand,
  more than the binder, determines whether the repair reads as invisible or as
  a patch. Mix a small test panel, let it cure and dry fully, and check the
  match in daylight — mortar is noticeably darker wet than cured.

**Pack and tool in stages.**

- Dampen the joint faces and surrounding brick before packing, enough to
  control suction without leaving standing water. A dry, thirsty brick pulls
  water out of the fresh mortar before it can set, and the joint never
  develops bond — the same failure described under *Mixing and using mortar*.
- Pack in thin layers, ¼ in or less, letting each layer stiffen to
  thumbprint-hard before adding the next. Filling a deep joint in one lift
  traps air and shrinks unevenly, leaving a joint that looks solid but is not
  bonded to the brick.
- Tool to a **concave joint** matching the original profile once
  thumbprint-hard, to compact the surface and shed water. Match the original
  joint profile — a flush or raked joint on brick originally struck concave
  both looks wrong and performs worse in this climate.

**Cure it like the wall depends on it, because it does.**

- Lime mortar sets by carbonation, not hydraulic reaction, and needs weeks of
  protection, not days: keep it damp by misting and shaded from direct sun and
  wind for the first several days.
- Protect fresh repointing from rain for at least 24–48 hours and from
  freezing for the entire first season. This is the practical reason late-fall
  repointing in this climate is poor timing — the work will not have enough
  warm weeks left to carbonate before frost.

### Mixing and using mortar

- **Measure by volume.** A shovel is not a unit of measure; use a box or a
  bucket.
- Mix mechanically for 3–5 minutes after all materials are in. Under-mixed
  mortar is weak and unworkable.
- **Retempering** — adding water to restore workability — is acceptable for the
  first couple of hours; discard mortar older than about 2½ hours, or 1½ hours
  in hot weather. Mortar that has begun to set cannot be revived.
- **Wet high-absorption brick** before laying, and let the surface dry. A dry,
  thirsty brick sucks the water out of the mortar before it can hydrate, and the
  joint never develops bond.
- **Tool the joints** when the mortar is thumbprint-hard. A **concave (rodded)
  joint** compacts the surface and sheds water best; raked and struck joints
  hold water and are poor choices in a freeze-thaw climate.
- Do not lay masonry on frozen units or in freezing conditions without
  cold-weather protection; mortar that freezes before it sets has no strength.

## Laying units

### Concrete block (CMU)

- Nominal 8 × 8 × 16 in, actually 7⅝ × 7⅝ × 15⅝ in — the ⅜ in difference is the
  mortar joint. Nominal dimensions include the joint, which is what makes block
  coursing arithmetic work.
- Standard running bond, joints ⅜ in.
- **Face-shell bedding** is normal: mortar on the face shells only, not the
  webs, except in the first course on the footing, which is fully bedded.
- Vertical reinforcement goes in the cores, which are then **grouted solid**.
  Grout is not mortar — it is a fluid, high-slump concrete with pea stone,
  intended to flow into the cores and consolidate around the steel.
- **Bond beams** — courses of channel block filled with grout and horizontal
  rebar — tie the wall together at the top and at floor lines.
- A freshly laid block wall is unstable until grouted and braced. See Safety.

### Brick

- Modular brick is nominally 4 × 2⅔ × 8 in, so three courses plus joints make
  8 in.
- In modern construction, brick is usually a **veneer**: a single wythe tied to
  a framed wall with corrugated ties, with a **1 in minimum air space** behind
  it, flashing at the base, and **weep holes** in the first course above the
  flashing at roughly 33 in on center.
- The air space and weeps are not optional details. Brick is not waterproof; it
  absorbs and leaks water inward, and the cavity plus the flashing plus the
  weeps are what carry it back out. Filling the cavity with dropped mortar is the
  most common cause of a wet wall behind brick veneer.

### Stone

- **Dry-laid stone** relies on friction, gravity, and geometry. The rules are
  ancient and sound: one stone over two, two over one; keep the length of every
  stone running *into* the wall, not along it; every stone bedded on its natural
  bed; hearting (small stones) packed tight, never loose fill.
- **Through-stones** (bond stones) spanning the full wall thickness every few
  feet vertically and horizontally tie the two faces together. A dry wall
  without them is two thin walls that will eventually part.
- **Batter** — leaning each face inward, roughly 1 in per foot of height — makes
  a freestanding dry wall self-correcting. Frost heave that shifts a battered
  wall tends to settle it back.
- A dry-laid wall drains and tolerates frost movement. A mortared stone wall on
  a shallow footing in this climate does neither, and cracks.

## Lime without industry

Portland cement cannot be made without a large rotary kiln. Lime can be made in
a pit, and was, for several thousand years. This is the most likely path back to
a usable binder if manufactured cement is unavailable.

The lime cycle:

1. **Calcine.** Heat limestone (CaCO₃) to drive off carbon dioxide, producing
   quicklime (CaO). The traditional and still-optimal temperature is around
   **850–900 °C** — a wood- or coal-fired kiln reaches this. Below it the stone
   does not fully convert; well above it the lime sinters and loses reactivity.
2. **Slake.** Add water. The reaction is violent and strongly exothermic,
   producing calcium hydroxide — hydrated lime as a powder if water is limited,
   or **lime putty** if water is in excess. Putty matured under water for weeks
   to months is the traditional high-quality product; Vitruvius describes the
   practice.
3. **Set.** In use, the lime slowly reabsorbs CO₂ from the air and reverts to
   calcium carbonate — back to stone. This takes months to years and requires
   air, so lime mortar sets from the surface inward and cannot cure under water.

Practical notes: limestone and marble are the feedstocks; oyster and clam shells
are calcium carbonate too and were burned for lime throughout coastal New
England. Lime mortar is weak, so build accordingly — thick walls, generous
bearing, modest spans, and no reliance on tensile strength anywhere.

!!! danger "Quicklime is genuinely dangerous"
    Quicklime reacts with the moisture in skin, eyes, and lungs, generating heat
    as it does. Slaking a batch can boil and spit. Handle it dry, with full eye
    protection and cover, add quicklime to water rather than water to quicklime,
    and stand upwind.

## Safety

- **Wet concrete and mortar cause alkaline burns.** Fresh cement paste runs pH
  12–13 against skin's 5.5. The burns are progressive, often painless at first,
  and continue to worsen after exposure ends — the worst cases come from cement
  that seeps into a boot or glove and stays there for hours. Third-degree burns
  requiring grafts are not rare.
    - Keep it off skin. Waterproof boots taped at the top, gloves, long sleeves,
      knee protection.
    - If it contacts skin, wash immediately and thoroughly with clean water.
      OSHA has accepted the use of a mildly acidic or buffering rinse, such as
      diluted vinegar, to neutralize residue.
    - Get anything in the eye irrigated immediately and at length, and seek
      medical attention.
- **Silica dust.** Cutting, grinding, or drilling concrete, block, brick, and
  stone releases respirable crystalline silica, which causes silicosis and lung
  cancer. OSHA's construction standard sets an 8-hour PEL of 50 µg/m³ and
  specifies control methods — principally **water delivery to the blade or bit,
  or vacuum dust collection**, plus respirators — for common tasks. Dry-cutting
  block with an abrasive saw and no water is among the worst exposures on a
  construction site. It is also entirely avoidable.
- **Fresh masonry walls collapse.** An ungrouted, unbraced block wall has very
  little resistance to wind or a shove. Brace as you build.
- **Weight.** A cubic foot of concrete is about 150 lb; a cubic yard is over two
  tons. A standard 8 in block is 35–40 lb, an 80 lb bag is 80 lb, and the
  cumulative back injuries from masonry work are the profession's signature.
- **Formwork** for walls must be designed and braced for the hydraulic pressure
  of wet concrete, which is enormous and rises with pour rate and low
  temperature. A blown form is dangerous as well as expensive.
- **Overhead**: never stand under a concrete bucket or a boom pump line.

## Common failure modes

| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Surface of a slab or step scaling and flaking after a few winters | No air entrainment, over-troweled surface, or water added on site |
| Slab dusting, surface powders off underfoot | Poor curing, bleed water troweled in, or unvented heaters during a cold pour |
| Random cracking across a slab | Control joints too shallow, too far apart, or cut too late |
| Honeycombed voids at the base of a wall | Segregation from free-fall, or no consolidation |
| Spalled faces on old brick after repointing | Portland mortar used on soft historic brick |
| Efflorescence — white bloom on masonry | Water moving through the assembly and depositing salts; a symptom, not the disease |
| Step cracking through a block wall in a stair pattern | Differential settlement, or no bond beam and no vertical steel |
| Cracked, displaced mortared stone wall | Footing above the frost line |
| Wet interior wall behind brick veneer | Cavity blocked with mortar droppings, or missing flashing and weeps |
| Corroded, spalling concrete exposing rebar | Insufficient cover, or chloride accelerator used with steel |

## Regional assumptions

This page assumes the northeastern United States: a **severe** weathering
classification, roughly a hundred freeze-thaw cycles a season at the ground
surface, deep frost, and a construction season that pushes concrete work into
weather ACI 306 exists to manage. In a mild climate, air entrainment is optional
and freeze-thaw scaling is not the governing durability concern. Here it is the
whole game.
