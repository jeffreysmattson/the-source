# Plumbing Rough-In

## Summary

Rough-in is everything that happens before the walls close: drain, waste, and
vent piping; water supply lines; and the holes, blocking, and protection they
need. It is the stage where mistakes get buried, and where a half-hour of extra
thought saves opening a finished ceiling later.

A house has two plumbing systems that work on opposite principles:

- **Drain, waste, and vent (DWV)** runs on **gravity and air**. It is a
  ventilated, unpressurized system. Its failure mode is sewer gas in the house
  or waste that does not move.
- **Water supply** runs on **pressure**. Its failure mode is a leak, and in this
  climate, a freeze.

They get roughed at the same time and share the same holes, but almost every
rule about one is wrong about the other.

!!! warning "Code editions and jurisdiction"
    Plumbing is heavily code-governed and the rules differ substantially between
    the IPC/IRC family and the Uniform Plumbing Code used in other states. Trap
    arm lengths, venting methods, and fixture unit values are all different. The
    numbers on this page are from the **IRC**. New Hampshire's State Building
    Code adopts the International Residential Code and International Plumbing
    Code by reference — as of the current statute, the 2024 editions, with the
    2018 IECC and the 2023 NEC. **The adopted edition changes; confirm it with
    the building department, and confirm any local amendments.**

## Sequence

1. Set the **drain locations** first. DWV runs on gravity and has almost no
   flexibility; everything else bends around it.
2. Confirm **fixture locations and clearances** before drilling anything.
3. Run the **building drain and stacks**, then branch drains, then vents.
4. Run **water supply** last — it is small, pressurized, and can go almost
   anywhere.
5. **Test** before covering. Water or air on the DWV, pressure on the supply.
6. **Inspect** and only then insulate and close the walls.

## Drain, waste, and vent

### Fall

Horizontal drainage piping must slope **not less than ¼ in per foot (2%) for
pipe 2½ in and smaller**, and **not less than 1/8 in per foot (1%) for 3 in and
larger**.

There is such a thing as too much slope on a drain carrying solids: if the
liquid outruns the solids, the solids are left behind and the line blocks. Keep
the slope uniform and near the specified value rather than as steep as the joist
bay allows.

### Sizing

Drainage is sized in **drainage fixture units (DFU)**, a dimensionless measure
of probable load. Rough values: lavatory 1, shower 2, kitchen sink 2, bathtub 2,
clothes washer 2, and a 1.6 gpf water closet 3. A "bathroom group" is counted as
a unit rather than summed.

For an ordinary house:

| Line | Typical size |
|---|---|
| Lavatory, bidet drain | 1¼–1½ in |
| Shower, tub, kitchen sink, laundry | 2 in |
| Water closet | 3 in minimum |
| Branch serving a full bathroom | 3 in |
| Building drain and building sewer | 3 or 4 in |

**A drain may never reduce in size in the direction of flow.** Not at a fitting,
not at a wall, not to fit through a joist.

### Fittings

DWV fittings are directional. Using the wrong one is the most common amateur
error and it produces a line that clogs forever.

- **Horizontal to horizontal, and horizontal to vertical**: long-sweep fittings —
  wyes, combination wye-and-eighth-bend, long-sweep quarter bends.
- **Vertical to horizontal**: a long-sweep or combination fitting, never a
  short-radius quarter bend for anything carrying solids.
- **A sanitary tee is a vent and vertical-drop fitting only.** Laid on its back
  in a horizontal run it forms a ledge that catches solids.
- **Double sanitary tees** ("cross tees") are not permitted for back-to-back
  fixtures carrying solids — one fixture discharges across into the other.

### Cleanouts

- At the junction of the building drain and the building sewer.
- At each change of direction greater than 45 degrees.
- At intervals of **not more than 100 ft** on horizontal runs.
- **Not in concealed locations.** A cleanout you have to open a wall to reach is
  not a cleanout.
- Clearance in front of the opening: **18 in for 6 in pipe and smaller**, 36 in
  for 8 in and larger.

### Traps

A trap holds a plug of water — the **trap seal**, between 2 and 4 in deep — that
blocks sewer gas. Everything about venting exists to protect that seal.

- **One trap per fixture; never two traps in series** on the same drain. A
  double-trapped line air-locks and drains slowly or not at all.
- **No S-traps.** A trap whose outlet drops vertically siphons itself dry as the
  fixture drains. This is why the drain must run horizontally to a vent before it
  falls.
- **No mechanical or "drum" traps** in new work.
- A floor drain or a rarely used fixture will **evaporate its seal** in a
  heated house over a season. Trap primers, or a cup of cooking oil on the water,
  solve it.

### Trap arms

The trap arm is the run from the trap weir to the vent connection. Too long and
the flow in the arm seals the pipe and siphons the trap.

**IRC Table P3105.1 — maximum distance of fixture trap from vent:**

| Trap arm size | Slope | Maximum developed length |
|---|---|---|
| 1¼ in | ¼ in/ft | 5 ft |
| 1½ in | ¼ in/ft | 6 ft |
| 2 in | ¼ in/ft | 8 ft |
| 3 in | 1/8 in/ft | 12 ft |
| 4 in | 1/8 in/ft | 16 ft |

The water closet is exempt: it is self-siphoning by design, so the developed
length from its trap weir to the vent is not limited.

!!! danger "The UPC numbers are much shorter"
    Uniform Plumbing Code jurisdictions allow roughly 2½ ft for 1¼ in, 3½ ft for
    1½ in, and 5 ft for 2 in. If you learned plumbing in a UPC state, or are
    reading a guide written in one, the trap arm numbers do not transfer. Check
    which code applies.

### Vents

Vents let air into the drain behind the falling water, so the water does not
drag the trap seals out with it, and let sewer gas out above the roof.

- **Every fixture must be vented**, individually or by an approved method —
  wet venting, common venting, circuit venting, or an island (loop) vent.
- **Vent size** is generally not less than half the diameter of the drain served,
  and never less than 1¼ in.
- **Vents must rise** to at least 6 in above the fixture's flood level rim before
  running horizontally. A vent that runs flat at trap-arm height is a drain, and
  will carry water.
- **The main stack** must be full-size through the roof.
- **Termination:** at least 6 in above the roof, or 6 in above the anticipated
  snow accumulation, whichever is greater — and not less than 7 ft above a roof
  used for anything other than weather protection.
- **Terminal location** (P3103.5): a vent terminal may not be less than **4 ft
  directly beneath** any door, openable window, or air intake, and may not be
  within **10 ft horizontally** of such an opening unless it is at least **3 ft
  above the top** of it.

!!! warning "Frost closure — the New Hampshire detail"
    Where the 97.5% outside design temperature is **0 °F or lower** — which is
    most of New Hampshire — a vent extension through the roof must be **not less
    than 3 in in diameter**, and the increase in size must be made **inside the
    structure, at least 12 in below the roof**.

    The reason is straightforward: moist air rising through a 1½ or 2 in vent in
    a cold snap deposits frost on the inside of the pipe, and the pipe closes.
    The fixtures then gurgle, drain slowly, and pull their traps. A 3 in vent
    takes far longer to close, and increasing the size inside the warm structure
    keeps the transition out of the freezing zone. This is a detail that a
    plumber trained in a mild climate will not know.

**Air admittance valves (AAVs)** are one-way vents that admit air without
allowing gas out. They are accepted in many jurisdictions for island sinks and
retrofits, prohibited in others, and are mechanical devices that eventually
fail. They cannot serve as the building's main vent, and they must remain
accessible. Use them where a real vent is impossible, not where it is
inconvenient.

## Water supply

### Materials

| Material | Notes |
|---|---|
| PEX | Flexible, freeze-tolerant (expands rather than bursting, though fittings still fail), fast to install, fewest joints. Degrades in UV — keep it out of sunlight. |
| Copper | Long-lived, rigid, tolerant of heat, recyclable. Requires soldering and a torch. Pinholes in aggressive or acidic water. |
| CPVC | Cheap, solvent-welded, brittle when cold and less tolerant of impact. |
| Galvanized steel | Obsolete for supply. Corrodes internally and closes up. If you find it in an old house, it is at the end of its life. |

PEX has become the default in cold climates specifically because of freeze
behavior and because a home run manifold layout puts a shutoff for every fixture
in one place.

### Layout

- **Trunk and branch** — a main line with tees off it. Less tubing, more joints,
  slower hot water at the far fixtures.
- **Home run manifold** — a separate line from a manifold to each fixture. More
  tubing, almost no concealed joints, individual shutoffs, fast hot water on
  small-diameter runs.

Either way: **minimize concealed joints.** Every joint in a wall or ceiling is a
future leak you cannot see.

- Provide a **shutoff at every fixture** and a main shutoff where the service
  enters.
- Provide **water hammer arrestors** at quick-closing valves — washing machines
  and dishwashers especially. An air chamber made from a capped stub eventually
  waterlogs and stops working; a proper arrestor does not.
- **Size for flow, not habit.** ¾ in mains, ½ in branches is the common
  residential pattern.

### Supports

**IRC Table P2605.1**, maximum spacing:

| Material | Horizontal | Vertical |
|---|---|---|
| PEX | 32 in | 10 ft (mid-story guide, 2 in and smaller) |
| Copper tubing, 1¼ in and smaller | 6 ft | 10 ft |
| Copper pipe | 12 ft | 10 ft |
| PVC / CPVC | 4 ft | 10 ft |
| Cast iron | 5 ft | 15 ft |

Unsupported PEX sags, traps air, and bangs. Supports also need to let the pipe
move: plastics expand a great deal with temperature, and a hot water line
clamped rigidly at both ends will tick, creak, and eventually chafe through.

## Freeze protection

The governing requirement in this climate, and the one that ruins buildings.

Where the winter design temperature is 32 °F or lower, water, soil, and waste
pipe **shall not** be installed outside the building, in exterior walls, in
attics, or in crawl spaces, or anywhere subject to freezing, unless protected by
insulation, heat, or both.

- **Water service pipe: not less than 12 in deep, and not less than 6 in below
  the frost line.** The frost line is a local number — see
  [Site Preparation](10-site-preparation.md).
- **Keep supply piping out of exterior walls.** If it cannot be avoided, run the
  pipe on the *warm* side of the cavity insulation, with insulation between the
  pipe and the sheathing — never the reverse. Insulation between the pipe and
  the heated room is exactly backwards and is the classic burst-pipe detail.
- **Nothing in an unconditioned attic.** In a cold climate this is a pipe in a
  freezer.
- **Frost-proof hose bibbs** (wall hydrants) with the valve seat inside the
  heated envelope, installed pitched slightly downward so the barrel drains —
  and the hose must be disconnected in fall or the thing cannot drain and will
  split anyway.
- **Design for draining.** In a building that may be left unheated, pitch the
  system to low-point drains and fit them. A house that can be winterized in an
  hour is worth the extra fittings.
- **Insulate, but understand what insulation does**: it slows heat loss, it does
  not add heat. An insulated pipe in an unheated space still freezes, just later.
  Heat is what prevents freezing; insulation buys time.

## Fixture clearances and rough-in dimensions

Set these before drilling. IRC R307.1:

- Water closet centerline: **15 in minimum** to any side wall or obstruction.
- **30 in minimum** center-to-center between fixtures.
- **21 in minimum** clear space in front of a water closet.

Common rough-in heights, from finished floor — conventions, not code:

| Item | Typical height |
|---|---|
| Water closet drain | 12 in from finished wall to center (10 and 14 in exist) |
| Water closet supply | 6–8 in, offset ~6 in from center |
| Lavatory drain | 18–20 in |
| Lavatory supplies | 20–22 in, 4 in each side of center |
| Kitchen sink drain | 16–18 in |
| Shower valve | 42–48 in |
| Tub spout | 4–6 in above tub rim |
| Shower head | 76–80 in |

Add **blocking** while the walls are open for everything that will later be
fastened to them: grab bars (or future grab bars), wall-hung lavatories, shower
valves, and tub surrounds.

## Cutting and drilling the frame

Rough-in is where framing gets destroyed. The notching and boring limits in
[Framing and Structural Basics](30-framing.md#drilling-and-notching-studs) are
not advisory.

- A 3 in drain does not fit through a 2×4 wall. Plan a **plumbing wall** of 2×6
  studs, or a chase, wherever a closet bend or a stack has to pass.
- **Never notch the bottom of a joist** near a bearing point, and never bore in
  the middle third of the depth.
- **Protect pipe from fasteners**: steel nail plates wherever a pipe is within
  1¼ in of the face of a stud or plate. Drywall screws find copper reliably.
- Drill holes **oversize** for plastic drain lines so the pipe can expand and so
  it is not gripped rigidly — a tightly gripped DWV line transmits noise through
  the whole frame.

## Testing

Test before anything is covered.

**DWV** — IRC P2503.5.1. Either:

- **Water test:** fill each section to **not less than 10 ft above the highest
  fitting connection** in that section, hold **15 minutes**, prove leak-free by
  inspection. (The head requirement returned to 10 ft in the 2021 IRC after a
  period at 5 ft.)
- **Air test at 5 psi**, or 10 in of mercury — **for piping other than plastic**.
- **Vacuum test** for plastic piping systems.

**Water supply** — test at working pressure or as the code requires, and leave it
under pressure while you do other work. A joint that weeps in an hour is far
cheaper to find now.

Walk the whole system before the inspector does: every trap armed, every vent
rising, every drain sloping the right way, no reducing fittings in the direction
of flow.

## Water heaters

- **Every storage water heater needs a temperature and pressure relief valve**,
  with a discharge pipe of the same size as the valve outlet, run full-size,
  downward, terminating 6 in or less above the floor or to an approved location,
  with **no valve, no threads on the end, and no reduction in size**. This valve
  is the only thing standing between a stuck thermostat and a rocket.
- Set the thermostat around 120 °F: hotter scalds, and much cooler risks
  *Legionella* growth in the tank.
- A drain pan and drain where a leak would damage the structure.
- Expansion tank where the supply has a check valve, pressure reducing valve, or
  backflow preventer — a closed system has nowhere to put the expansion of
  heated water.

## Wells and septic

Outside the sewer districts, this is most of New Hampshire.

- **Well and septic separation distances** are regulated by the state and are
  substantial. Design them together, and site them before the foundation.
- **Septic systems fail from what goes into them** more often than from age:
  grease, wipes, excessive water, and water softener discharge.
- A **septic tank is a confined space** and has killed people who entered it.
  See Safety.
- **Roof and foundation drainage must stay out of the septic field.**
- If the well is drilled, plan how the rig reaches it. If the pump is submersible,
  plan how it comes out again.

## Without utilities

- **Gravity is a complete water system** if you have head. A spring or tank
  roughly 2.3 ft above a fixture gives 1 psi; a tank 70 ft up the hill gives
  about 30 psi, which is ordinary house pressure.
- **Hand pumps** — pitcher pumps for shallow wells, deep-well hand pumps to about
  200 ft — work with no power. A deep-well hand pump installed alongside a
  submersible is a common and worthwhile redundancy here.
- **DWV still needs venting** even with no municipal sewer. A septic system's
  gases are the same gases.
- **Composting and sawdust toilets** remove the largest water demand in the
  building and the hardest plumbing to build. They are a legitimate design
  choice, not just an emergency measure, but they are a *system* requiring
  management — not simply a bucket.
- **Greywater** separated from blackwater reduces septic load substantially, and
  the rules for its reuse are jurisdictional.

## Safety

- **Sewer gas** is a genuine hazard, not just an unpleasant one. It contains
  methane (flammable) and hydrogen sulfide (toxic, and it deadens the sense of
  smell at exactly the concentrations that become dangerous). Never leave an open
  drain unplugged in an occupied building.
- **Never enter a septic tank, cistern, or deep sump.** These are oxygen-deficient
  confined spaces, and the characteristic fatality is a second person going in
  after the first. Call for professional confined-space rescue.
- **Solvent cement and primer** are flammable and the vapors are heavy. Ventilate,
  and keep them away from pilot lights and torches.
- **Soldering** starts fires. Open the wall enough to see behind the joint, use a
  flame shield, keep water and an extinguisher at hand, and **check the space an
  hour after you finish** — most torch fires in walls are discovered late.
- **Scalding**: domestic hot water above 120 °F burns quickly, and a small child
  or an older adult much faster than an adult.
- **Trenches** for water and sewer service are excavations. See the excavation
  warnings in [Site Preparation](10-site-preparation.md).
- **Old pipe** may be lead, and old solder is lead. Lead service lines and
  lead-soldered joints are a drinking-water hazard; do not disturb them without
  knowing what you have.

## Common failure modes

| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Sewer smell in one room | Dry trap seal, or a fixture with no vent losing its seal |
| Gurgling fixtures, slow drains across the house | Blocked or frost-closed vent |
| Fixture drains slowly but the line is clear | Trap arm too long, or wet-vented incorrectly |
| Chronic clog in one branch | Sanitary tee on its back, short-radius bend, or a belly in the line |
| Waste backing up into a lower fixture | Double sanitary tee, or a drain reduced in the direction of flow |
| Burst pipe at an outside wall | Pipe on the cold side of the cavity insulation |
| Split frost-proof hose bibb | Hose left connected, so the barrel could not drain |
| Banging pipes when a valve closes | No arrestor, or waterlogged air chambers, or unsupported runs |
| Slow hot water everywhere | Long trunk runs in oversized pipe |
| Ticking or creaking in walls when hot water runs | Plastic pipe clamped rigidly, unable to expand |
| Water heater relief valve dripping | Thermal expansion in a closed system with no expansion tank — or a genuine overpressure, which is worse |

## Regional assumptions

This page assumes northern New England: a winter design temperature below 0 °F
across most of New Hampshire, a deep and locally set frost line, private wells
and septic systems as the norm outside town centers, and buildings that may be
left unheated for part of the year. The frost closure vent rule, the burial
depth, and the emphasis on drainability are all consequences of that.

The code family matters as much as the climate. These numbers are IRC/IPC.
Verify the adopted edition and local amendments before relying on any of them.
