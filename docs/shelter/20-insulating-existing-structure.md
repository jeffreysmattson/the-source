# Insulating an Existing Structure

## Summary

Retrofitting insulation into a building that already exists is a different
problem from insulating one under construction. The framing is closed up, the
wiring and plumbing are already in the cavities, and every assembly — attic,
wall, rim joist, basement — has its own access constraints and its own way of
failing if the work is done wrong. There is no single procedure; the right
method depends on which assembly is being touched.

Two ideas apply to all of it. First, **air sealing comes before insulation,
not after** — moving air defeats insulation in a way that no amount of added
R-value fixes. Second, **an older house was not designed to be tight**, and
tightening it changes how it handles moisture and how its combustion
appliances get air to burn. Insulation retrofits done without accounting for
both of those routinely trade a comfort problem for a durability or safety
problem.

## Air sealing before insulating

Insulation slows *conductive* heat flow — heat moving through a solid
material. It does nothing to stop *convective* heat flow — heat carried by air
that is actually moving through or around the insulation. In an existing
house, convection through leaks and bypasses is usually the bigger loss of the
two, and it is invisible in a simple R-value number.

The classic bypasses in a northeastern balloon- or platform-framed house:

- Top plates of interior partition walls, where the wall cavity is open to the
  attic
- Plumbing and wiring penetrations through the attic floor
- Chimney and flue chases, which often run uninsulated and unsealed from
  basement to roof
- Recessed light fixtures (see *Safety*, below)
- The attic access hatch or pull-down stair, usually the single largest
  unsealed hole in the ceiling plane of the house
- Rim joists and sill plates, where the framing changes direction and sealing
  was never continuous to begin with

Stack effect drives this: warm air rises, escapes high in the house, and pulls
replacement air in low — through the rim joist, through basement penetrations,
through every one of the bypasses above. Blowing insulation over an unsealed
bypass does not stop the airflow; it just gives the moving air something to
filter through, which usually shows up years later as dirty, compacted
insulation in a streak leading straight to the leak.

The practical order of operations is **air seal first, insulate second,
ventilate third**. Seal bypasses with caulk, canned spray foam, rigid foam
cut to fit, or sheet metal and high-temperature sealant at flue chases, before
any insulation goes in on top.

## Target R-values for this region

New Hampshire spans two IECC climate zones: **Zone 5** (Cheshire,
Hillsborough, Rockingham, and Strafford counties) and the colder **Zone 6**
(Belknap, Carroll, Coös, Grafton, Merrimack, and Sullivan counties). As of this
writing, New Hampshire's state building code incorporates the **2018 IECC** by
amendment under RSA 155-A, not the current model edition — confirm both your
county's zone and the code edition your building department is actually
enforcing before treating any number below as a target rather than a
reference point.

Prescriptive minimums for new construction and full-assembly renovation,
IECC Table R402.1.3:

| Assembly | Zone 5 | Zone 6 |
|---|---|---|
| Ceiling | R-49 | R-60 (R-49 permitted only with a raised-heel truss) |
| Wood-frame wall | R-20, or R-13 cavity + R-5 continuous | R-20 + R-5 continuous, or R-13 + R-10 continuous |
| Floor over unconditioned space | R-30 | R-30 |
| Basement wall | R-15 continuous, or R-19 cavity, or R-13 + R-5 | R-15 continuous, or R-19 cavity, or R-13 + R-5 |
| Slab, depth of R-10 edge insulation | 4 ft | 4 ft |

That table describes what a code official checks on new work. It is the wrong
frame for the common retrofit case — topping up an attic, or dense-packing an
already-finished wall — where DOE and ENERGY STAR publish separate
recommendations aimed at existing homes:

| Assembly | Zone 5–6 guidance |
|---|---|
| Uninsulated attic | R-49 to R-60 |
| Attic with 3–4 in of existing insulation | Add up to R-49 total |
| Floor over unheated space | R-19 to R-38 |

The attic number is the one worth taking seriously even where it exceeds the
code minimum. Loose-fill attic insulation is the cheapest R-value available in
the entire building — there is little reason to stop at R-49 if the joist
depth and budget allow more.

## Attic

### Material choice

| Material | Notes |
|---|---|
| Blown loose-fill cellulose | Denser than fiberglass loose-fill, settles less, and — being made of shredded paper treated with borate — resists both fire and insects. Fills irregular spaces around wiring and framing well. The default choice for a full attic floor retrofit. |
| Blown loose-fill fiberglass | Lighter, cheaper per bag, non-combustible by nature, but more prone to **wind-washing** — cold air from the soffit scouring insulation value out of the material near the eaves — if baffles are inadequate. |
| Fiberglass batts | Labor-intensive to fit around joists, wiring, and obstructions in an existing attic floor with no cavity to contain them; prone to gaps that blown insulation does not have. Mostly useful for small or hard-to-blow areas, not a full attic floor. |

### Ventilation and baffles

A vented attic needs continuous intake low (soffit vents) and exhaust high
(ridge or gable vents), with airflow between them kept open along the full
underside of the roof deck. **Baffles** — rigid channels installed at each
rafter bay before insulating — hold that airflow path open at the eave, where
loose-fill insulation would otherwise migrate into the soffit and block
intake air entirely. Install baffles before blowing insulation, not after;
retrofitting them into a deep pile of existing insulation is a much worse job.

### Ice dams

Ice dams are a symptom of exactly the failure this page is about — air
leakage and insufficient insulation at the attic floor, not a defect of the
roof itself.

The mechanism: heat escaping from the living space, mostly by air leakage
through the bypasses listed above, warms the underside of the roof deck.
Snow on that warm section of roof melts. The meltwater runs down the roof
until it reaches the eave, which overhangs unheated space and stays at
outdoor temperature — where it refreezes. Ice accumulates at the eave over
successive melt-refreeze cycles until it dams standing water back under the
shingles, which then leaks into the house.

The fix is sealing and insulating the attic floor so the roof deck stays at
outdoor temperature everywhere, not just at the eaves — combined with the
soffit-to-ridge ventilation above to flush any residual warmth out before it
reaches the shingles. **Heat tape at the eave treats the ice, not the cause,
and does nothing for the heat loss driving it.** A "warmer" roof assembly
(unvented, insulated at the rafters instead of the attic floor) is a valid
alternative design, but it is a different assembly, not a patch — see
[Roofing and Weatherproofing](../building/structure/40-roofing.md) for insulated
cathedral ceilings.

!!! tip "Icicles are a diagnostic, not just a nuisance"
    A pattern of ice buildup that recurs at the same spot every winter points
    at a specific bypass or an insulation gap directly above it — often a
    bath fan duct, a recessed light, or a chimney chase. Worth investigating
    from the attic before assuming the whole ceiling needs more insulation.

### The attic hatch

Weatherstrip the hatch or pull-down stair and insulate its back to match the
surrounding field — a rigid foam box glued to the back of a hatch, or a
purpose-made insulated cover for a pull-down stair. An unsealed, uninsulated
hatch can undo a substantial fraction of an otherwise well-executed attic
retrofit by itself.

## Walls without opening them

**Dense-pack cellulose**, blown through holes drilled into each stud bay, is
the standard way to add insulation to a finished wall that was built empty or
under-filled, without removing plaster, drywall, or siding.

- Cellulose is packed to roughly **3.5 lb/ft³** — dense enough that, at that
  density, the fill itself functions as an air barrier under BPI 104 testing,
  not just a thermal one.
- Access holes are typically drilled one per stud bay from the exterior
  (through siding, later plugged and painted or capped) or from the interior
  (through drywall or plaster, later patched), sized to the blower hose.
- A borescope or infrared camera during the work catches missed cavities —
  common where fire-blocking, diagonal let-in bracing, or plumbing and wiring
  runs divide a stud bay into isolated pockets that each need their own fill
  point.

Dense-pack has real limits. It does not work in a masonry wall with no stud
cavity, and a balloon-framed wall that runs open past a floor line needs
fire-blocking addressed as part of the job, not incidentally. It is also not
a substitute for dealing with active knob-and-tube wiring in the same wall —
see *Safety*, below, before drilling into any bay that might contain it.

## Rim joist and basement or crawlspace

**Rim joist (band joist).** Per square foot, the rim joist is usually the
leakiest and least-insulated assembly in a basement — it sits at the
transition between foundation and framing, where continuous air sealing was
rarely a priority in original construction. The standard retrofit is rigid
foam board cut to fit each joist bay, sealed to the framing around its full
perimeter with canned spray foam, or two-part spray foam applied directly.
Either way, air sealing the perimeter of every bay matters more here than the
R-value of the foam itself.

**Basement walls.** The cold-climate-correct assembly insulates the exterior
of the foundation, but that means excavating around an existing house, which
is rarely practical as a retrofit. The common interior retrofit instead
applies rigid foam (XPS or polyiso) directly to the interior face of the
foundation wall, tapes the seams to control air movement, and covers the foam
with an ignition barrier — commonly 5/8 in drywall, or a code-listed coating
— since exposed foam is a code fire concern. Address any active water
intrusion before covering the wall; insulation over a wet wall hides the
problem instead of solving it.

**Crawlspace.** A vented crawlspace with insulation held up against the
underside of the floor above is the traditional approach, but it leaves
plumbing in the crawlspace exposed to freezing and the floor above cold
regardless. Current cold-climate building science generally recommends the
opposite: close the vents, run a continuous ground vapor barrier sealed at
the seams and up the walls, and insulate the crawlspace's perimeter walls
instead of the floor above it — effectively pulling the crawlspace inside the
building's thermal and air boundary. This is a bigger job than adding batts
between joists, but it performs substantially better in this climate.

## Vapor retarders — and the double vapor barrier mistake

In a cold climate, the rule is: **the vapor retarder goes on the warm-in-winter
side of the insulation** — the interior side, toward the living space. In
winter, water vapor moves from the warm, moist interior air toward the cold,
dry exterior. A retarder on the interior face slows that vapor before it
reaches cold surfaces deep in the wall where it could condense.

The mistake that causes real damage is placing a vapor retarder on **both**
faces of the same assembly — a new interior layer of polyethylene sheeting or
vinyl wallpaper added during a retrofit, on top of a wall that already has an
exterior vapor-retarding layer such as foil-faced sheathing, rigid foam
sheathing under the siding, or old asphalt-impregnated building paper. Any
moisture that gets into that cavity — from a small leak, from construction
moisture, from vapor that gets past the interior layer anyway — has nowhere
to dry in either direction. It condenses seasonally inside the wall and rots
the framing from the inside, unnoticed for years because the damage is
invisible from both faces until the wall is opened for another reason.

Before adding any vapor retarder in a retrofit: find out what is already in
the assembly. Old kraft-faced batts, foil-faced foam board, multiple coats of
oil-based paint, and rigid foam sheathing under siding all act as vapor
retarders whether or not anyone thought of them that way. Never add a second
one on the opposite face. Where the existing assembly is unclear, a
variable-permeance ("smart") membrane on the interior — tight in winter,
more open to drying in summer — or simply standard latex paint, which is
vapor-semipermeable, is safer than a second impermeable layer.

## Safety

- **Knob-and-tube wiring cannot be insulated over.** NEC 394.12(5) prohibits
  covering concealed knob-and-tube wiring with loose-fill, blown-in, rolled,
  or foamed insulation that envelops the conductors, because it prevents the
  wiring from dissipating heat and can lead to insulation breakdown and fire.
  Identify any knob-and-tube in an attic or wall before insulating over it —
  it must be addressed, generally by an electrician disconnecting and
  replacing it, before that bay or attic area is insulated. Many homeowners'
  insurers decline or cancel coverage over active knob-and-tube regardless of
  insulation status, which is its own reason to deal with it as part of the
  same project.
- **Recessed lighting must be IC-rated before insulation touches it.**
  Non-IC-rated fixtures generate heat that insulation traps, which can ignite
  the insulation; code (IRC E4004.12 and the parallel NEC listing
  requirement) requires at least 3 in of clearance from insulation for
  non-IC fixtures, while IC-rated fixtures may be buried directly. Identify
  the fixture type before blowing insulation over a ceiling with recessed
  lights — airtight IC-rated trim kits exist for retrofitting fixtures that
  cannot be fully replaced.
- **Tightening the house changes combustion air supply.** Naturally drafting
  ("atmospherically vented") furnaces, boilers, and water heaters rely on
  house infiltration for combustion air and for flue draft. Air sealing —
  and to a lesser extent, insulation work that incidentally reduces
  leakage — can starve these appliances of air or cause **backdrafting**,
  spilling combustion gases, including carbon monoxide, into the house
  instead of up the flue. This gets worse when exhaust fans (range hood,
  bath fans, a clothes dryer) depressurize the house at the same time. Have
  combustion appliances tested for spillage and draft after any significant
  air-sealing project, install carbon monoxide alarms near sleeping areas and
  combustion equipment, and treat a sealed-combustion (direct-vent)
  replacement appliance as the durable fix rather than relying on house
  leakage to feed the flame.
- **Old vermiculite attic insulation may contain asbestos.** Loose-fill
  vermiculite (often sold as Zonolite — a gray-silver, pebble-like
  loose-fill) was overwhelmingly sourced from a mine in Libby, Montana that
  was also contaminated with asbestos. EPA guidance is to assume any
  vermiculite attic insulation is contaminated and **not disturb it**: do not
  blow new insulation over it, shovel it, vacuum it, or otherwise handle it
  without professional assessment. Get it tested, and professionally removed
  if warranted, before doing any other work in an attic where it is present.
