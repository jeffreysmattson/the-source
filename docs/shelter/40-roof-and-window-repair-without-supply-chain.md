# Roof and Window Repair Without Supply Chain Access

## Summary

This page covers stopping water and air intrusion at the roof and windows when
matching shingles, glazing compound, replacement sash, or a contractor are not
available — after a storm, during a grid-down period, or any time normal supply
chains are disrupted. The goal at this stage is not a permanent repair. It is
buying time without making the eventual permanent repair harder, which means
two rules govern everything below: **shed water, don't just block it**, and
**don't trap water that gets in**. A patch that looks sealed but holds water
against the deck or the sash rots faster than an open hole would have.

Some of what follows — reglazing with putty and points, tarping technique —
is not really a "without supply chain" workaround at all. It is how roofs and
windows were repaired for most of their history, using materials (glass, wood,
tarp, putty) that can be salvaged or improvised rather than ordered.

## Materials

- Tarps — heavy-duty poly or, better, woven reinforced poly ("tarp" quality
  varies enormously; the reinforced, grommeted kind sold for agricultural or
  construction use holds up far longer than a lightweight hardware-store tarp)
- 1× or 2× wood battens for securing tarp edges
- Roofing nails or exterior screws, long enough to bite the deck through the
  batten and tarp
- Aluminum step flashing or a roll of flashing coil stock, tin snips
- Roofing cement, as a secondary sealant only
- Poly sheeting or rosin paper, as emergency underlayment
- Salvaged shingles, siding, or roofing material from a lower-priority
  structure — shed, garage, barn — if the primary roof's own material is
  unobtainable
- Plywood or OSB, sized to the full window frame, and exterior screws
- Salvaged glass (from a matching sash, a picture frame, salvaged storm
  windows, or a glazier who will cut to size), a glass cutter and straightedge
- Glazing points and glazing compound or linseed-oil putty
- Heavy plastic sheeting or shrink film, and tape, for interior storm glazing
- Ladder rated for the work, roof brackets or a harness and anchor if the roof
  and duration justify it (see Safety)

## Procedure — Roof

### 1. Tarp a damaged section correctly

A tarp that only covers the hole is the most common tarping mistake, and it
fails the first time wind drives rain uphill under the edge.

- **Run the tarp over the ridge** whenever the damage is anywhere near it, and
  let it hang well down the far slope. Water has to be shed off the tarp
  entirely, onto an undamaged part of the roof or the ground — not pooled or
  redirected onto a seam.
- **Extend past both eaves.** The tarp should overhang the drip edge on the low
  side, not stop at it.
- If one tarp does not cover the damaged area, **overlap tarps shingle-fashion**
  — upper tarp over lower, by at least 12 to 18 inches — never the reverse, and
  never edge-to-edge.
- **Secure with battens over the tarp edges**, fastened to the roof deck or
  framing through the tarp margin, not through the tarp field. Every fastener
  driven through the flat part of a tarp is a new hole in a material that
  cannot be flashed or caulked reliably. Wrap the tarp edge around the batten
  before fastening (a "burrito" wrap) so the fastener never bears directly on a
  grommet or edge that can tear out in wind.
- Batten at the ridge, at the eaves, and at intervals up the field — roughly
  every 2 feet along an edge — and add a batten across the middle of a large
  tarp so wind cannot get under the field and balloon it.

This is essentially the technique FEMA and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers
use at scale after hurricanes under **Operation Blue Roof**: reinforced
polypropylene sheeting run over the ridge and secured with wood furring strips
screwed through the sheeting's edge into the roof structure, sized generously
past the damaged area rather than trimmed tight to it. A well-installed Blue
Roof is rated to last up to about 30 days and has held up considerably longer
in practice; the same technique with a good tarp is the right target for an
improvised repair, not a lesser version of it.

### 2. Patch missing or damaged shingles with flashing, not caulk

- Slide **step flashing or a cut piece of aluminum coil stock** up under the
  course above the damaged shingle and down over the course below, so water
  runs over the patch the same way it runs over an undamaged shingle — lapped,
  not sealed face-to-face.
- A single replacement shingle, where matching stock exists, should be woven
  back into the coursing under the course above and nailed above the point
  the next course will cover, exactly as original installation.
- **Roofing cement is a backup, not a roofing material.** Use a small amount to
  tack down a lifted tab or bed a nail head, not as the primary seal across a
  patch. Cement skins over, cracks, and traps whatever water gets past the
  skin against the deck, where it does more damage hidden than the original
  leak did visible. Any patch whose water-tightness depends on a bead of
  cement rather than an overlap will eventually fail at the cement.

### 3. Improvised underlayment

- **Poly sheeting or rosin paper** can substitute for felt or synthetic
  underlayment in an emergency re-cover, run shingle-fashion up the roof with
  generous overlaps, stapled just enough to hold it until the roofing goes on
  over it.
- This is a stopgap, not a substitute for code-compliant underlayment on
  finished work: poly degrades quickly under UV and foot traffic, doesn't
  breathe, and isn't self-sealing around fasteners the way synthetic
  underlayment is. Use it to get a temporary or salvaged covering on quickly,
  and plan to replace it with proper underlayment when the roof is redone
  properly.

### 4. Salvage material from a lower-priority structure

When the primary roof's shingle or panel type is unobtainable, stripping
usable material from a shed, garage, or barn is a legitimate source, in order
of preference:

- Match **type and profile first** (asphalt to asphalt, standing-seam to
  standing-seam), color second. A mismatched patch that sheds water correctly
  is a better outcome than a matched one that doesn't.
- Pull material from the least visible or least weather-exposed slope of the
  donor structure so what remains is easiest to tarp or patch later.
- Inspect salvaged asphalt shingles for brittleness before reusing — old
  shingles crack rather than bend in cold weather, and a shingle that cracks
  during removal will crack again on the new roof.

## Procedure — Windows

### 5. Board a broken or missing window

- Cut plywood or OSB to cover the **full window frame**, not just the broken
  pane, so wind and water can't get behind the board at an exposed sash edge.
- **Screw, don't nail.** Screws hold better in wind, remove cleanly for the
  eventual real repair, and don't split trim the way nails driven for a quick
  job tend to.
- Treat boarding explicitly as a **security and moisture stopgap**, not a
  repair. A boarded window blocks light, ventilation, and — if it is a
  required bedroom egress window — the emergency escape opening the code
  requires (IRC R310). Board a required egress window only as a last resort,
  keep the fasteners removable, and restore the opening as soon as the
  hazard that required boarding has passed.

### 6. Reglaze a broken single pane

This is a durable, permanent fix using materials that can be improvised or
salvaged, not a stopgap — it is simply the traditional way a single-pane sash
was always repaired.

- Remove the remaining glass and old, hardened putty. Warming brittle old
  putty with a heat gun makes it come free in larger, safer pieces. Wear
  gloves and eye protection for this step (see Safety).
- Clean the rabbet (the recess the glass sits in) back to bare wood, and prime
  bare wood before glazing — raw wood pulls the oil out of putty and causes it
  to fail early.
- Cut salvaged glass roughly 1/16 to 1/8 inch smaller than the opening in each
  dimension, so it isn't stressed by the frame as the wood moves seasonally.
- Lay a thin bed of glazing compound or linseed-oil putty in the rabbet, set
  the glass into it, and press until the bed squeezes out to a uniform thin
  layer — this bed cushions the glass and is what actually seals the joint,
  not the visible top layer of putty.
- Set **glazing points** (small metal tabs or triangles) against the glass
  every 6 to 8 inches to hold it mechanically while the putty is still soft.
- Apply the final bead of glazing compound over the points, tool it to a
  clean bevel from the glass to the wood sash, and trim the excess. A skin
  forms in about 2 to 3 days, at which point the sash can be primed and
  painted; let the putty cure fully (weeks, not days) before a full paint
  film, and lap the paint slightly onto the glass to seal that edge.

### 7. Improvised interior storm glazing

Until a broken pane can be properly reglazed, or over a sound but leaky single
pane, an interior layer cuts heat loss and air infiltration substantially at
almost no cost:

- Tape clear poly sheeting or window shrink film to the interior trim,
  covering the full sash. Shrink film tightened with a hair dryer or heat gun
  gives a nearly invisible, taut layer and is the more durable of the two.
- This is a heat-loss and comfort measure, not a structural repair — it will
  not hold back wind-driven rain at a missing pane the way boarding does, so
  use it in addition to boarding for an actual opening, not instead of it.

### 8. Temporary fix for a failed insulated glass unit

A failed seal on a double-pane unit (visible fogging or condensation between
the panes) is not fixable with sealant — the desiccant inside is saturated and
the airspace is compromised permanently. Until the unit can be replaced:

- Tape a poly or shrink-film layer over the interior face, as in the previous
  step, to restore some of the dead-air insulating benefit and stop the
  draft, if the seal failure has also become an air leak.
- Do not attempt to reseal the unit's edge to "cure" the fogging. It will not
  work, and it can trap moisture that then supports mold growth inside the
  sealed cavity.

## Safety

- **Fall protection.** Federal OSHA requires fall protection for residential
  construction work, including roofing, at **6 feet or more above a lower
  level** (29 CFR 1926.501(b)(13)) — guardrails, safety nets, or a personal
  fall arrest system with a harness anchored to a point capable of holding the
  load. An improvised emergency repair does not suspend this: if the work is
  above 6 feet, rig an anchor and harness before going up, or do the repair
  from a ladder without stepping onto the roof.
- **Never work on a wet, icy, frosted, or frozen roof**, and never in high
  wind — asphalt shingles and most roof coverings have essentially no
  traction when wet, and a gust can catch a tarp or a person at the same time.
- **Never work alone at height if it can be avoided.** Have someone below who
  knows where you are and checks in.
- **Ladder setup.** Use the **4-to-1 rule** — the base set out 1 foot from the
  wall for every 4 feet of height to the support point — and extend the
  ladder at least 3 feet above the roof edge or landing so there is something
  solid to hold while stepping on and off (OSHA 1926.1053). Secure the base
  against a helper or stakes, and tie off the top where possible.
- **Overhead electrical service drops.** Roof edges, eaves, and gutters near
  the electrical service entrance are exactly where a ladder or a length of
  aluminum flashing is most likely to contact a live conductor. OSHA sets a
  **minimum clearance of 10 feet** between ladders, equipment, and materials
  and overhead lines up to 50 kV (29 CFR 1926.416). Use a wood or fiberglass
  ladder, never metal, anywhere near a service drop, and treat every overhead
  line as energized.
- **Glass handling.** Broken panes and old dried glazing produce sharp edges
  and brittle shards that let go without warning. Wear cut-resistant gloves
  and eye protection when removing, handling, or cutting glass, and sweep up
  and bag fragments immediately — don't leave them in a work area or a snow
  pile where they'll be found by hand or by foot later.
- **Winter roof work in the Northeast** carries added load hazards: a tarp
  must be secured against wind uplift *and* against the weight of
  accumulating wet snow or ice, which can tear an under-battened tarp loose
  or collapse a already-damaged section it's covering. Check and clear
  emergency tarps after every significant snowfall, and treat any roof with
  standing snow as untrustworthy underfoot regardless of its normal slope.
