# Emergency Shelter Construction

## Summary

This page covers building shelter with no structure available — lost, stranded,
or displaced outdoors — and the related but distinct problem of a structure
that exists but is damaged or unheatable after a disaster. In both cases the
goal is the same: stop heat loss faster than you can replace it, using
whatever is on hand.

Survival training commonly ranks priorities with the "rule of threes" — roughly
three minutes without air, three hours without shelter in a harsh environment,
three days without water, three weeks without food. Treat the specific hours
and days as a teaching mnemonic, not a clinical timeline; exposure to cold
water or high wind can kill well inside the three-hour figure, and a calm, dry
70 °F night needs no shelter at all. What the mnemonic gets right, and why
shelter outranks water and food in the field, is the ordering: exposure kills
faster than dehydration, and dehydration kills faster than starvation.

Two decisions matter more than shelter type: **where** you put it, and
**what is under you**, not what is over you. Most field shelter failures are
site-selection or ground-insulation failures, not design failures — a good
lean-to on bad ground, or with nothing but bare earth under the sleeper, fails
where a poor shelter with a good site and a good bed does not.

## Materials

**Kit** (carried in, the fast path):

- Tarp or heavy poly sheeting, at least 8 × 10 ft
- 25–50 ft of cordage (paracord or equivalent)
- Emergency/space blanket (reflective mylar)
- Folding saw or hatchet
- Fixed-blade knife
- Headlamp or flashlight — most of this work in winter happens in the dark,
  since the whole build has to finish before daylight runs out
- A shovel, if snow shelter construction is a realistic scenario

**Found materials** (no kit, or supplementing one):

- Deadfall poles for a ridgepole and ribbing
- Evergreen boughs — thatching, ground bedding, and windbreak
- Dry leaves, duff, grass, or bark for ground insulation and thatch fill
- Natural cordage from inner bark (basswood, cedar) or split roots, if no
  cordage is carried
- Snow, where present, is itself a building material and a very good
  insulator once shaped — see *Winter snow shelters* below

## Procedure

### 1. Decide: build new, or seal a room in an existing structure?

If a structure exists but is damaged, without power, or too large to heat with
what fuel is available, do not try to heat the whole building. Collapse to one
small **warm core** — a single interior room, ideally windowless or with a
small window, away from the exterior walls that lose heat fastest.

- Cover broken glazing with poly sheeting or a tarp, taped or battened at the
  edges, before doing anything else — an open window undoes any heating effort.
- Hang a blanket or tarp across the doorway to cut the room's effective air
  volume; heating a 10 × 10 ft room is a different problem than heating a whole
  floor.
- Block interior doors to unused rooms rather than heating them incidentally.
- This is the same principle as the outdoor debris hut and the quinzhee below:
  minimize the air volume you are asking a limited heat source to warm.
- See *Insulating an Existing Structure* (this section) for the retrofit
  detail, and *Wood Stove Installation and Clearances* before bringing any
  combustion heat source into a sealed room — see **Safety**, below, first.

### 2. Site selection

Apply the same logic used for siting a building (see
[Site Preparation](../building/10-site-preparation.md)) at a much smaller
scale and on a much faster timeline.

- **Avoid low ground** — ravines, narrow valleys, streambeds. Cold air is
  denser and sinks, pooling in low spots overnight; a site 20 vertical feet
  higher can be measurably warmer. Low ground also floods with little warning
  and collects biting insects in season.
- **Avoid hazard trees.** Look up before committing to a site: dead standing
  trees, and dead or broken limbs hung up in otherwise live trees
  ("widowmakers") can fall with no warning, and wind or snow load is what
  brings them down. Do not camp under a tree you have not looked up into.
- **Avoid drainage channels and runout paths** — dry channels run again with
  no local warning after rain or snowmelt upstream, and a site at the base of
  a steep slope is exposed to rockfall or avalanche debris.
- **Use, don't fight, the wind.** Put a windbreak — a rise, a boulder, dense
  evergreens — between the shelter and the prevailing winter wind, generally
  out of the northwest in this region. An exposed ridge or saddle is
  dramatically colder and windier than a site a short distance off it.
- **Site near materials, not in the middle of the only patch of them.**
  Deadfall, boughs, and (in winter) drift snow deep enough to dig are all
  shelter inputs; consuming everything within reach at the campsite trades a
  problem now for a worse one after dark.

### 3. Ground insulation before overhead cover

Build the ground layer before the roof. A body in contact with bare ground or
snow loses heat to it by direct conduction, which is far faster than the
convective and radiant losses a roof and walls address — the reason people who
build an elaborate overhead structure and lie on bare ground are cold all
night regardless of how good the roof is.

- Lay down a base of dry material — evergreen boughs (flat side down, tips
  overlapping like shingles), dry leaves, grass, or bark — thick enough that
  you cannot feel the ground through it once compressed by your body weight.
  Loose debris compresses substantially once you lie on it, so build it
  visibly overthick; a bed that looks sufficient before use is usually
  inadequate after an hour.
- A pack, foam pad, or folded extra clothing under the torso and hips
  supplements or substitutes for natural insulation and does not need drying
  out or replacing.
- In snow shelters, the sleeping platform must sit *above* the level of the
  entrance — see *Winter snow shelters*, below. Cold air sinks and pools at the
  lowest point of the shelter; sleeping at that low point defeats an otherwise
  correctly built shelter.

### 4. Three-season woodland shelter: the debris hut

The debris hut is the standard low-tool, no-kit shelter for the northeastern
forest outside of winter snow cover.

- Lean a ridgepole from the ground to a forked support (a standing tree, a
  tripod of poles, or a low branch) at roughly hip to chest height.
- Lean ribbing poles against both sides of the ridgepole at a steep angle,
  close enough together to hold debris, forming an A-frame just large enough
  to admit your body — build it close, coffin-sized, not roomy. A smaller
  interior volume is faster to warm with body heat alone and is the same
  principle used in the quinzhee and snow cave below.
- Pile loose debris — leaves, ferns, duff — over the ribbing from the bottom
  up, shingle-fashion, so upper layers shed water over lower ones. A
  commonly cited target is a finished thickness on the order of **3 ft of
  compressed debris** over the body of the shelter for near- or below-freezing
  conditions, more at colder temperatures — thick enough that daylight does
  not show through when you are inside. Loose leaves compress a great deal, so
  pile well beyond what looks sufficient.
- Build the ground bed inside to the same standard as above before closing up
  the entrance, and block the entrance itself with a stuffed pack, a bundle of
  debris, or a door of woven boughs once inside.

### 5. Lean-to with a reflector fire

Where an open fire is safe and permitted, a lean-to paired with a reflector
fire is faster to build than a debris hut and works with less skill, at the
cost of needing a maintained fire through the night.

- Build a single-pitch roof of a ridgepole and angled poles, thatched with
  boughs or debris as in the debris hut, open on the side facing the fire.
- Build a low reflector wall — logs, stacked stone, or a snow wall — on the far
  side of the fire from the shelter, angled to bounce radiant heat back toward
  the opening. This roughly doubles the usable heat from a given fire.
- Keep the fire far enough from the thatched roof and walls that a flare-up or
  wind-blown spark cannot reach them — dry debris shelters ignite readily. Clear
  the ground between fire and shelter to mineral soil.
- Someone needs to feed this fire through the night for it to keep working;
  budget fuel accordingly before dark.

### 6. Tarp and poncho configurations

With a tarp, cordage, and trees, several fast configurations cover most
conditions:

- **A-frame**: ridgeline between two anchor points, tarp draped over it and
  staked out both sides — sheds wind and rain from two directions, fully
  enclosed ends optional.
- **Lean-to**: one edge staked to the ground, the opposite edge raised and
  guyed or tied to a ridgeline — open on one side, pair with a reflector fire
  as above.
- Pitch the closed or windward side into the prevailing wind, and pitch low
  and taut in wind rather than tall and roomy — a tall pitch catches wind like
  a sail and is more likely to fail overnight.
- A natural feature — a downed tree's root wall, a boulder, a rock overhang —
  used as one side of the shelter cuts the material and time needed by half.

### 7. Winter snow shelters

Once there is enough snow to dig or pile, snow shelters outperform anything
built from bare-ground materials, because still air trapped in snow is an
excellent insulator and a snow structure can be shaped close to the body.
**Every snow shelter needs a ventilation hole, without exception — see
Safety, below, before building or sleeping in one.**

- **Snow trench**: fastest and lowest-skill option, suitable for one or two
  people with only a shovel or improvised digging tool. Dig a body-width,
  body-length trench, roof it with a tarp, skis, or branches, and pile snow
  over the roof for insulation. Adequate for a single cold night, not
  comfortable for longer.
- **Quinzhee** (piled and hollowed snow dome): pile loose snow into a mound
  roughly **5 ft high and 7–10 ft in diameter**, mixing snow of different
  densities as you pile it to help it bond, then let it sinter — harden as
  the piled snow metamorphoses — for **an hour or more** before hollowing.
  Hollow from inside, checking wall thickness by pre-marking depth on the
  digging tool; leave walls **at least 1 ft thick, 2 ft where practical**.
  Leave the floor and a sleeping platform higher than the entrance tunnel, so
  the tunnel acts as a cold sink below the sleeping area. Smooth the interior
  ceiling with a gloved hand or shovel so it doesn't drip meltwater onto
  occupants.
- **Snow cave**: dug directly into a deep drift or slope rather than a piled
  mound, using the same principles — raised sleeping platform above the
  entrance, smoothed dome ceiling, minimum wall thickness, maintained vent
  hole.
- **Size close to the body** in all three types. A smaller enclosed volume is
  faster to build, structurally sounder, and far faster for body heat alone to
  warm.
- **Build in pairs when possible**, with one person outside during hollowing
  in case of a partial collapse, and carry a shovel inside a completed shelter
  for self-rescue. Mark the shelter location visibly from a distance (skis,
  poles, a flag) for anyone searching for you.

## Safety

- **Hypothermia.** Onset is dangerous partly because it is easy to miss in
  yourself. CDC lists early signs as shivering, fatigue, loss of coordination,
  and confusion or disorientation, progressing in later stages to shivering
  stopping, bluish skin, slowed pulse and breathing, and loss of consciousness.
  Clinical staging commonly bands this as mild (roughly 95–89.6 °F core
  temperature), moderate (roughly 89.6–82.4 °F), and severe below that —
  treat these bands as approximate, since sources vary on the exact cutoffs,
  and act on symptoms rather than waiting for a temperature reading you
  usually cannot take in the field. A person who has stopped shivering in the
  cold is *more* concerning than one who is shivering hard, not less.
- **Snow shelter suffocation is a real and recurring cause of death, not a
  theoretical risk.** An unventilated snow shelter traps exhaled carbon
  dioxide, and occupants can lose consciousness within minutes with no
  warning symptoms. Maintain at least one ventilation hole through the roof at
  all times — commonly sized around **4 in diameter at 30 °F, narrowing toward
  1–2 in at 0 °F and below** — and check it periodically through the night,
  especially during active snowfall, since drifting snow closes small vents
  silently.
- **Never bring open flame or fuel-burning combustion of any kind into a
  sealed shelter of any type** — snow shelter, sealed room, tent, or debris
  hut. This includes camp stoves, lanterns, charcoal, and unvented heaters, not
  only obvious cases. CDC and CPSC data attribute dozens of deaths a year to
  fuel-burning stoves, lanterns, and charcoal used inside tents and enclosed
  camping shelters, and cracking a flap or door for ventilation is documented
  as insufficient to prevent lethal carbon monoxide buildup — sleeping
  occupants and anyone who has been drinking can die before noticing symptoms.
  A candle or coals inside a snow shelter or a sealed warm-core room is the
  same hazard.
- **Fire near debris construction.** Dry thatch and duff ignite easily and a
  wind shift can put a reflector fire's flame or embers against a shelter
  wall. Keep working distance between fire and shelter, clear fuel from
  between them, and do not leave a reflector fire unattended near a shelter.
- **Falling limbs and trees** are a hazard during both site selection (see
  above) and while cutting deadfall or firewood — look up, and be aware that
  wind and snow load can bring down a limb with no warning even in a site that
  looked safe when chosen.
- **Mark yourself for rescue.** A shelter built for concealment from weather
  is also concealment from anyone searching for you. Where a search is
  plausible, mark the site from the air and from a distance, and consider the
  trade-off before digging a shelter that disappears into a hillside.

## Regional assumptions

This page assumes the northeastern United States: mixed hardwood-softwood
forest that reliably supplies deadfall and evergreen boughs even in winter, a
winter snowpack that is typically dense and wet rather than light powder
(denser snow sinters and bonds faster for quinzhee construction but is heavier
to move), routine sub-freezing nights across the cold season, and short winter
daylight that compresses the build window. In a dry or sparsely forested
climate, material availability and snow behavior both change and the specific
construction notes above may not transfer directly.
