# Water Storage and Rotation

## Summary

Treating water solves contamination; storage solves the much larger problem of
having none on hand when a well pump loses power, a boil-water order runs for
days, or a storm closes the roads to town. The core numbers are simple — a
gallon per person per day, for at least two weeks — but getting there safely
depends on container choice, storage location, and a rotation habit, since a
container storing water for months behaves differently than one you fill and
drink from in an afternoon.

This page covers **how much to store, what to store it in, where to keep it,
and how to rotate it.** For how to make water safe in the first place, see
[Water Purification](20-water-purification.md); its "Storing treated water"
section is the short version of what follows here.

!!! warning "Stored water is not automatically safe forever"
    A sealed container of properly treated water is safe for a long time, but
    a broken seal, a long enough interval, or an unsuitable container can
    quietly turn stored water into a source of illness rather than a backup
    supply. When in doubt about a specific container's history or age,
    re-treat before drinking — see [Re-treating stored water](#re-treating-stored-water)
    below.

## How much to store

### The baseline: one gallon per person per day

The CDC, Ready.gov, and the American Red Cross all use the same baseline
figure: **at least one gallon of water per person per day**, covering
drinking, food preparation, and basic hygiene. The Red Cross breaks that
gallon down further — a normally active person needs to *drink* at least two
quarts (half a gallon) a day, with the remaining half gallon going to cooking
and hygiene. One gallon is a survival-level number: it assumes minimal
hygiene, no laundry, and no toilet flushing, not a comfortable normal
routine.

### Multipliers on top of the baseline

All three agencies flag the same factors as reasons to store more than the
one-gallon baseline, though none publishes a precise multiplier for each —
treat these as "store noticeably more," not as exact math:

- **Heat.** Hot conditions can double water needs. This matters less as a
  year-round New Hampshire concern than it does in a summer heat wave or for
  physically active outdoor work.
- **Pregnancy and nursing.** Add roughly an extra half gallon per day per
  pregnant or nursing person.
- **Illness.** Sick household members need more water, particularly with
  fever, vomiting, or diarrhea, which is also when clean water matters most.
- **Children.** Ready.gov and the CDC both note children need more water
  relative to body size than the flat per-person number implies.
- **Pets.** Ready.gov's rule of thumb is roughly a half gallon per day for a
  medium dog and a quarter gallon for a cat; scale for a large dog, multiple
  animals, or livestock, which are not covered by any of these figures at
  all and need their own calculation.
- **Medical equipment.** Anyone using equipment that requires clean water
  (CPAP humidifiers, wound care, certain medications reconstituted with
  water) should add a dedicated reserve for that use, separate from the
  household drinking supply.

### Duration: two weeks is the standard target

Government guidance is consistent that **three days is the bare minimum and
two weeks is the actual target**: the CDC's water page recommends storing a
2-week supply "if feasible," and the Red Cross states plainly that "you
should store at least a two-week supply of water for each member of your
family." A single person storing two weeks at the one-gallon baseline needs
14 gallons; a household should multiply straight across.

**Longer-term storage** (a month or more, or an ongoing reserve rather than a
single emergency buffer) is a personal choice none of these agencies
specifically size — none of them publishes a number past two weeks. If you
store beyond that, the container and rotation guidance below matters more,
not less, since the water sits longer between checks.

| Household size | 3-day minimum | 2-week target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 person | 3 gal | 14 gal |
| 2 people | 6 gal | 28 gal |
| 4 people | 12 gal | 56 gal |
| 4 people + 1 dog | ~13.5 gal | ~63 gal |
| 6 people | 18 gal | 84 gal |

Figures are the 1 gal/person/day baseline with no heat, illness, or pregnancy
multiplier applied — add accordingly for your household.

## Container types

### What's actually food-grade

"Food-grade" plastic is rated safe for contact with food and drink and
doesn't leach harmful compounds into what it holds — the Red Cross and
Ready.gov both point to it as the standard. In practice this means:

- **High-density polyethylene (HDPE), recycling code #2.** This is what
  water storage barrels, camping water jugs, and most commercial water
  containers are made from. It's rigid, opaque or can be made opaque,
  chemically stable, and doesn't degrade meaningfully over years of storage.
- **PET (recycling code #1),** the material of a standard 2-liter soda
  bottle. Ready.gov specifically calls out cleaned 2-liter soft drink bottles
  as an acceptable reused container, in contrast to milk or juice jugs (see
  below).
- **New, food-grade IBC totes** (275–330 gal cube tanks) for whole-house
  quantities, provided the tote is documented food-grade and was never used
  to hold chemicals. A reconditioned tote of unknown history is a real risk —
  even a thorough cleaning cannot guarantee residue from a prior industrial
  contents is gone, and there is no reliable way to verify a used tote's
  history without documentation from the seller.
- **Stackable modular containers** ("water bricks" and similar products),
  typically food-grade HDPE in flat rectangular or brick-shaped units
  designed to interlock. Their advantage is space efficiency in a closet or
  under a bed rather than any material difference from a barrel.

### Containers to avoid, and why

!!! warning "Don't reuse milk or juice jugs"
    Ready.gov's own guidance on preparing your own water containers names
    plastic milk jugs and juice jugs specifically as containers **not** to
    use, because "milk protein and fruit sugars cannot be adequately removed
    from these containers and provide an environment for bacterial growth"
    even after washing. University extension programs add related, slightly
    different reasons: Oregon State University Extension notes the jugs are
    made from a thinner plastic intended for short-term retail use, not
    long-term storage, and that it breaks down over time; Mississippi State
    and Utah State Extension add that the hollow carrying handle is very
    difficult to clean and sanitize inside. The sources don't agree on which
    single reason is primary, but they agree on the conclusion: don't reuse
    milk or juice containers for stored water.

- **Milk and juice jugs** — see above. Use a cleaned 2-liter soda bottle
  instead if you're reusing retail containers.
- **Cardboard containers** (including gallon jugs with foil/cardboard
  laminate) — Ready.gov flags these as prone to leaking and not durable
  enough for extended storage.
- **Any container that ever held a toxic or petroleum substance** (bleach,
  pesticide, motor oil, fuel) — both Ready.gov and the CDC are explicit that
  no amount of cleaning makes these safe for water storage. Plastic absorbs
  trace amounts of what it previously held.
- **Glass** — this is a case where sources genuinely disagree. The Red
  Cross's own "Food and Water in an Emergency" guidance lists glass among
  acceptable storage containers (alongside plastic, fiberglass, and
  enamel-lined metal). Ready.gov and several university extension programs,
  by contrast, advise against it because of breakage risk and weight. Glass
  does have the advantage of not leaching anything into the water over long
  storage, which is a real reason to prefer it for a small emergency
  reserve. For anything larger than a few gallons, or for storage in a
  location where a dropped or frozen container is likely, the breakage
  concern is the practical one — treat glass as suitable for a modest,
  carefully-placed reserve, not for bulk storage.

### Mylar and aluminized bags

Heavy-gauge (5 mil or thicker) aluminized Mylar bags, the same material used
for long-term dry food storage, are sold specifically as water storage
bladders, sometimes bundled with a long-shelf-life chemical treatment. They
are lightweight, collapse flat when empty, and block light completely, which
suits them to a "store dry goods and water together" pantry approach. Their
tradeoff is durability: a bag depends entirely on its heat seal and puncture
resistance, with no rigid structure to protect it, so it belongs in a box or
bin rather than loose on a shelf, and it is a poor choice for a location
where anything might be stacked against or dropped on it. Treat Mylar water
bags as a supplement to rigid HDPE storage, not a full replacement for it —
this is a thinner base of independent testing than the rigid-container
guidance above, which comes directly from CDC, Ready.gov, and Red Cross
publications.

### Container comparison

| Container | Typical size | Best for | Key caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| HDPE water jug/jerrican | 1–7 gal | Portable, grab-and-go supply | Opaque, food-grade only |
| Reused 2-liter PET soda bottle | 2 L | Free, small-scale supplementing | Clean thoroughly; not for years-long storage |
| Stackable modular container ("water brick") | ~1.5–3.5 gal each | Closet/under-bed storage, portability | Cost per gallon is higher than a barrel |
| 55-gal HDPE barrel | 55 gal | Whole-household base supply | ~460 lb full; needs a hand pump or siphon to draw from |
| IBC tote | 275–330 gal | Whole-property or retreat-scale reserve | Must be new/documented food-grade; ~2,300+ lb full |
| Mylar/aluminized water bag | Varies | Space-constrained, light-blocking storage | No rigid protection; box it |
| Glass jug | 1–5 gal | Small reserve, zero-leach storage | Breakage and weight; avoid bulk use |
| Commercially bottled water | Retail sizes | Convenience, no prep needed | See below on shelf life |

## Storage conditions

Every source agrees on the same basic environment: **opaque, cool, dark, and
away from anything with a smell.**

- **Opaque or shaded from light.** Light drives algae growth in stored water.
  If a container is translucent, keep it in a dark closet, wrap it, or store
  it inside a box.
- **Cool and stable temperature.** The CDC's specific range is **50–70°F**.
  Heat accelerates any degradation of the container itself and any breakdown
  of a chlorine residual.
- **Off a bare concrete floor.** Concrete can transmit chemicals to a
  container's underside over long contact, and a concrete slab is also the
  coldest surface in an unheated space — exactly where freezing risk is
  highest. Set containers on a pallet, boards, or a shelf rather than
  directly on the slab.
- **Away from gasoline, solvents, pesticides, and cleaning products.**
  Oregon State University Extension notes that plastic containers can absorb
  strong odors from nearby chemicals and transfer that smell — and by
  extension the chemical itself — to the water. Don't share shelving between
  the water reserve and the garage chemical shelf.
- **Sealed, and left sealed until use.** Every source's storage instruction
  includes sealing the container tightly and not reopening it until you're
  ready to use the water; once opened, use it rather than resealing and
  returning it to long-term storage.
- **Labeled with the fill date.** Needed for rotation — see below.

### The New Hampshire-specific problem: freezing

None of the national guidance above is written with a hard winter climate in
mind, and it shows: freezing is the dominant storage hazard in New Hampshire
and gets no mention in the CDC or Ready.gov pages at all. Water expands by
about 9% as it freezes (per the USGS), and a rigid, fully sealed container
has nowhere for that expansion to go — it cracks the container, splits a
seam, or pops a lid, and a cracked container is a silent failure you won't
discover until you need the water and find the barrel empty or the water
contaminated by whatever leaked in through the crack.

This is a real planning problem for a typical NH home, where the available
storage space is rarely heated:

- **Unheated basement.** Often the best available option — usually stays
  above freezing even in winter because of ground contact and heat loss from
  the house above, but verify this for your specific basement rather than
  assuming it; a walkout basement or one with an uninsulated slab can still
  freeze in a cold snap.
- **Unheated garage or outbuilding.** Commonly drops well below freezing for
  the entire winter in this region. Full, rigid, sealed containers stored
  here are at real risk. If this is your only space, either move the water
  supply to a heated area for winter, leave meaningful headspace (10% or
  more) in every rigid container so it has room to expand, or store in
  flexible containers (collapsible jugs, Mylar bags) that can bulge rather
  than crack.
- **Heated living space.** Safest for the water, but competes with normal
  living space — a case for the compact stackable containers over a 55-gallon
  barrel if storage is going in a closet rather than a basement.

If a container does freeze, let it thaw fully and inspect it before use —
treat a frozen-and-thawed container the same as one with a broken seal (see
re-treatment below), since ice expansion is exactly the kind of stress that
opens a hairline crack you can't see from outside.

## Commercially bottled water

Store-bought sealed water is the simplest option and needs no preparation,
but its labeling is more confusing than it looks.

- **The FDA does not require an expiration date on bottled water.** Water
  that is produced under standard food safety regulations and kept in an
  unopened, properly sealed container is treated by the FDA as having an
  indefinite safety shelf life — it does not become unsafe simply because
  time has passed.
- **"Best by" dates on the bottle are about taste, not safety.** Many
  bottlers print a date anyway, typically one to two years out, largely as a
  voluntary practice or a plastic-shelf-life estimate rather than a
  regulatory requirement. Long storage can change the water's taste or the
  bottle's appearance without making the water unsafe.
- **This is a case where "indefinite" claims deserve a caveat.** The
  indefinite-shelf-life claim applies to the *water*; it assumes the seal
  hasn't been broken, the bottle hasn't been exposed to heat or sunlight for
  years, and the plastic itself hasn't degraded. Treat "indefinite" as "very
  long under good conditions," not as a reason to skip checking bottles
  during rotation.
- **Ready.gov's own instruction is simpler than the shelf-life debate: store
  it in the sealed original container, in a cool, dark place, and observe
  the printed date anyway** as a practical rotation trigger even if the
  underlying safety margin is longer.

## Treating water before long-term storage

Whether you're filling containers from the tap or from a treated field
source, the water going into long-term storage should already be safe:

- **Municipal tap water** is already treated at the utility and needs no
  additional treatment before storage — fill clean, food-grade containers
  directly.
- **Well or spring water** of unknown or unverified safety should be treated
  before storage: filter to clear, then either bring to a rolling boil for
  one minute (three minutes above about 6,500 ft) and let cool, or add
  unscented household bleach at the standard dose. See
  [Water Purification](20-water-purification.md) for full dosing.
- **A light bleach dose helps the water hold up in storage**, not just at the
  moment of treatment. Some extension guidance (Oregon State) recommends
  adding a small chlorine dose specifically for water destined for storage,
  separate from a boil step, because the residual chlorine helps suppress
  regrowth over the following months — boiling alone kills what's present
  but leaves no residual protection.

## First-in, first-out rotation

Storage without rotation just becomes a shelf of water you're afraid to
drink. A simple FIFO system fixes that:

1. **Label every container with the fill date** when you fill it — a paint
   pen or permanent marker directly on the container, not a sticker that
   falls off.
2. **Arrange storage so the oldest water is drawn first.** In practice this
   means loading a shelf or rack from one end and drawing from that same
   end, the same principle used for canned food rotation: new containers go
   to the back, older ones move to the front for use.
3. **Set a recurring check**, not just a mental note. Twice a year — spring
   and fall, or tied to another recurring household task like changing smoke
   detector batteries — inspect every container: check the seal, look for
   cloudiness or sediment, smell it, and confirm the label's age.
4. **Replace home-filled water every six months.** This is the consistent
   figure across the CDC, Ready.gov, the Red Cross, and university extension
   guidance for water you treated and bottled yourself. It is a
   conservative, easy-to-remember number rather than a hard safety cliff —
   water doesn't necessarily go bad at six months and one day — but rotating
   on schedule means you're never relying on water old enough for anyone to
   have to wonder.
5. **Use rotated-out water rather than dumping it** where practical — garden
   watering, cleaning, or topping off a rain barrel are all reasonable uses
   for water you're cycling out of the drinking reserve.
6. **Commercially bottled water can run on the printed date** as its own
   rotation trigger, separate from the six-month homemade-water cycle, since
   it's already sealed and dated at the factory.

## Re-treating stored water

Treat any of the following as reasons to re-treat before drinking, not just
inspect:

- **The seal is broken, cracked, or was ever opened and reclosed.** A broken
  seal means the water has had contact with air and whatever was in it,
  regardless of how long ago that happened.
- **The container froze and thawed.** As above, ice expansion stresses seams
  and lids in a way that can open a leak path invisible from the outside.
- **Storage time is at or beyond your rotation point** (six months for
  home-filled water) and you're using it rather than dumping and refilling.
- **Anything looks, smells, or tastes off** — cloudiness, sediment, a musty
  or chemical smell, or a flat taste are all reasons to treat before
  drinking. A flat taste alone (from dissolved oxygen loss, not
  contamination) can often be fixed by pouring the water back and forth
  between two containers to re-aerate it, but that's a taste fix, not a
  safety step — it does not substitute for re-treatment if any of the above
  also apply.

Re-treatment for stored water uses the same methods as treating it the first
time: a rolling boil for one minute, or a fresh dose of unscented household
bleach at the standard rate, with 30 minutes of contact time. Full dosing,
including the drop and milliliter measurements, is in
[Water Purification](20-water-purification.md).
