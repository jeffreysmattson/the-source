# Water Purification

## Summary

Water from a roof, well, stream, or rain barrel looks clear but can carry the
bacteria, viruses, and parasites that cause waterborne illness. Making it safe
is a two-part job: **get it clear**, then **kill what you can't see.** No single
step does both — sediment must come out before chemical disinfection works well,
and disinfection does not remove chemicals or heavy metals.

The methods below are the standard emergency field values used by the CDC and
American Red Cross. They assume you have no laboratory testing available, which
is exactly the situation this wiki is for: **when in doubt, treat the water as
contaminated.**

!!! warning "Not a substitute for professional help"
    This page is for situations where a tested municipal supply or professional
    water treatment is unavailable. If you have access to bottled water, a known
    safe well, or a water authority's guidance, prefer those.

## Quick reference

| Method | Dose / rule | Contact time | Removes | Key caveat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Boiling** | Rolling boil 1 min (3 min above ~6,500 ft) | — | Bacteria, viruses, parasites | Not chemicals or sediment; clarify first |
| **Bleach** (unscented, ~6%) | 8 drops per gallon (~2 per L); double if you can't clear it | 30 min | Bacteria, viruses | Weak vs. some parasite cysts; must smell faintly of chlorine after |
| **Iodine** (2% tincture) | 8 drops/gal clear, 16 cloudy (~5 / ~10 per L) | 30 min | Bacteria, viruses, parasites* | Not for pregnancy, thyroid problems, or long-term use; *less reliable in cold water |
| **Filtration** | 0.1–0.2 µm absolute (bacteria/protozoa); ~0.03 µm or virus-rated for viruses | — | Particles + microbes per rating | Best as the first clarifying step; carbon is for taste/chemicals, not germs |
| **SODIS** (sunlight) | Clear PET bottle in full sun | 6 h clear / 2 days overcast | Bacteria, viruses, many protozoa | Free; needs clear water and real sun; not chemicals |

## Measuring the dose

"Drops" is not a standard unit — a drop's size depends on the tip and the liquid.
The working value behind the doses above is **1 drop ≈ 0.05 mL (50 µL), about 20
drops per milliliter**, which is what a standard medicine dropper delivers. That
makes:

- Bleach (~6%): 8 drops/gallon ≈ **0.4 mL per gallon** (≈ 0.1 mL per liter)
- Iodine (2%): 8 drops/gallon clear ≈ **0.4 mL**; 16 cloudy ≈ **0.8 mL**

**Best tool: a marked oral syringe (1–3 mL).** Measuring by volume removes the
ambiguity of counting drops — draw up exactly 0.4 mL, squirt it into the
container, then stir. A standard medicine dropper/eyedropper (~20 drops/mL) is
what "8 drops" refers to and is fine for single-gallon doses. For a full barrel
or IBC, don't count hundreds of drops — measure by the milliliter (55 gal ≈ 22 mL
bleach; 275 gal ≈ 110 mL). An unmarked squeeze bottle or a container's spout gives
inconsistent drop sizes; avoid relying on it.

## Step 0 — Get it clear first

Chemical disinfection fails in cloudy or dirty water because suspended organics
consume the chlorine or iodine before it reaches the pathogens. So the order of
operations is always: **settle → filter to clear → disinfect.**

1. Let standing water sit so grit and sediment settle, then draw from the top.
2. Pass it through a cloth, then a finer filter (coffee filter, then 0.1–0.2 µm
   if you have one). The goal is water that looks clear enough to read a page
   through.
3. Only then add chemical disinfectant or boil.

## Boiling

The most reliable method for biological contamination and the one that needs no
consumables.

- Bring water to a **full rolling boil** (not a simmer) for **1 minute** at sea
  level, or **3 minutes** above roughly **6,500 ft (2,000 m)** elevation.
- Kills bacteria, viruses, and parasites. A longer boil does no harm if you want
  margin; the altitude adjustment matters only because boiling temperature drops
  with altitude — in practice a solid rolling minute is the working rule.
- **Does not** remove chemicals, heavy metals, nitrates, or sediment. If your
  concern is chemical contamination (industrial runoff, fuel, saltwater),
  boiling is the wrong tool — use distillation.
- Let it cool before storing; store in clean, sealed containers.

## Household bleach (chlorine)

Plain **unscented** liquid household bleach releases free chlorine, which kills
most bacteria and viruses. This is the most common chemical method because the
bottle is already in the house.

- **Use only plain, unscented household bleach with no additives.** Not scented,
  not "splashless," not color-safe, not a concentrate for laundry. It should list
  sodium hypochlorite as the active ingredient, typically **5–9%** (about 6% is
  common in the US). The dose below assumes ~6%. If your bottle prints a
  different strength, scale proportionally — and when you can't tell how old or
  weakened it is, assume it is weaker.
- **Clear water: 8 drops per gallon** (about **2 drops per liter**).
- **Cloudy water you could not clear:** use twice that dose, but understand the
  result is less certain — clarifying first is strongly preferred.
- Stir, then let it stand **30 minutes**.
- **The check that matters:** after 30 minutes there should be a *slight*
  chlorine smell and taste. If there is none, the dose was too low (old or
  concentrated-down bleach) — add another small amount and wait 15 more minutes.
  A faint residual is what protects stored water; a strong, harsh taste means you
  over-dosed (harmful in large amounts but not from a single glass).
- Target free-chlorine residual is roughly **0.2–0.5 ppm**; up to about **4 ppm**
  is considered safe to drink.
- **Limitation:** chlorine kills bacteria and viruses well but is *less reliable
  against some parasite cysts* (notably *Cryptosporidium*). If you suspect
  protozoan contamination from surface water, prefer boiling or a proper filter.
- Bleach degrades with age, heat, and light — a bottle that's been sitting in the
  garage for years under-doses. The "does it still smell of chlorine" check above
  is your safeguard.

## Iodine

Tincture of iodine (**2%**) or iodine-based purification tablets are a good
secondary method and useful where you don't want to store bleach long-term.

- **Clear water: 8 drops of 2% tincture per gallon** (about **5 drops per
  liter**). **Cloudy water: 16 drops per gallon** (about **10 per liter**).
- Let stand **30 minutes**.
- Tablets follow their own label — typically one tablet per quart or gallon; read
  the specific product.
- **Limitations:** iodine is less effective than chlorine, and *less effective in
  cold or alkaline water* (a real consideration in a northeastern climate). It
  tints and flavors the water.
- **Do not use long-term or for daily drinking**, and **avoid it during
  pregnancy** or with thyroid conditions — iodine accumulates and affects the
  thyroid. Treat it as an emergency/short-term method, not a permanent supply.

## Filtration

Filtration physically removes particles and, depending on the rating, microbes.
It is the best **first step** (it gets water clear for chemical treatment) and on
its own can make biologically contaminated water safe.

- **0.1–0.2 micron (absolute)** removes bacteria and protozoa (e.g., *Giardia*,
  *Cryptosporidium*).
- **Viruses are smaller** — reliably removing them takes about **0.03 micron** or
  a filter explicitly rated for viruses. Many "0.1 micron" filters are *not*
  virus-rated; check the spec.
- **Ceramic candle filters** handle bacteria/protozoa well and can be scrubbed
  or re-burned to clean them.
- **Activated carbon** improves taste and odor and removes some chemicals, but it
  does **not** remove pathogens by itself — pair it with a disinfection step.
- A filter rated for the organisms you actually need to remove beats a higher
  micron number on paper.

## Solar water disinfection (SODIS)

Free, no-consumable, and genuinely effective in sunny conditions — worth knowing
for preparedness.

- Use **clear PET plastic bottles** (the kind soda or water comes in), filled to
  about 10 cm from the top, laid **horizontally in direct sun**.
- **Clear sky: ~6 hours.** **Overcast: ~2 consecutive days.**
- The water must be **clear** — turbid water blocks the UV that does the work.
- Kills bacteria, viruses, and many protozoa via UV-A plus heat. Does **not**
  remove chemicals or sediment.

## Distillation

The one method that handles **chemical** contamination as well as biological.

- Boil the water, capture the vapor, and condense it on a cool surface into a
  clean container. Microbes and most dissolved solids, heavy metals, nitrates,
  and salts stay behind in the boiling pot; the condensate is very pure.
- It does **not** remove *volatile* compounds (some solvents, alcohol), which
  co-distill with the water.
- Downside: energy-intensive, slow, and low-volume. Use it when the concern is
  chemicals or heavy metals rather than just germs.

## What does *not* make water safe

A few common ideas are myths. Naming them here so they don't get used in a pinch:

- **Vinegar.** A frequent suggestion, but vinegar (~5% acetic acid) is **not a
  reliable drinking-water disinfectant.** Lowering the pH can slow some growth
  and it helps with taste, odor, and cleaning containers — but it does not
  reliably kill the viruses, bacteria, or parasites that cause waterborne
  illness. Do not rely on it to make water safe. (A little in already-safe water
  is harmless; that's flavoring, not purification.)
- **Salt.** Does not disinfect and can actually encourage some bacterial growth.
- **Rubbing alcohol / isopropyl.** Not a drinking-water treatment — the dose
  needed would be toxic, and it isn't effective for this purpose.

## Storing treated water

Disinfection leaves a residual that keeps stored water protected, but that
residual decays with time, light, and temperature.

- Store treated water in **clean, opaque, tightly sealed** containers (opaque to
  stop algae; sealed against recontamination).
- **First-in, first-out:** use the oldest water first and rotate on a schedule.
- **Re-treat** (re-chlorinate or boil) if water has been stored for weeks or
  more, or if a container's seal was broken.
- In cold climates, keep stored drinking water above freezing — a frozen,
  re-thawed container is a recontamination risk.
