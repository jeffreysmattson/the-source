# Surface Water Sourcing and Risk Assessment

## Summary

A stream, pond, river, or lake is often the largest volume of water visible
from a property, and in a prolonged emergency it may be the only option once
stored water runs out. But raw surface water is also the least predictable
source there is: it carries whatever the watershed upstream of the draw point
is doing, and that can change with a rainstorm, a thaw, or a warm week in a
way a well or municipal supply does not.

This page covers how to **choose and collect** from a surface source with the
lowest achievable risk before any treatment happens. It does not cover
treatment — that is [Water Purification](20-water-purification.md), and this
page ends by pointing there rather than repeating it. One risk covered here
does change the treatment answer: **harmful algal bloom toxins are not
reliably destroyed by boiling**, which is the one case where source selection
and treatment interact directly (see below).

!!! warning "Not a substitute for a known-safe source"
    This page is for situations where a tested municipal supply, a known-safe
    well, or bottled water is unavailable. If any of those exist, prefer them
    over untreated surface water regardless of how careful the site selection
    is. No amount of upstream inspection substitutes for a water quality test,
    and none of the judgment calls below are a guarantee — they only reduce
    the odds.

## Contamination risk factors: what's upstream

Surface water has no walls and no casing. Everything that touches the ground
in its watershed is a potential input. Before drawing water, account for what
sits upstream (or up-slope, for a pond or lake) of the point you'd collect
from:

- **Agriculture and livestock.** Pastures, feedlots, and manure storage or
  spreading areas contribute bacteria (including fecal coliform and
  pathogens like *Giardia* and *Cryptosporidium*), and cropland contributes
  fertilizer and pesticide runoff. Cattle with direct access to a stream bank
  are a concentrated, ongoing source right at the water's edge, not just a
  seasonal one.
- **Septic systems.** A failing or poorly sited septic tank or leach field
  near a shoreline is a direct pathway for sewage-borne bacteria, viruses, and
  nutrients into surface water. Septic setback requirements from surface
  water are set by state and local health codes and vary widely — commonly
  somewhere in the **25–200 ft range** depending on the jurisdiction and the
  water body's classification — so treat any specific number as a guess and
  check with the local health department or county if you need to know
  whether a given shoreline is likely to have systems close to the water.
- **Industrial and commercial sites.** Old mill sites, fuel storage, salvage
  yards, and active industrial discharge points can put chemicals and heavy
  metals into water that neither boiling nor standard filtration removes.
  This is a case where the concern is not biological at all — see
  [Water Purification's section on distillation](20-water-purification.md)
  for the one method that addresses chemical contamination.
- **Road runoff and de-icing salt.** Paved roads and parking areas shed
  petroleum residue, and in a northeastern climate, winter road salt
  application raises sodium and chloride levels in nearby surface water,
  sometimes for months after the snow is gone. A source running alongside or
  just downhill of a regularly salted or plowed road carries this load.
- **Wildlife density.** Even with no human development nearby, water with
  heavy beaver, waterfowl, or other wildlife traffic can carry meaningful
  pathogen loads — *Giardia* is nicknamed "beaver fever" for a reason. A
  pristine-looking backcountry pond is not a sterile one.

None of these are visible in the water itself most of the time. Reading the
land around the source — is there a farm upslope, a cluster of shorefront
septic systems, a road crossing, a beaver lodge — matters as much as
inspecting the water.

## Biological risks

### *Giardia* and *Cryptosporidium*

These two protozoan parasites are the standing baseline risk of untreated
surface water, present in a large share of U.S. surface water and not
reliably signaled by clarity, taste, or smell. The CDC states plainly that
water straight from lakes, rivers, springs, ponds, or streams "is generally
not safe to drink," and recommends assuming any such source is contaminated
regardless of appearance. Both organisms are shed in the feces of infected
humans and animals (livestock, pets, and wildlife alike), which is why
upstream land use and wildlife density both matter.

The relevant good news: both are reliably killed by a rolling boil, and both
are removed by a filter rated for "cyst"/"oocyst" reduction (NSF 53 or 58, or
an absolute pore size of 1 micron or smaller) — see
[Water Purification](20-water-purification.md) for the specifics. Chlorine is
notably *less* reliable against *Cryptosporidium* specifically, which is a
reason to prefer boiling or filtration when a source has a strong protozoan
risk profile (livestock or heavy wildlife use upstream).

### Bacteria and viruses

Sewage, septic, and manure inputs also carry fecal bacteria and viruses. This
is the standard case that boiling, chlorine, iodine, and most filtration
methods all address — nothing about this differs from the general treatment
guidance already covered in [Water Purification](20-water-purification.md).

### Harmful algal blooms and cyanotoxins

This is the one hazard where the normal rules break down, and it deserves its
own warning separate from the rest of this page.

Warm, nutrient-rich still water — ponds and lakes especially — can grow
blooms of cyanobacteria ("blue-green algae") that produce toxins:
microcystins (liver), cylindrospermopsin (liver and kidney, with DNA damage
at higher exposure), and anatoxins (neurological, capable of causing
respiratory failure in severe cases). Symptoms in people range from skin
irritation and gastrointestinal illness to, in extreme cases, life-threatening
effects. Blooms are also frequently fatal to dogs that drink affected water or
lick it off their fur.

!!! danger "Boiling does not make bloom-affected water safe"
    The CDC states directly: **"Boiling water containing toxins will not make
    it safe to drink. Boiling does not remove toxins and can concentrate the
    toxin."** Heat ruptures algal cells and releases more toxin into the
    water, and as water boils off, whatever toxin is present becomes *more*
    concentrated, not less. This is the opposite of how boiling works against
    bacteria, viruses, and protozoa, and it is the reason this page exists
    separately from the purification page: **source selection is the primary
    defense against cyanotoxins, because treatment is not.**

    Standard filtration and disinfection are not reliable against cyanotoxins
    either. Some point-of-use filters are specifically tested and certified
    to reduce microcystin (look for NSF/ANSI 53 or 477 certification naming
    microcystin by name), and granular or powdered activated carbon can
    adsorb dissolved cyanotoxins — but an uncertified filter, boiling,
    chlorine, or iodine should all be assumed ineffective against this
    hazard. If a source shows bloom signs, the correct response is to **not
    collect from it at all**, not to collect and treat harder.

Visual and sensory warning signs of a bloom — treat any of these as a reason
to walk away from that source entirely:

- Water that looks discolored: green, blue-green, reddish-brown, or like
  spilled paint
- Surface scum, foam, or mats, especially collected along a downwind shore
- A musty, grassy, or unusually foul odor
- Dead or dying fish, or sick/dead waterfowl, along the shore

Blooms are not always visible — a waterbody can carry cyanotoxins without an
obvious surface bloom — but the presence of any of the above is a hard stop.

## Regional and seasonal considerations (northeastern US / New Hampshire)

This wiki assumes a northeastern US setting, and New Hampshire specifically.
That changes the calendar of risk:

- **Spring snowmelt and runoff surges.** Snowmelt combined with spring rain
  produces the year's highest flows and the highest sediment and pathogen
  loading, as the thaw flushes a winter's accumulation of manure, road
  residue, and de-icing salt off the land all at once. Turbidity is often at
  its worst in this window, which also makes chemical disinfection less
  reliable (see [Water Purification's clarification step](20-water-purification.md)).
- **Summer harmful algal bloom season.** NH DES tracks cyanobacteria blooms
  on lakes and ponds statewide through its Healthy Swimming program, with
  reports concentrated in the warmer months (roughly June through October)
  and dozens of NH waterbodies affected in a typical year. Guidance and
  monitoring change over time — check the NH DES Healthy Swimming Mapper for
  current advisories on a specific waterbody rather than assuming last
  year's status still holds.
- **Winter road salt runoff.** Road salt applied through the winter continues
  releasing into nearby surface water well after the snow is gone, elevating
  sodium and chloride for an extended period. A source near a state or town
  road that gets salted is affected essentially year-round, not just in
  winter.
- **High water table and shallow bedrock.** New Hampshire's glacial silts,
  shallow bedrock, and high spring water table mean septic systems and
  drainage in general have less soil to filter through before reaching
  surface water than in a region with deep, well-drained soils. Treat
  shoreline septic density as a higher-consequence risk factor here than a
  generic national guide might suggest.

## Site selection and collection technique

Given a choice of access points on the same water body, these factors reduce
risk before any treatment step:

### Position relative to contamination sources

Draw from **upstream** of anything identified above — pastures, septic
fields, road crossings, marinas, developed shoreline — never downstream of
it. On a river or stream this is directional and usually obvious from a map.
On a pond or lake, "upstream" means upwind and away from inflows, developed
shoreline, and any visible discharge or drainage point; water tends to
collect surface contaminants on the downwind shore.

How far is "far enough" is jurisdiction- and situation-dependent — it depends
on flow rate, soil type, slope, and the size of the contaminating activity —
so there is no single safe distance to print here. As a rough planning range,
even a few hundred feet of separation from an identified source is better
than none, and the general guidance behind agricultural buffer-strip
standards (commonly 20–100+ ft of vegetated buffer, depending on the pollutant
and slope) gives a sense of scale, but **verify against local health
department or extension guidance** rather than treating any number here as
authoritative.

### Moving water over still water

Prefer a flowing stream or river over a pond or lake when both are available.
Moving water is more oxygenated, dilutes contaminants faster, and does not
support the warm, still conditions that harmful algal blooms need — running
water essentially does not bloom the way a pond does. Still water also
accumulates whatever settles or floats: sediment, algae, and surface film all
concentrate in low-flow and stagnant areas. Within a stream, prefer a
faster-moving, well-mixed section over a stagnant eddy or backwater.

### Depth of draw

Draw from **mid-depth**, not the surface and not the bottom:

- The **surface film** is where floating scum, pollen, insects, and — during
  a bloom — cyanobacteria and their toxins concentrate.
- The **bottom** stirs up sediment, is closer to anaerobic decomposition on
  the substrate, and disturbs settled material back into suspension.
- A cup, pot, or intake hose held a few inches to a foot below the surface,
  away from the bank and any visible film, gives the cleanest draw available
  without equipment.

### Before you collect: a quick visual check

- Look upstream/upslope for the risk factors listed above.
- Check for any of the harmful-algal-bloom warning signs; if present, do not
  collect from that source at all, regardless of what treatment is planned.
- Prefer clear, moving, cool water over warm, still, discolored water.
- Avoid drawing near a bank with obvious animal traffic (game trails, beaver
  activity, livestock access) or immediately below a culvert or storm drain
  outfall.

## Next step: treatment

Site selection lowers risk; it does not eliminate it. Every method of
untreated surface water still needs treatment before drinking — see
[Water Purification](20-water-purification.md) for boiling, chemical
disinfection, filtration ratings, solar disinfection, and distillation, along
with dosing and the limitations of each method. The one exception is the
cyanotoxin case above: if a source shows bloom signs, treat that as a reason
to choose a different source, not a harder treatment step.
