# Well Water and Hand Pumps

## Summary

A well is the most self-sufficient water source a fixed dwelling can have — no
roof runoff to wait for, no municipal supply that can fail upstream. But
"private well" covers three very different structures with different depths,
different contamination risks, and different options for pumping water when
the grid is down. This page covers well types, how to size up a well you
already have (rather than how to site or drill a new one, which is a licensed
contractor's job), hand-pump options for grid-down operation, shock
chlorination of a bacterially contaminated well, and the water-quality testing
every private well owner should be doing on a schedule regardless of any
emergency.

**Regional assumptions (northeastern US / New Hampshire):** shallow bedrock
under a thin mantle of glacial till and silt makes drilled bedrock (artesian)
wells the dominant type here — dug and driven wells are the exception, not the
rule, and are mostly older construction. Frost depth is significant enough
that every buried water line matters: New Hampshire wells run the supply line
from the casing to the house through a **pitless adapter**, conventionally
buried **4–5 feet** for frost protection. Exact frost depth is set town by
town and by soil — verify locally with a well contractor or the building
department rather than treating any single number as universal. Groundwater
in this region also carries naturally occurring **arsenic and radon** from
granite bedrock at rates well above the national average, which shapes the
testing section below.

!!! warning "Not a substitute for a lab or a licensed well contractor"
    This page is for situations where professional water testing, a licensed
    well/pump contractor, or municipal water is not available — assessing what
    you have, keeping it usable, and treating a known bacterial problem in the
    field. It is not a substitute for periodic laboratory testing, which is
    the only way to detect odorless, invisible contaminants like arsenic and
    radon, and it is not a substitute for a licensed well contractor when a
    well has a structural problem. Get the well tested and inspected
    professionally as soon as that's possible.

## Well types

| Type | Typical depth | Construction | Contamination vulnerability |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dug / bored** | 10–30 ft | Hand- or backhoe-excavated, large diameter (often 3+ ft), lined with stone, brick, tile, or concrete rings; not continuously cased | **Highest.** Shallow, wide-mouthed, and drawing from the water table nearest the surface |
| **Driven / sand-point** | 30–50 ft | A hardened well point and screen driven into unconsolidated sand or gravel; continuously cased but shallow | **Moderate.** Cased, but still draws from a near-surface aquifer easily reached by surface contamination |
| **Drilled (bedrock / artesian)** | Commonly 200–500 ft in NH bedrock (median ≈400 ft), some over 1,000 ft | Rotary- or percussion-drilled through overburden into bedrock; steel or heavy plastic casing seated at least 10 ft into competent rock, typically 6 in diameter | **Lowest for bacteria** (long percolation path through rock), but the type most associated with naturally occurring arsenic, radon, uranium, and manganese from the bedrock itself |

Dug and driven wells are essentially impractical to newly construct in most of
New Hampshire — shallow bedrock and glacial till full of cobbles and boulders
favor drilling, which is why drilled bedrock wells are the regional norm and
older dug/driven wells you may inherit with a property are worth assessing
with extra caution. Driven (sand-point) wells also need enough clean,
boulder-free unconsolidated material to drive into, which is common in the
sandy river valleys and coastal plain but not in typical NH upland till.

## Assessing an existing well

Before relying on a well, or before adding any grid-down backup to it, find
out what you actually have:

- **Get the well completion report.** New Hampshire requires drillers to file
  a report with NHDES's Water Well Program; look up the property's well record
  (or ask the previous owner) for depth, casing length, static water level,
  and tested yield. Without a record, a well contractor can usually determine
  depth and water level with a sounding tape or sonic meter.
- **Identify the pump type.** Bedrock wells in this region are almost always
  fitted with a **submersible pump** hanging on the drop pipe near the bottom
  of the casing — it requires grid or generator power and cannot be adapted
  into a hand pump without well contractor work. A **jet pump** sitting above
  ground (basement or well house) pulling water by suction is only workable
  where the static and pumping water levels are shallow; if present, it's a
  sign the well is shallow enough that a hand pump backup is more plausible.
- **Know the static water level, not just the casing depth.** A 400 ft
  bedrock well with water sitting 40 ft down behaves, for pumping purposes,
  like a 40 ft well — casing depth alone tells you nothing about what lift a
  backup pump needs to handle. This number is on the well completion report or
  measurable with a weighted tape lowered down a sounding tube if the wellhead
  has one.
- **Check the wellhead itself.** A tight-fitting vented cap with intact,
  sealed electrical conduit, casing standing well above grade, and no signs of
  rodent or insect intrusion are all part of keeping surface contamination out
  — and all worth a visual check even if you never expect to touch the well
  otherwise.
- **Never enter a well pit.** Older properties sometimes have a buried
  concrete pit around the wellhead from before pitless adapters were standard.
  Gases can accumulate in these pits to hazardous or lethal concentrations —
  treat them like a confined space and do not go down into one.
- **Adding a hand-pump backup to an existing well is a retrofit, not an
  accessory purchase.** A submersible-pump well typically has only one drop
  pipe sized for that pump; fitting a separate hand pump usually means adding
  a dedicated sleeve/pipe into the well or a specific dual-pump wellhead
  adapter, which is casing and wellhead work best planned with, or done by, a
  licensed well contractor — not something to improvise on an emergency
  timeline.

## Hand pumps for a grid-down well

All manual well pumps fall into two families, split by how they get water to
the surface — and the split matters because it sets a hard physical limit on
what each type can do.

### Pitcher pumps and other shallow-well (suction) pumps

A pitcher pump, and any shallow-well hand pump, works by suction: the piston
above ground creates a partial vacuum that atmospheric pressure pushes water
up into. That mechanism has a ceiling. At sea level, atmospheric pressure can
theoretically support a column of water about **34 ft** high; friction losses,
pump inefficiency, and any loss of seal cut the *practical* lift to around
**25 ft** from the pump to the water surface, and less at altitude. No amount
of pumping harder gets past this — above that depth to water, the water
column separates from the piston and the pump moves nothing.

This makes suction pumps a fit for dug wells, driven wells, and shallow
sections of a water table — not for a typical NH bedrock well, where the
static water level is very often deeper than 25 ft even when the casing is
hundreds of feet long. A common resilience approach where the main well is
deep is to drive a **separate shallow sand-point well**, sized and sited
specifically to reach a shallow water table for hand-pump backup, rather than
count on reaching the main well's water column by suction.

### Deep-well (cylinder/piston) hand pumps

A deep-well hand pump does not rely on suction at all: the actual pump
cylinder and piston sit down in the well, at or below the water level, and a
rod running up to a lever or flywheel at the surface drives it. Because the
piston is already below the water, atmospheric pressure isn't doing the
lifting — the mechanical linkage is — so there's no 25–34 ft ceiling. The real
limit becomes the amount of physical work a person can sustainably put into
lifting that much water that far; a commonly cited practical ceiling for
sustained manual effort is on the order of **30 m (about 100 ft)**. Products
marketed for considerably greater depths exist and rely on gearing or a
flywheel for mechanical advantage, but the work per stroke rises accordingly —
treat manufacturer depth ratings as a starting point to verify against your
well's actual static and pumping water levels (not casing length) and against
how much sustained effort you're actually willing to put into a bucket of
water, not as a guarantee.

Installing a deep-well hand pump generally means a dedicated pipe down the
well alongside (or in place of) the electric pump's drop pipe, and a wellhead
fitting to match — again, retrofit work for a well contractor, not a weekend
DIY swap, especially through a pitless adapter buried 4–5 ft down.

### Well buckets

The oldest method: a bucket on a rope, lowered and drawn up by hand. It only
works on wide-mouthed dug or bored wells with an open enough casing to pass a
bucket — not on a 6 in drilled bedrock well casing. It's a genuine emergency
fallback for a dug well with no pump at all, but every dip introduces the
bucket, rope, and hands to the well water, so treat anything drawn this way as
needing disinfection before drinking, and don't rely on it as a routine
method.

## Shock chlorination (disinfecting a contaminated well)

Shock chlorination is the standard response to total coliform or *E. coli*
bacteria detected in a well, to recent work done on the well or pump, or to
certain non-coliform bacterial odors. It is not a fix for a well with an
ongoing structural problem — a cracked casing, a bad seal, or a shallow rock
fracture — which will simply become recontaminated. If a well needs shocking
more than once a year, that's a sign to call a well contractor rather than
repeat the treatment.

!!! warning "If E. coli has been detected, don't wait on this to drink safely"
    If a lab result shows *E. coli*, do not drink the water until it has been
    **boiled at a rolling boil for at least 1 minute**, or use an alternate
    source — do this before, during, and after the shock chlorination process,
    which takes days to complete. See [Water Purification](20-water-purification.md).

### Materials

- Plain **unscented** household bleach (sodium hypochlorite, commonly
  5.25–8.25%) or NSF-60 calcium hypochlorite pellets (typically ~65–70%
  available chlorine) — nothing with added fragrance, algaecide, or
  "concentrated"/thickened formulas
- Two 5-gallon buckets for mixing
- Rubber gloves and eye protection
- A garden hose long enough to reach from an outside spigot back to the well
- Chlorine test strips (not required, but the only way to confirm dose and,
  later, confirm the well is clear before sampling)
- An alternate water source, because the system is unusable for drinking,
  cooking, or bathing for at least 24 hours and should not be sampled for
  another week after that

### Procedure

1. **Inspect and fix pathways first.** Check the well cap, casing height (at
   least 8 in above grade for a residential well), electrical conduit seal,
   and casing for cracks or rodent/insect entry. Chlorinating a well with an
   open pathway only masks the problem temporarily.
2. **Arrange an alternate water supply** before starting — the house water
   will be unusable for at least a day, and unpleasant-tasting for longer.
3. **Flush sediment.** Mud, rust, and biofilm shield bacteria from chlorine.
   For a dug well, scrub the walls and flush until clear (treat a dug well as
   a confined space — do not enter it). For a drilled well, pump to waste
   through an outside faucet until the water runs clear.
4. **Bypass any treatment equipment** (filters, softeners, UV) per the
   manufacturer's instructions — chlorine at shock concentration can damage
   resins, membranes, and gaskets.
5. **Calculate the well's water volume**, then the chlorine needed to reach
   target concentration, from the tables below (or your own state health
   department's chart — see the note on disagreement below). If the depth to
   water is unknown for a NH bedrock well, NHDES's own guidance says to
   presume 400 ft, its statewide median.
6. **Shut off power to the pump**, remove the well cap, and add the chlorine
   solution or pellets to the well.
7. **Recirculate.** Connect the hose to a spigot ahead of any treatment
   equipment, restore power, and run the hose water back down the well for
   roughly 30 minutes, wetting the casing walls, until a strong chlorine odor
   is present. If it isn't after 30 minutes, add more chlorine.
8. **Run every fixture** in the house — hot and cold, inside and out —
   one at a time until chlorine odor is detected at each, then shut it off.
9. **Let it stand.** Minimum 12 hours, 24 preferred, with the system otherwise
   unused (moderate toilet flushing is fine). Don't leave chlorine in the
   system longer than about 24 hours — it's corrosive to pump wiring and
   fittings.
10. **Flush it out** through an outside faucet to a dry area or drainage
    swale — **never** directly into a septic leach field, a stream, a pond, a
    wetland, or onto garden plants, which heavily chlorinated water will
    injure. This can take repeated flushing sessions over several days,
    especially on a low-yield well.
11. **Wait at least a week**, confirm no chlorine residual with a test strip,
    then submit a sample for total coliform/*E. coli* to a state-certified
    lab. A lab will typically reject a sample that still smells of chlorine.
12. **If bacteria persist** after a second round of shock chlorination and a
    second failed test, that's a strong sign of a structural problem in the
    well rather than something more disinfectant will fix — call a licensed
    well contractor.

### Dosage: how much chlorine, and what the sources disagree on

**Sources genuinely differ here, and not just in units.** New Hampshire's own
health department targets a lower chlorine concentration for routine
bacterial shock chlorination than CDC's general emergency-disinfection
guidance does — likely because CDC's page is written for post-flood/
gross-contamination scenarios and NHDES's is written for routine coliform
response. Use your own state or local health department's chart when you have
one; the NHDES approach below is what applies in this region.

**Well water volume**, if you know the depth to water and the casing
diameter, follows a fixed relationship (independent of state):

| Casing diameter | Gallons per foot of water depth |
|---|---|
| 6 in (typical NH bedrock well) | ≈1.5 |
| 8 in | ≈2.6 |
| 36 in (dug well) | ≈53 |
| 48 in (dug well) | ≈94 |

Add roughly 50 gallons for the piping and pressure tank on a typical private
system.

**NHDES's chlorine dose**, targeting an EPA-recommended initial concentration
of 10–50 mg/L (ppm) held for 12 hours (up to 50 mg/L if contamination has been
persistent; halve the amount shown for a 5 ppm dose):

| Water volume | Bleach (~8.25%) for 10 ppm | Bleach (~8.25%) for 50 ppm |
|---|---|---|
| 100 gal | 1/4 cup | 1 cup |
| 500 gal | 1 cup | 4 cups |
| 1,000 gal | 2 cups | 1/2 gallon |

Most private wells need well under a half-gallon of bleach total; a
high-yielding or larger-diameter well needs more.

**Maine CDC's bedrock-well chart** takes a simpler depth-based approach for a
typical 6 in bedrock well, using 6% bleach, with a shorter 3-hour "settle"
period in the well before flushing the casing and standing 12–24 hours:

| Well depth | Bleach (6%) |
|---|---|
| 50 ft | 1 qt |
| 100 ft | 1½ qt |
| 150 ft | 2 qt |
| 200 ft | 2½ qt |
| 250 ft | 3 qt |
| 300 ft | 3½ qt |

These two state charts don't reduce to the same number for a given well —
they reflect different target concentrations and different assumptions about
where the chlorine needs to reach. Either produces a working shock
chlorination when the whole procedure (recirculation, full-house flush,
adequate contact time) is followed; what matters most is completing every
step, not hitting an exact ppm figure.

### Safety

- **Electrical shock risk.** Shut off power to the pump before removing the
  well cap and before restoring it, especially with a submersible pump.
- **Confined space.** Do not enter a dug well or a buried well pit to clean
  or inspect it — hazardous gases can accumulate in either.
- **Chlorine is hazardous.** Wear gloves and eye protection, work in
  ventilated conditions, and never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaning
  chemicals.
- **Corrosion.** Don't leave shock-strength chlorine in the system longer than
  about 24 hours; rinse chlorine off wiring and the pitless adapter fitting
  during recirculation, since it will otherwise degrade them over time.
- **Discharge location matters.** Heavily chlorinated flush water can kill
  a septic system's bacteria, harm plants, and is illegal to discharge
  directly to surface water in most places — route it to a dry area instead.
- **This is not a permanent fix for recurring contamination.** Repeated
  positive bacteria tests after correcting obvious pathways mean the well
  itself likely needs professional evaluation, and possibly UV disinfection
  or a new well.

## Water testing and contamination risks

Private wells are unregulated — nobody tests them but the owner. CDC and EPA
both recommend, at minimum, **testing annually for total coliform bacteria,
nitrate, pH, and total dissolved solids**, with additional parameters added
based on local conditions. New Hampshire's own guidance is more specific,
reflecting what's actually common in the state's bedrock geology:

| Test for | Frequency (NHDES) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Total coliform / *E. coli* | Annually | Indicator of fecal or surface-water intrusion; a positive result means treat as unsafe until resolved |
| Nitrate / nitrite | Annually | From septic systems, fertilizer, or manure; acute risk to infants (methemoglobinemia, "blue baby syndrome") |
| Arsenic | Every 3–5 years | Naturally occurring in NH bedrock; **colorless, odorless, and tasteless** — undetectable without a lab |
| Radon | Every 3–5 years | Naturally occurring in NH bedrock; a gas that off-gasses from water into indoor air as well as being ingested |
| Uranium, lead, copper, manganese, iron, fluoride, sodium, chloride, hardness, pH | Every 3–5 years | Geology- and plumbing-dependent; informs whether treatment is needed |
| PFAS | Baseline, then per household budget/local land use | Elevated near current or former military, airport, or industrial sites in this region |

**Arsenic in New Hampshire is not a minor regional footnote.** Roughly one in
five bedrock wells statewide exceeds the federal drinking water limit of 10
parts per billion (ppb), and New Hampshire adopted its own stricter standard
of 5 ppb in 2019 (effective 2021) for public systems — a level that an
estimated three in ten private wells also exceed. Because arsenic has no
taste, odor, or color, testing is the only way to know. Wells even fairly
close together (NHDES cites roughly 50 ft) can differ sharply because of how
fractured the bedrock is.

**Radon** in well water is treated by NH health authorities as warranting
treatment at or above **10,000 pCi/L**, with treatment "may be advisable"
between 2,000 and 10,000 pCi/L depending on indoor air readings, and simple
retesting every 3–5 years below 4,000 pCi/L. As a rule of thumb, 10,000 pCi/L
of radon in water raises indoor air radon by roughly 1 pCi/L as it off-gasses
from taps, showers, and toilets.

**Bacteria is different from the rest of this list** in one important way:
coliform bacteria are themselves mostly harmless, but their presence is used
as an indicator that a pathway exists for genuinely harmful organisms
(pathogenic bacteria, viruses, parasites) to have entered the well the same
way. A positive coliform test means treat the water as unsafe and find the
pathway — not necessarily that the specific organisms found are dangerous.

### Where contamination comes from

Common sources near a private well, and why setback distances exist:

| Source | New Hampshire setback |
|---|---|
| Septic tank | 75 ft (can be 50 ft with SDR 26 pipe and a sealed plastic tank) |
| Leach field / effluent disposal area | 75 ft |
| Property boundary | 75 ft |
| Livestock pen | 75 ft (100 ft for dug wells) |
| Underground fuel storage tank | 250 ft |
| Surface water / wetland / swamp | 50 ft |
| State highway right-of-way | 50 ft |

These are New Hampshire's Water Well Board figures for a residential well and
septic system — **setback rules are jurisdiction-dependent**; verify the
actual numbers with your state or local health/environmental department
rather than assuming these apply elsewhere. They're included here mainly to
explain *why* a well positioned close to a septic system, road, or fuel tank
carries elevated risk even before a test confirms anything, and as a sanity
check when assessing a well you didn't site yourself.
