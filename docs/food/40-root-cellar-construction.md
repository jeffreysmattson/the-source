# Root Cellar Construction

## Summary

A root cellar is a storage space that uses the earth's thermal mass instead of
mechanical refrigeration to hold produce at cold, humid, but above-freezing
conditions through the winter. It works because soil a few feet down barely
tracks the swing between a July heat wave and a January cold snap — it settles
near the region's mean annual temperature and stays there. Build the storage
zone below the local frost line and you get a space that hovers in the high
30s to low 40s Fahrenheit for months with no power input at all.

That single fact — cold ground is stable, and stability is the entire point —
drives every other design decision on this page: how deep to dig, how to
ventilate without losing the stability you just bought, and how to keep
groundwater out of a hole you deliberately put below grade.

This page assumes the **northeastern United States, New Hampshire in
particular**: a deep frost line, a spring water table that sits much higher
than it looks in a summer test hole, and heavy snow load on anything built
partly above grade. [Site Preparation](../building/site-prep/10-site-preparation.md)
covers frost depth, grading, and subsurface investigation in more detail —
read it first if you haven't sited a structure in this climate before. The
short version repeated here because it matters this much: **frost depth is
set town by town, not by a chart on the internet.** Get the number from your
local building department before you decide how deep to dig.

## Before you begin

- **Local frost depth**, from the building department, in writing. This is
  the number that sets how deep the cold-storage zone has to sit.
- **Seasonal high water table.** A site that looks dry in August can flood a
  cellar dug below spring grade. Dig a test hole, or watch an existing
  basement or foundation hole through spring, before committing to a
  location.
- **A rough inventory of what you intend to store.** Root vegetables, apples,
  and onions want different conditions (see below); knowing the mix affects
  whether one room is enough or whether you want two zones.
- **Building/zoning check** if you're adding a standalone buried structure
  rather than partitioning existing basement space — some jurisdictions treat
  a buried structure like any other outbuilding.
- Basic tools for whichever approach you pick: framing tools and rigid foam
  for a basement-corner conversion; an excavator or a lot of hand digging for
  a buried structure.

## Why a root cellar works

Deep soil temperature tracks the site's mean annual air temperature, damped
and lagged by depth — the deeper you go, the less it moves and the further
behind the surface season it runs. That's why a well-sited root cellar
reads cold in summer and merely cool, never freezing, in winter, without any
equipment. Below the frost line, the ground doesn't freeze at all in a normal
winter, which is the whole reason foundations and cold storage are built
below it in the first place.

Most root vegetables want it **cold and wet**: roughly 32–40°F with 90–95%
relative humidity. That combination slows respiration and sprouting to a
crawl while the high humidity keeps the vegetables from wilting and going
limp. But "a root cellar" is really a compromise between several different
target conditions, not one number:

| Crop group | Temperature | Humidity | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beets, carrots, parsnips, turnips, celery, winter radish, leeks, Jerusalem artichokes | 32–40°F | 90–95% | The classic "cold and moist" group; most sources agree closely here |
| Potatoes | 38–40°F | 85–95% | Slightly warmer than other roots; sources give humidity anywhere from 85–95% |
| Cabbage, apples | 32–40°F | 80–90% | Cold, but somewhat less humid than root vegetables |
| Onions, garlic | 32–50°F | 55–70% | Wants **dry** air — high humidity here causes rot, not shelf life |
| Winter squash, pumpkins, sweet potatoes | 50–60°F | 50–70% | Wants it noticeably warmer and drier than everything above |

Sources disagree on the exact numbers in each cell — University of Alaska
Fairbanks Extension, University of Missouri Extension, and UConn Extension
each give slightly different humidity bands for the same crop — but they
agree on the shape of the problem: **root vegetables want cold and wet, alliums
want cold and dry, and squash/sweet potatoes want it warm.** A single cellar
at one temperature and humidity is a compromise for whichever crops don't
match it exactly. Two zones — a cold, humid main room and a smaller cool, dry
shelf or box for onions and garlic near the door where it's slightly warmer —
solves this better than one room does. If you only have one room, side with
the root vegetables (cold and humid) and give onions, garlic, and winter
squash a separate spot elsewhere in the house instead of forcing them into
the same air.

!!! warning "Keep apples and pears away from vegetables"
    Ripening apples and pears give off ethylene gas, which accelerates
    ripening and sprouting in whatever is stored near them — potatoes
    sprouting early and vegetables going soft are the classic symptom.
    Store fruit in a closed container (a lidded tub or bin) even within the
    same room, or better, in a separate space entirely.

## Core design principle: depth plus controlled ventilation

Two things have to be true at once, and they pull in opposite directions:

1. **The storage zone's floor and most of its walls need to sit below the
   local frost line**, so the surrounding earth stays at a stable, above-
   freezing temperature all winter regardless of what the air is doing.
2. **The space still needs ventilation**, or it will run too warm, too damp,
   and too high in respiration gases (carbon dioxide and ethylene given off
   by the stored produce itself) for good keeping. A sealed hole in the
   ground is not a root cellar — it's a mold incubator.

The standard way to reconcile these is a **two-pipe passive vent system**
using the stack effect: a low intake pipe that draws in cold outside air
near the floor, and a high exhaust pipe that lets warmed, moisture- and gas-
laden air escape near the ceiling. Cold air is denser, so it sinks in and
pushes the lighter, warmer air up and out — no fan required. The bigger the
height difference between the two openings and the bigger the temperature
difference between outside air and cellar air, the stronger the draw.

Practical points on sizing and running these pipes, drawn from several build
guides (there is no single official engineering standard for a home root
cellar, so treat these as workable starting points, not code):

- **3–4 inch pipe diameter** is the commonly cited size for a small room
  (roughly up to a 6×8 ft cellar); step up to 6 inches, or add a second pair,
  for a larger room.
- **Same diameter on both pipes.** Intake near the floor, exhaust near the
  ceiling, and space the two openings as far apart horizontally as the room
  allows so air actually crosses the space instead of short-circuiting.
- **Screen every outside opening** against rodents and insects — an
  unscreened vent pipe is an invitation.
- **Add a damper or cap you can close** on the intake. In a genuine cold
  snap, outside air can be cold enough to freeze produce near the vent;
  close it down until temperatures moderate. Watch a thermometer, not a
  calendar.
- In spring and fall, when outside air runs warmer than the cellar, close the
  vents entirely rather than warming the space you spent all winter keeping
  cold.

!!! tip "A thermometer and hygrometer are not optional"
    Every source agrees on this even where they disagree on numbers: put a
    min/max thermometer and a hygrometer in the cellar and check them
    regularly, especially through the first winter. The vents are the only
    control you have, and you can't run them correctly by feel.

## Common build approaches

None of these is universally "correct" — the right one depends on what
you're starting with (an existing basement or bare ground), your budget, and
how much you plan to store.

### 1. Partition a basement corner

The lowest-effort option if you already have a full basement, and the one
most home builds in this region use. Wall off a corner — ideally the
coldest one, away from the furnace and ductwork, where the foundation wall
meets undisturbed soil rather than a walkout or window.

- **Insulate the ceiling and the interior partition walls** that separate
  the cold room from the rest of the heated basement — this keeps house
  heat from leaking in. Rigid extruded polystyrene is commonly recommended
  over fiberglass batts because it tolerates damp conditions without
  absorbing moisture.
- **Leave the exterior foundation walls and the floor uninsulated.** That's
  the mechanism — deep-ground cold needs to conduct in through those
  surfaces, not stop at an insulated boundary.
- **Use an exterior-grade insulated door** to seal the room off from heated
  basement air. This one detail is frequently cited as the difference
  between a cold room that actually holds near-freezing temperatures and
  one that never gets much below ordinary basement temperature (55–65°F,
  well above target).
- **Vent to outside, not into the rest of the basement.** This approach
  only reaches root-cellar temperatures if the vent pipes draw genuinely
  cold outdoor air rather than recirculating heated house air.

Framing the partition itself is ordinary stud-wall construction — see
[Framing and Structural Basics](../building/structure/30-framing.md); this
page covers only what's specific to a cold room.

### 2. Standalone buried or bermed structure

A structure built separately from the house, either fully buried (entered by
a stairway or hatch) or bermed against a slope (entered at grade on the
downhill side, covered with earth on the other three sides and the roof).
Closer to the traditional root cellar, and doesn't depend on having a
basement at all.

- Aim for **at least a couple of feet of earth cover** over the roof, and
  more if you can — more surrounding soil mass means the interior sits
  closer to true deep-ground temperature and swings less with the weather
  above.
- Concrete block, poured concrete, or a properly braced timber structure are
  the common wall materials; whichever you choose, it has to carry the
  weight of the soil and any snow load on top, which is a real structural
  load, not a finishing detail. See
  [Masonry, Concrete, and Mortar Mixes](../building/structure/50-masonry-concrete.md)
  and [Foundations](../building/structure/20-foundations.md) — a buried
  root cellar faces the same soil and water loads a foundation does.
- The entry door and any above-grade portion still need to be insulated and
  sealed like a basement-corner door.

### 3. Low-cost buried containers: chest freezers and culvert pipe

Two frequently used shortcuts substitute a manufactured container for built
walls:

- **A buried chest freezer or refrigerator**, door up or on its side, set
  into a dug pit with several inches of gravel underneath for drainage,
  backfilled around the sides, and left with a few inches of the lid above
  final grade. Cut intake and exhaust holes for vent pipe as described
  above — an airtight buried box with no ventilation will sweat, mold, and
  rot its contents as fast as any other sealed hole.
- **A buried culvert pipe** (concrete or corrugated steel), laid
  horizontally in a trench with a couple of feet of soil cover, capped at
  both ends with insulated wooden bulkheads that include a door and the
  vent pipes. Some builds combine a larger horizontal pipe with a smaller
  vertical pipe as a ladder-access entrance.

Both are inexpensive and fast relative to excavating and building walls, but
both concentrate the same failure mode: a metal container buried in the
ground is exactly the shape of a bathtub, and if drainage around it is
wrong, it fills with water instead of holding produce. Treat drainage as
non-negotiable for either build, not an afterthought.

### 4. Full excavated masonry/concrete cellar

The traditional, most durable option: excavate to the required depth, build
block or poured-concrete walls with a proper footing, and backfill. This is
substantially more work and cost than the other options but gives the most
control over dimensions, drainage detailing, and long-term durability. It's
the right call when you're building new construction anyway (a root cellar
under a section of a new house, sharing the excavation) rather than
retrofitting.

## Drainage

A root cellar is, structurally, a hole in the ground that you need to stay
dry — the same problem a foundation faces, and it fails for the same
reasons. This is not optional detailing; a flooded root cellar is a ruined
one.

- **Site it on high ground within the available area**, and away from any
  low spot where surface water or snowmelt collects.
- **Grade the surrounding ground to fall away from the structure**, the same
  minimum slope discussed in
  [Site Preparation](../building/site-prep/10-site-preparation.md#6-establish-grade-and-drainage)
  — roughly 6 inches of fall in the first 10 feet, more if you can get it.
- **Check the site through spring**, not just in a dry summer test hole. The
  seasonal high water table in this region sits much higher than the water
  table you'll find in August, and a cellar floor that's dry in July can
  flood every April if it's below the spring water table.
- **Put a layer of coarse gravel under the floor** of a buried structure or
  container, both for drainage and to break capillary wicking of
  groundwater up into the space.
- **Plan a footing drain or daylighted outlet** exactly as you would for a
  basement, if the structure has real walls and a footing — see Site
  Preparation's drainage section for the mechanics. A dug pit or buried
  container without a gravity outlet needs to sit high enough, and on
  soil permeable enough, that it doesn't need a sump — if it doesn't, that's
  a sign the site is wrong for this use.

## Materials and layout

- **Avoid materials that off-gas or rot in a cold, damp, enclosed space.**
  Treated lumber intended for ground contact, composite decking, or masonry
  are reasonable choices for anything touching soil or moisture; ordinary
  softwood framing lumber in constant contact with damp earth will rot over
  a few seasons. Avoid strong-smelling materials (fresh treated lumber,
  certain sealants) near open produce, since root vegetables pick up odors
  readily.
- **Shelving should allow air to move**, not just hold weight: open wire
  shelving or slatted wood shelving with gaps, rather than solid sheet
  material, so air can circulate top to bottom the way the ventilation
  system is trying to move it. Leave a gap between shelving and walls too.
- **Bins for root vegetables** are traditionally layered with damp sand,
  leaves, or wood shavings between layers of produce — this cushions the
  vegetables, buffers humidity, and reduces rot spreading from one spoiling
  item to its neighbors. Perforate or leave gaps in solid bins so they don't
  trap standing moisture.
- **Keep produce off the floor** on pallets, boards, or shelving — floor
  temperature swings least but floor moisture is highest, especially near a
  vent intake in a cold snap.
- **Separate onions/garlic and any fruit from the main root-vegetable
  storage**, per the temperature/humidity table and the ethylene warning
  above — a shelf near the door, or a lidded bin, is enough separation in a
  single-room cellar.

## Rough sizing

There's no single figure that fits every household — the right size depends
on family size, how much you actually grow or buy in bulk, and which crops
you're storing (bulky winter squash takes more volume per meal than compact
root vegetables). One frequently cited rule of thumb for a small household
storing a home garden's worth of crops is a room in the rough neighborhood
of **4×6 to 8×10 feet**, but treat that as a starting point for planning
shelf and bin space, not a design target. It's more useful to size the room
around measured bushels of what you actually intend to store than to build
to a number from an article.
