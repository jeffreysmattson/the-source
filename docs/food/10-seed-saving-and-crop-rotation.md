# Seed Saving and Crop Rotation

## Summary

Seed saving and crop rotation are the two habits that make a garden
self-renewing instead of dependent on a mail-order catalog and a functioning
supply chain every spring. Seed saving closes the loop: grown food produces
next year's seed, indefinitely, as long as you save from the right kind of
plant and keep varieties from crossing into mush. Crop rotation is the
companion habit that keeps the soil itself productive year after year by
starving out the diseases and pests that build up when the same family grows
in the same ground repeatedly.

Neither is difficult, but both reward planning a season ahead. This page
assumes a home garden in the **northeastern United States, New Hampshire in
particular** — a frost-free season that commonly runs only from late May to
mid-September, which matters for seed saving because some crops (long-season
winter squash, dry corn) need the full season just to mature seed, not just a
harvestable fruit.

!!! note "Sources disagree on the numbers below"
    Isolation distances and seed-viability years are not physical constants —
    they depend on local insect pressure, wind, variety, and how much genetic
    drift you're willing to tolerate. Where the sources below give different
    numbers, this page says so rather than picking one and presenting it as
    settled.

## Why save seed at all

- **Supply chain independence.** Seed companies can sell out, discontinue a
  variety, or simply be unreachable when roads, power, or mail service are
  disrupted. A jar of your own seed is not subject to any of that.
- **Local adaptation.** Seed saved year after year from plants that performed
  well in *your* soil and *your* microclimate slowly adapts to it — earlier
  maturity, better cold tolerance, whatever your conditions select for. A
  catalog packet never does this.
- **The seed-to-seed cycle is the actual skill.** Growing a plant to
  harvestable food is only half the loop. Growing it all the way to mature,
  viable, stored seed — and successfully germinating that seed the following
  year — is the part that makes a garden resilient rather than merely
  productive in a good year.
- **Cost and resilience compound.** A few years of successful seed saving
  across a handful of staple crops (beans, tomatoes, peas, lettuce) removes an
  entire recurring expense and an entire point of dependency at once.

## Open-pollinated, hybrid (F1), and heirloom

These three terms describe *how a variety was bred*, not how good it is, and
only one of them is generally worth saving seed from.

| Term | What it means | Worth saving seed from? |
|---|---|---|
| **Open-pollinated (OP)** | Pollinated naturally (self- or insect-pollinated) within a genetically stable, uniform population. Offspring "come true" — they resemble the parent. | **Yes.** This is the whole category seed saving works on. |
| **Heirloom** | An open-pollinated variety, typically defined as pre-1950s or handed down through a family or community for generations. All heirlooms are open-pollinated; not all open-pollinated varieties are old enough to be called heirloom. | **Yes** — heirlooms are OP varieties, saved the same way. |
| **Hybrid (F1)** | The first-generation offspring of a deliberate cross between two distinct, inbred parent lines, bred for traits like disease resistance, uniformity, or yield (hybrid vigor). | **No, not usefully.** |

**Why hybrid seed isn't worth saving:** an F1 hybrid is heterozygous — it
carries two different versions of many genes, one from each inbred parent
line, and that combination is what produces the vigor and uniformity the
hybrid is bred for. When an F1 plant self-pollinates, its own offspring (the
F2 generation) re-shuffle those genes and mostly segregate back toward the
original, less desirable parent lines. The University of Missouri Extension's
integrated pest management program puts it plainly: saved hybrid seed
"tend[s] genetically to revert back to the parents" used to make the original
cross, losing the traits the hybrid was bred for. The seed will usually still
germinate and grow — it just won't resemble the parent plant, and results are
inconsistent from seed to seed. Seed packets and plant tags marked "F1" are
the ones to grow for eating, not for seed.

## Isolation distance and cross-pollination risk

Some crops pollinate themselves inside a single flower before it even opens
and are nearly impossible to cross accidentally. Others are pollinated by
insects or wind that will happily carry pollen between two varieties planted
in the same bed. Isolation distance — how far apart two varieties of the same
species need to be to stay true to type — is the seed-saving concept that
trips up beginners the most, and the recommended numbers vary a lot between
sources because insect pressure, wind, and acceptable drift all differ by
site.

| Crop | Pollination | Beginner difficulty | Typical isolation distance cited |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Beans** (common bean) | Self-pollinating; flower structure largely excludes insects | Easy | 10–20 ft is the commonly cited home-garden figure; some seed-saving guides recommend up to 100 ft if strict genetic purity matters, since bees do visit bean flowers occasionally |
| **Peas** | Self-pollinating | Easy | Same range as beans, 10–20 ft |
| **Tomatoes** | Mostly self-pollinating (modern varieties have the stigma enclosed inside the flower) | Easy–moderate | 10–50 ft for standard varieties; potato-leaved and some heirloom varieties have an exposed stigma that insects can reach, and guides recommend caging or hand-pollinating those, or increasing distance |
| **Squash / cucurbits** (summer & winter squash, pumpkins, melons, cucumbers) | Insect-pollinated (bees); each species crosses freely within itself | Hard | Commercial seed-production guides cite roughly **1/2 mile** between varieties of the same species; this is impractical for most home lots, so home growers typically hand-pollinate and tape or bag flowers instead of relying on distance |
| **Corn** | Wind-pollinated, pollen travels far | Hard | A quarter mile is a frequently cited minimum, but multiple sources note pollen can travel much farther on wind, so distance alone is considered unreliable; hand-pollination (bagging tassels and ear shoots) or time isolation (staggering planting dates so tasseling doesn't overlap) is preferred. Corn also needs a **minimum population** (rough guidance: 100–200+ plants) to avoid inbreeding depression, which distance figures alone don't address |
| **Brassicas** (broccoli, cabbage, kale, Brussels sprouts, collards, kohlrabi, cauliflower, mustard) | Insect-pollinated; most are the same species (*Brassica oleracea*) and cross with each other readily | Hard | Roughly **1/2 mile** is commonly cited for seed-purity production; realistically, a home grower lets only one *Brassica oleracea* variety go to flower per season, or isolates flowering heads in mesh cages |

A few things worth understanding beyond the table:

- **Self-pollinating crops are genuinely easier**, not just conventionally
  recommended for beginners — the flower structure itself does most of the
  isolation work. Beans, peas, and (mostly) tomatoes are the sensible place to
  start.
- **eOrganic's organic seed-production guidance warns that older isolation
  charts understate the risk**: "much of the literature on isolation
  distances states that a 'break' of a few feet between crops of the same
  species will insure adequate isolation. This information is misleading...
  increased insect activity often leads to increased crossing rates, even in
  self-pollinated crops." Treat the smaller numbers above as a floor, not a
  guarantee.
- **Only one variety flowering at a time** is the practical home-garden
  substitute for a half-mile of isolation on squash and brassicas: grow one
  variety of a given cucurbit or *B. oleracea* species per year if saving
  seed from it, or hand-pollinate and bag/tape the flowers.
- Crossing between *different* species (a zucchini and a butternut squash,
  different cucurbit species) is not a concern; crossing within the *same*
  species is the real risk.

## Wet-seed vs. dry-seed processing

Seeds are processed differently depending on whether they're embedded in wet
fruit flesh or contained in a dry pod or head.

### Wet processing (tomatoes, squash, cucumbers, melons)

1. Let the fruit ripen fully — past the point you'd eat it, for tomatoes.
2. Scoop the seeds and surrounding pulp into a jar with a little water.
3. **Ferment** at room temperature (roughly 70–85°F) for **2–4 days**,
   stirring once or twice a day. A layer of white or grey mold/froth on top is
   normal and expected — it signals the fermentation is breaking down the
   germination-inhibiting gel coating the seeds, and it also helps kill some
   seed-borne pathogens.
4. Add more water, stir, and let the seeds settle: **viable seeds sink**,
   pulp and non-viable seeds float. Pour off the floaters and rinse the
   remainder in a strainer under running water until clean.
5. Spread seeds in a single layer on a screen, coffee filter, or plate (not
   paper towel, which they stick to) and dry out of direct sun with airflow.

Squash and cucumber seeds don't strictly require fermentation — many growers
simply scoop, rinse, and dry them — but fermenting tomato seed specifically is
close to universal advice because of the inhibitor gel.

### Dry processing (beans, peas, lettuce, brassicas, most grains)

1. Leave pods, seed heads, or seed stalks on the plant until they've dried and
   browned as far as the weather allows — for beans, until the pod is too
   dry and brittle to dent with a fingernail.
2. If frost or rain threatens before pods finish drying, pull whole plants and
   hang them somewhere dry and ventilated to finish.
3. **Thresh**: the simple method is to lay dried pods on a tarp or sheet and
   walk on them, or beat them in a bag, until the pods crack open, then
   winnow (pour in front of a fan, or toss in a light breeze) to separate
   seed from the lighter chaff.

## Drying, the moisture test, and storage

Seed that isn't dry enough before it's sealed away will mold or rot in
storage — this is the single most common seed-saving failure, more common
than pollination mistakes.

- **The snap test:** a properly dried seed should **snap or shatter** when
  bent or crushed with a fingernail, not bend or feel spongy. A bean that
  dents under fingernail pressure isn't dry enough yet. This is the standard
  field check when you have no other way to measure moisture content.
- Dry seed for roughly **1–2 weeks** in a dark, low-humidity spot with
  airflow before sealing it away. In a humid stretch of weather, use a fan;
  seed that takes too long to dry can mold even after fermentation and
  rinsing were done correctly.
- **Storage rule of thumb (Harrington's rule):** for a given seed, storage
  life roughly doubles for every 1% reduction in seed moisture content, and
  roughly doubles again for every 5°C (9°F) drop in storage temperature —
  down to practical limits. A commonly cited shorthand version of the same
  idea is that **the sum of storage temperature (°F) and percent relative
  humidity should stay under 100** (e.g., 40°F + 50% RH). Cool and dry both
  matter, and they partially substitute for each other.
- **Practical storage:** airtight glass jars or sealed containers, in a cool,
  dark location — a refrigerator (roughly 35–41°F) is close to ideal if you
  have the space; a cool basement or unheated closet is a workable substitute.
  A silica gel packet or a few tablespoons of dry powdered milk wrapped in
  cloth inside the jar absorbs incidental moisture. Let a chilled jar come
  back to room temperature before opening it, so humid room air doesn't
  condense on the cold seed.
- **Label everything** with crop, variety, and year saved — seed that looks
  identical to last year's is not a substitute for knowing which jar is
  older.

### Viability by crop (approximate, under good storage)

Sources vary meaningfully here — Johnny's Selected Seeds' published storage
guidelines and other seed-industry charts give ranges rather than single
numbers, and actual viability depends heavily on how well the seed was dried
and how it was stored:

| Crop | Typical viability (years) |
|---|---|
| Onion | 1–2 |
| Corn (sweet) | 1–3 |
| Peppers | 2–5 |
| Beans | 2–4 |
| Lettuce | 1–6 |
| Brassicas (cabbage, broccoli, Brussels sprouts) | 3–5 |
| Cucumber | 3–6 |
| Tomato | 3–7 |
| Squash / pumpkin | 4–6 |
| Watermelon | 4–5 |

Treat these as planning figures, not guarantees — and note UNH Extension's
plainer version of the same point: viability declines with every year in
storage regardless of crop, so a **germination test** (count how many of a
known sample, say 10–20 seeds, sprout on a damp paper towel) before planting
a full bed from old seed is cheap insurance against a wasted season.

## Crop rotation basics

Crop rotation means not planting the same plant family in the same bed year
after year. It matters for two independent reasons, and it's worth knowing
both:

1. **Soil-borne disease and pest cycles.** Many pathogens and pest larvae
   overwinter in soil and specifically attack one plant family. Leaving that
   family out of the bed for several years starves the pathogen population
   down before it returns. Some of these persist far longer than a simple
   rotation schedule suggests — clubroot, a serious brassica soil disease, can
   survive as resting spores for **more than 10 years**, and NC State
   Extension recommends rotating brassicas away from infested ground for a
   **minimum of 4–7 years**, while cautioning that even that is not a
   guarantee once a field is infested. A basic 3–4 year rotation is a
   maintenance practice that keeps ordinary pressure down; it is not a cure
   for an established, persistent soil pathogen.
2. **Nutrient depletion and fixation.** Different families draw down
   different nutrients at different rates ("heavy feeders" like tomatoes and
   brassicas vs. lighter feeders), and **legumes** (beans, peas, clover) host
   root-nodule bacteria that pull nitrogen from the air and fix it into the
   soil, leaving measurably more available nitrogen behind than they used.
   Rotating a heavy-feeding family into a bed the season after legumes grew
   there takes advantage of that.

### The rotation families

| Family | Common garden members |
|---|---|
| **Nightshades (Solanaceae)** | Tomato, pepper, potato, eggplant |
| **Brassicas (Brassicaceae)** | Broccoli, cabbage, kale, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, radish, turnip, arugula |
| **Legumes (Fabaceae)** | Beans, peas |
| **Cucurbits (Cucurbitaceae)** | Squash, cucumber, melon, pumpkin, zucchini |
| **Alliums (Amaryllidaceae)** | Onion, garlic, leek, shallot |
| **Umbellifers (Apiaceae)** | Carrot, celery, parsnip, dill |
| **Chenopods (Amaranthaceae)** | Beet, chard, spinach |

Cornell Cooperative Extension's home-gardener guidance frames the minimum
useful rotation as **3 years** — two full seasons of unrelated crops between
plantings of the same family in a given bed. Other extension and grower
sources recommend stretching that to **4 years** where space allows,
particularly for nightshades and brassicas, whose soil-borne diseases
(verticillium and early blight for nightshades; clubroot and black rot for
brassicas) are among the more persistent ones a home garden encounters.

### A simple 4-year home rotation

A workable default for a garden divided into four beds or sections:

| Year | Bed A | Bed B | Bed C | Bed D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Legumes | Nightshades | Brassicas | Cucurbits |
| 2 | Nightshades | Brassicas | Cucurbits | Legumes |
| 3 | Brassicas | Cucurbits | Legumes | Nightshades |
| 4 | Cucurbits | Legumes | Nightshades | Brassicas |

Each family returns to a given bed only once every four years. Alliums and
the umbellifer/chenopod crops are lighter feeders and less prone to family-
specific soil disease in most home gardens; they can be slotted into whichever
bed has room in a given year, or added as a fifth rotation slot if space
allows, rather than needing a dedicated position in the cycle. Put legumes
immediately **before** a heavy feeder (nightshades or brassicas) wherever
the schedule allows, to put the nitrogen they leave behind to use.

## Organizations and further guidance

- **Seed Savers Exchange** — nonprofit founded in 1975 to locate and preserve
  heirloom varieties; maintains one of the largest non-governmental seed
  banks in the US and publishes crop-by-crop seed-saving guides.
- **Organic Seed Alliance** — detailed, crop-specific seed production guides
  aimed at small-scale and organic growers, useful once basics are
  comfortable.
- **Land-grant cooperative extension services** — UNH, Cornell, and
  University of Maine Cooperative Extension all publish free, regionally
  relevant guidance on seed saving and rotation, and their county offices
  answer specific questions directly — worth knowing about even though this
  wiki assumes you may not have internet access to reach them when it
  matters most.
