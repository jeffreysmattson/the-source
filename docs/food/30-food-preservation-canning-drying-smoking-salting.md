# Canning, Drying, Smoking, and Salting

## Summary

These are four different tools, and each one is the *right* tool for only
some foods. Canning stops spoilage by sterilizing food and sealing it away
from air; drying stops it by removing the water microbes need; smoking adds
flavor and, done a specific way, some preservation; salting/curing pulls
water out and (for meat, when done right) adds a specific chemical that
plain salt does not provide. None of them is a general-purpose "make food
last" button, and picking the wrong one for a given food is not just a
quality problem — for canning and for meat curing/smoking, **it is a real
botulism risk**, not a theoretical one.

The single most dangerous mistake on this page is treating **low-acid**
food (vegetables, meat, poultry, seafood, most tomatoes) as if it were
**high-acid** food (fruit, pickles, jam) for canning purposes, or curing
meat with only table salt and no nitrite when a recipe assumes a proper
cure. *Clostridium botulinum*, the bacterium behind botulism, forms spores
that survive boiling, survive most home curing without nitrite, and grow
happily in the oxygen-free environment a sealed jar or the interior of a
cured meat provides — and the toxin it produces is lethal in small amounts
and cannot reliably be detected by smell, taste, or appearance.

!!! warning "This page assumes no professional help is available"
    Home preservation carries real, well-documented risk when a method is
    mismatched to a food or a step is skipped. The guidance below reflects
    USDA and National Center for Home Food Preservation (NCHFP) recommended
    practice. **When a recipe or family tradition conflicts with USDA/NCHFP
    guidance on canning acidity or meat curing, follow USDA/NCHFP.** These
    are exactly the areas where old habits and internet folklore diverge
    from what's actually safe.

## Quick reference

| Method | Best suited to | Typical shelf life | Key risk if done wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Water-bath canning** | High-acid foods only: fruit, jam/jelly, pickles, sauerkraut, salsa/tomatoes with added acid (pH ≤ 4.6) | 1+ year, sealed, cool & dark | Using it on a low-acid food — 212°F (100°C) does not kill *C. botulinum* spores |
| **Pressure canning** | Low-acid foods: vegetables, meats, poultry, seafood, most tomato products, mixed dishes | 1+ year, sealed, cool & dark | Underprocessing — wrong time/pressure/jar size for altitude leaves spores alive |
| **Drying / dehydrating** | Fruit, vegetables, herbs, jerky, some fish | Months (fruit/veg); jerky weeks–months if kept cool and dry | Uneven drying leaves wet pockets where mold or bacteria grow |
| **Hot smoking** | Meat, poultry, fish that reach a safe internal cook temperature | Days (refrigerated) unless also cured/dried | Treating "smoked" as "fully cooked" without checking internal temp |
| **Cold smoking** | Already-cured meat or fish, as a flavor step, at low temperature | Not a preservation method by itself | Anaerobic, 40–140°F range with no nitrite cure is a botulism incubator |
| **Salting / curing** | Meat, fish — dry salt-cure or brine | Weeks–months for a proper dry cure; indefinite only if also dried/smoked/refrigerated appropriately | Old "just salt" folk recipes for meat, without measured curing salt, don't reliably stop *C. botulinum* |

## Canning

Canning preserves food by heating it in a sealed jar hot enough, long
enough, to destroy spoilage organisms and (for the food types that need it)
*C. botulinum* spores, then relying on the vacuum seal to keep air and new
contamination out. **Which of the two canning methods is safe is decided
entirely by the acidity of the food — not by tradition, not by "it's always
been done this way."**

### The acid line: pH 4.6

The National Center for Home Food Preservation (NCHFP, University of
Georgia) and USDA divide canned foods into two groups by pH:

- **High-acid / acid foods — pH 4.6 or below.** Most fruits, jams, jellies,
  fruit butters, pickles, sauerkraut, and relishes. At this acidity, *C.
  botulinum* cannot grow, so a **boiling-water bath (212°F / 100°C at sea
  level)** is enough to destroy the yeasts, molds, and other spoilage
  organisms that *can* grow in acid food.
- **Low-acid foods — pH above 4.6.** All fresh vegetables, meats, poultry,
  seafood, and most mixed foods (a vegetable soup with a little tomato in
  it is still low-acid). Boiling water never gets hot enough to destroy *C.
  botulinum* spores in these foods. They **must** be processed in a
  **pressure canner**, which reaches roughly **240–250°F** at 10–15 psi —
  the temperature actually required to kill the spores.

**Tomatoes and figs are the classic trap.** Both are commonly treated as
"acid" foods, but modern low-acid tomato varieties, and figs, can test
above pH 4.6. NCHFP's guidance is to acidify them — a measured amount of
bottled lemon juice, citric acid, or vinegar per jar — before water-bath
canning, and to not rely on "tomatoes are a fruit" folklore. If a tomato
recipe doesn't specify an added acid, that's a sign it predates or ignores
current guidance.

Boiling water simply cannot get hot enough, no matter how long you boil
it, to solve a low-acid food's spore problem — that's a temperature
ceiling, not a time problem. Boiling a low-acid food longer in a water-bath
canner does not make it safe.

### Basic procedure, in outline

Both methods share a structure: prepare and pack food into clean jars
(raw-pack or hot-pack, depending on the recipe), leave the correct
**headspace** (the gap between the food/liquid and the jar rim — too little
and jars can boil over or fail to seal; too much and there may not be
enough heat penetration or vacuum; recipes typically call for somewhere
around 1/4 to 1 1/4 inch depending on the food, so follow the specific
recipe rather than a single rule), wipe the rim, apply a new lid and a
band, and process for a specific time.

**Processing time is not a single number for "canning" — it depends on the
specific food, the jar size, whether the pack is raw or hot, and your
altitude.** A pint of green beans, a quart of green beans, and a pint of
whole potatoes all have different tested times even though they're all
low-acid vegetables in a pressure canner. This page deliberately does not
print a table of times, because a wrong number that looks authoritative is
worse than no number — **use NCHFP's tested recipes and their processing
tables for the specific food, jar size, and pack style you're using.**

### Altitude matters

Water boils at a lower temperature as elevation increases, which changes
both methods:

- **Water-bath canning:** add processing time as altitude increases (the
  adjustment steps up in bands above roughly 1,000 ft).
- **Pressure canning:** increase the *pressure*, not the time, above 1,000
  ft — commonly 15 psi instead of 10 psi on a weighted-gauge canner above
  that altitude, with finer 1-lb adjustments possible on a dial-gauge
  canner. Check your canner's gauge type and NCHFP's altitude tables for
  the exact adjustment for your elevation.

Most of the northeastern US is below 1,000 ft, but hill and mountain towns
are not — check your actual elevation rather than assuming sea-level
values apply.

### Spoilage, failed seals, and the precaution that actually matters

Signs a jar has spoiled or never sealed properly: a bulging or unsealed
lid, a lid that flexes when pressed, spurting liquid or foam when opened,
mold, an off or unnatural smell, or cloudy liquid in a food that should be
clear. Discard any jar showing these signs without tasting it — pour it out
somewhere pets and wildlife can't reach it, or follow NCHFP's
detoxification procedure for jars you suspect are contaminated (boiling the
discarded contents and container before disposal, since the toxin itself
is destroyed by heat).

The harder problem: **improperly processed low-acid food can contain
active botulinum toxin with no visible, smellable, or tasteable sign at
all.** This is why NCHFP and USDA add a specific extra precaution for
home-canned low-acid vegetables: **boil them for 10 minutes before tasting
or serving** (add roughly a minute per 1,000 ft of elevation), even if the
jar looks and smells fine — the toxin, unlike the spore, is destroyed by
boiling. This is a precaution, not a license to relax on processing times;
current CDC guidance is still to discard home-canned food known or
strongly suspected to be underprocessed, rather than to boil and eat it.

## Drying (dehydration)

Drying preserves food by lowering **water activity (Aw)** — a measure of
how much water in the food is actually available for microbes to use, on
a scale from 0 (bone dry) to 1 (pure water) — rather than by lowering total
moisture content per se. Most bacteria stop growing below about Aw 0.90,
most molds stop below about Aw 0.70, and essentially nothing grows below
about Aw 0.60. Properly dried fruits, vegetables, and jerky are well below
these lines; the danger is food that is dry on the outside but still moist
in the middle, which can sit at an Aw high enough to support mold or
bacterial growth while looking finished.

### What dries well

Fruits, vegetables, and herbs dry well because they're mostly water and
carbohydrate. Meat (jerky) and some fish dry well but carry an extra
safety step because raw meat can carry pathogens that drying alone does
not reliably kill (see below). Fatty meats and fish dry poorly and go
rancid faster — trim fat before drying.

### Temperature and humidity

- **Fruits and vegetables:** roughly 125–135°F, with good airflow and
  ambient relative humidity kept low (under about 60%) so moisture actually
  leaves the food instead of re-condensing. Most vegetables benefit from a
  quick steam or boiling-water blanch first, which shortens drying time and
  helps preserve color and nutrients.
- **Herbs:** much lower heat than fruit or vegetables — they scorch and
  lose flavor/aroma easily.
- **Meat (jerky):** dried at a *higher* temperature specifically because of
  the pathogen risk in raw meat — see the jerky section below.

### Sun-drying vs. oven vs. dehydrator

- **Sun-drying** is free and traditional but depends on consistent hot, dry,
  breezy weather that the northeastern US doesn't reliably provide, and it
  exposes food to insects and airborne contamination unless well screened.
  It is a poor fit for meat because it can't reliably hit the pathogen
  kill-step temperature.
- **A kitchen oven** on its lowest setting, propped open a crack for
  airflow, works as a dehydrator substitute but is imprecise at the low end
  and less energy-efficient for long runs.
- **A dedicated food dehydrator** gives even, controllable temperature and
  airflow and is the most reliable option for jerky specifically because it
  can hold a steady, known temperature.

### Jerky-specific safety: the pre-heat step

Homemade jerky has caused real *Salmonella* and *E. coli* O157:H7
outbreaks, because a dehydrator's drying temperature alone does not
reliably kill bacteria in raw meat, and once meat is dried those bacteria
become *more* heat-resistant, not less — so heating dried jerky afterward
is a worse strategy than heating it before drying. Current USDA guidance:

- **Heat meat to 160°F and poultry to 165°F** (measured internal
  temperature) **before** dehydrating — for example, simmering strips in
  their marinade for several minutes, or oven-heating them, so any bacteria
  present are killed by moist heat while the meat is still easy to heat
  evenly.
- Then dry at a dehydrator/oven temperature in roughly the **130–140°F**
  range until a test piece cracks but doesn't break when bent.
- If you skip the pre-heat step, USDA's fallback is to heat the *dried*
  jerky afterward (for example, 10 minutes at 275°F for thin strips) to
  reach the same internal temperature — but pre-heating raw is the more
  reliable order of operations and the currently recommended approach.
- **Wild game and pork** carry an additional parasite (*Trichinella*) risk;
  freezing at 0°F for the specific time/thickness combination USDA
  publishes, or cooking to temperature, addresses that separately from the
  bacterial pre-heat step.

### Storage

Dried food needs an airtight container, kept cool and dark, ideally with
a desiccant or vacuum seal for longer storage; moisture re-entering the
package is what restarts spoilage. Dried fruit keeps roughly a year at
cool room temperature and considerably less in a hot space; dried
vegetables have roughly half that shelf life; jerky is best treated as a
weeks-to-a-few-months food unless frozen, since it's rarely dried to a low
enough water activity for true long-term shelf stability at home.

## Smoking

Smoking is really two different processes that share an apparatus and get
confused constantly: **hot smoking**, which cooks the food, and **cold
smoking**, which does not. Conflating them is the actual danger here.

### Hot smoking

Hot smoking holds food in the smoker at a temperature high enough to cook
it through — think of it as slow cooking with smoke flavor added, not as a
preservation method in its own right. The safety bar is the same as any
other cooked meat: reach and hold a safe internal temperature for the
specific food (whole poultry, ground meat, and whole muscle cuts each have
their own USDA target temperature), then treat the finished product like
any other cooked food — refrigerate it, and don't count on the smoke alone
to extend its shelf life beyond what cooked, refrigerated food normally
gets. Hot-smoked food that is *also* properly cured and dried (traditional
smoked hams, some smoked fish) can last longer at room temperature, but
that shelf-stability comes from the curing and drying, not from the smoke
itself.

### Cold smoking — the real risk

Cold smoking applies smoke at a temperature low enough that the food's
proteins don't cook (well under 100°F, often closer to ambient
temperature), specifically to add flavor and color without cooking. That
low-temperature, low-oxygen, extended-time environment is close to ideal
for *C. botulinum*: USDA identifies the danger zone for bacterial growth as
roughly **40–140°F**, and a cold smoker sits in the low end of that range
for hours, in a chamber that's also low in oxygen — exactly the combination
the bacterium likes.

**Cold smoking raw, uncured meat or fish at home and treating it as
finished/shelf-stable is genuinely risky.** USDA's practical guidance:
treat home cold smoking as a **flavoring step only**, not a cooking or
preservation step. Either:

- Fully cook the food afterward to a safe internal temperature, or
- Cure it first with salt **and measured sodium nitrite/nitrate** (not
  plain salt — see below) before cold smoking, which is how traditional
  cold-smoked products (some bacon, some smoked salmon at a commercial
  scale) are actually made safe.

Commercial cold-smoked products rely on precise combinations of salt
concentration, nitrite level, pH, water activity, and refrigerated storage
that are difficult to replicate reliably in a backyard setup — which is why
extension and USDA guidance is consistently cautious about home cold
smoking of anything that isn't already cured.

## Salting and curing

Salt preserves food by pulling water out through osmosis, which lowers
water activity the same way drying does. For meat specifically, though,
**plain salt is not enough to control *C. botulinum*** — that job is done
by sodium nitrite (and, for long dry-cures, sodium nitrate, which slowly
converts to nitrite), which is why "curing salt" and ordinary table salt
are different products used for different reasons.

### Dry curing vs. brining

- **Dry curing:** salt (and, for meat, curing salt) is rubbed directly onto
  the food's surface and it's left in a cool environment for days to weeks;
  moisture drips away as it's drawn out, concentrating flavor. Traditional
  dry-cured hams and some fish are made this way.
- **Brining (wet curing):** the food is submerged in a salt solution, or
  the solution is injected, rather than packed in dry salt. Brine
  concentration matters a lot: household guidance for general preservation
  brines runs roughly in the 15–20% salt range, while FDA treats a water-phase
  salt concentration of about 20% as sufficient on its own to stop
  botulism-causing bacteria in shelf-stable salt-cured products — a much
  higher concentration than most home "brine for flavor" recipes use.

### Why plain-salt meat curing is a real botulism risk

*C. botulinum* is anaerobic — it thrives specifically where oxygen is
absent, which describes the interior of a whole cut of meat, a sausage, or
a ham just as well as it describes the inside of a canning jar. Ordinary
table salt at the concentrations used in most flavor-motivated recipes does
not reliably stop it inside that anaerobic interior. **Curing salts —
sodium nitrite, sold blended with salt as products like Prague Powder
#1/Cure #1 (about 6.25% sodium nitrite, the rest salt), or Prague Powder
#2/Cure #2 for longer unrefrigerated dry cures — are what specifically
inhibit *C. botulinum* germination and growth** in cured meat, in addition
to contributing the characteristic pink color and cured flavor.

This is exactly where **old family or folklore "just use salt" meat-curing
recipes can be genuinely unsafe applied to modern meat and modern
conditions**: they may predate widespread nitrite use, assume a colder or
more controlled traditional curing environment than a home kitchen or
garage provides, or simply omit a step that was assumed common knowledge at
the time. A curing recipe for meat that specifies no measured curing salt
and no refrigeration/freezing plan is not something to take on faith just
because it's traditional.

Practical guidance for home curing of meat:

- Use a commercial curing-salt product, measured to the amount the product
  label specifies for the weight of meat — don't estimate, and don't
  substitute plain saltpeter (potassium/sodium nitrate) for a proper
  premix, since accidental over-concentration of pure nitrate/nitrite is
  itself toxic.
- Follow a tested cure ratio and time from an extension or USDA source for
  the specific product (bacon, ham, sausage, jerky-style cured meat) rather
  than scaling a recipe by feel.
- Keep cured-but-not-yet-finished meat refrigerated during the cure unless
  the specific recipe (and its salt/nitrite concentration) is explicitly
  designed for room-temperature curing.
- Dry-cured products that are never cooked, smoked hot, or refrigerated
  (traditional country hams, some salamis) depend on a specific, tested
  combination of salt %, nitrite/nitrate %, drying time, and final water
  activity — this is not a category to improvise in.
