# Choosing Ham Radios

## Summary

Ham radio equipment is not subject to the same certification restrictions as
GMRS: you may use any transmitter that operates within the amateur frequency
allocations and power limits, including used, imported, and homebrew gear. The
practical constraint is matching the radio to your intended use — a handheld
for field work, a mobile for the vehicle, a base for the home station — and
buying from a source where you can verify the unit works before you rely on it
in an emergency.

## Station types

| Type | Typical power | Antenna | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Handheld portable** | 1–8 W (FM), 5–20 W (SSB) | integral whip or external SMA | field work, hiking, emergency go-bag; the workhorse for mobile operations |
| **Mobile** | 25–100 W | vehicle-mounted external | vehicle-to-vehicle and long-range simplex; repeater access from the car |
| **Base / transceiver** | 50–200 W (VHF/UHF), 100–200 W (HF) | rooftop, mast, or wire antenna | permanent home station: talks to everything within range and through repeaters; HF for long-distance |
| **Repeater controller** | n/a (controls a separate repeater unit) | — | access/management of a local repeater you own or volunteer-run |

## What to buy by use case

### Handhelds (portables)

The most important radio in a preparedness kit: it goes with you when
everything else is at home.

**What matters:**
- **Battery life.** A 5 W handheld transmitting continuously will drain a
  standard Li-ion pack in 1–2 hours. Prioritize models with large batteries
  (1800 mAh or more) and buy at least one spare pack per radio. Some models
  accept standard 18650 cells — useful if you already stock them for other
  gear.
- **Ruggedness.** IP65 or better weather sealing. A drop onto gravel shouldn't
  kill it.
- **Weight and size.** You will carry this in a pack. Under 300 g is the
  target for daily-carry use.
- **Frequency coverage.** For local work you need 2 m and 70 cm. Some radios
  also cover HF or 6 m — convenient but adds weight and complexity.
- **Modes.** FM voice is the priority. SSB on VHF is a bonus (useful for
  weak-signal work). D-STAR/YSF/DMR digital modes are optional; they expand
  your reach via internet-linked reflectors but add a learning curve.

**Common models (as of 2026):**
- **Baofeng UV-5R / UV-82HP:** $30–50, dual-band 2 m/70 cm FM. The UV-82HP
  adds 8 W on 2 m. Cheap, ubiquitous, and good enough for a backup or spare.
  Not rugged; battery life is mediocre. Fine as a "what if" radio in the
  glovebox.
- **Baofeng BF-H9:** ~$60, similar to UV-5R but slightly more power.
- **TYT TH-F8 / TH-UV8G:** $50–70, dual-band, decent battery life.
- **Kenwood TH-D7A:** ~$300, 2 m/70 cm FM + D-STAR digital, APRS built in,
  GPS. The "do everything" handheld for a ham who wants digital modes and
  position reporting without a laptop.
- **Yaesu FT-6R / FT-8900:** ~$300–400, dual-band + tri-mode (FM/AM/NFM),
  good battery life, rugged.
- **Icom IC-92AD / ID-52A:** $400–700, high-end handhelds with D-STAR, GPS,
  and long battery life. Overkill for most preparedness use but excellent.

**Recommendation for a household go-bag:** One mid-range dual-band handheld
(TH-D7A or FT-6R class) per adult who will operate, plus a Baofeng UV-5R as a
"dead man's switch" spare that costs almost nothing to replace. A spare
battery pack and a hand crank or solar charger for each.

### Mobiles (vehicle)

The biggest range upgrade short of a repeater: 25–100 W through a proper
antenna on a moving platform.

**What matters:**
- **Power.** 50 W is the sweet spot: enough to hit any local repeater and work
  long-range simplex, without excessive heat or battery drain on the vehicle
  electrical system. 25 W is adequate if you primarily use repeaters.
- **Antenna mount.** A trunk-mounted antenna (magnetic base or permanent
  mount) with a good ground plane is the standard. Magnet mounts are
  convenient but can slip at speed; a permanent trunk mount or a roof mount
  (with proper gasketing) is more reliable.
- **Cabling.** A quality coax run (LMR-400 or better for runs over 10 ft)
  matters more than the radio's power rating. A bad cable eats watts.
- **Power supply.** Direct from the vehicle battery through an inline fuse
  and a noise filter. Do not run a 50 W mobile off a cigarette-lighter
  adapter with a thin wire.

**Common models:**
- **Yaesu FT-65R / FT-8800a:** ~$300–400, dual-band 2 m/70 cm FM, compact,
  reliable. The standard "first mobile."
- **Icom IC-2820H / IC-92AD (mobile version):** $300–500, dual-band, good
  audio.
- **Kenwood TM-V71A:** ~$300, dual-band, simple and tough.

### Base transceivers (home station)

The permanent station: highest power, best antenna, always on (or at least
always ready). For a household in New England, the priority is VHF/UHF for
local repeater access and emergency work, with HF as a secondary for
long-distance.

**VHF/UHF base:**
- **Yaesu FT-DX101D / FT-MD200:** $400–700, dual-band 2 m/70 cm, D-STAR,
  built-in TNC for APRS. A solid all-around home station.
- **Icom IC-91AD / IC-7100 (VHF/UHF portion):** high-end, excellent audio and
  features.
- **Budget:** A used Yaesu FT-25R or Icom IC-2730H (single-band 2 m, 50 W)
  for $100–200 is a perfectly good first base radio.

**HF transceiver (for long-distance):**
- **Yaesu FT-891 / FT-991A:** ~$600–800, 100 W HF + 50 W 2 m/70 cm in one
  unit. The standard "one radio does everything" for a home station. The
  FT-891 is the budget version (no built-in ATU).
- **Icom IC-7300:** ~$1000, 100 W HF only, excellent receiver and interface
  for digital modes. The "serious HF" choice.
- **Xiegu G90 / G100:** $200–400, 5–100 W HF, compact, modern design. A
  surprising amount of radio for the price; good as a first HF rig or a
  portable field station.

**Recommendation for a home station in New England:**
A dual-band VHF/UHF base (FT-MD200 or similar) for local repeater and simplex
work, plus an HF transceiver (FT-891 or Xiegu G90 to start) with a simple
wire antenna (end-fed random wire or a vertical on the roof) for 40 m and 80 m.
This covers local emergency communication (VHF/UHF repeaters + simplex) and
long-distance backup (HF SSB on 40/80 m) without a six-figure installation.

## Used vs. new

The ham radio used market is deep and healthy: eHam.net, the ARRL Exchange,
and local club swapmeets are full of working gear at half or less of the new
price. A ten-year-old Yaesu FT-897D (100 W HF/VHF/UHF all-in-one) in good
condition is a better emergency station than a new $300 handheld, and it costs
less.

**When buying used:**
- Ask for a photo of the unit powered on with a signal generator or another
  radio confirming it transmits.
- Check the FCC equipment authorization (FCC ID) if the unit is imported —
  some Chinese-made HF radios are not certified and technically should not be
  operated in the US, though enforcement against individual amateurs is rare.
- Budget for a new battery pack on handhelds; old Li-ion packs degrade
  regardless of storage.

## Accessories that matter

| Item | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Spare batteries** | A dead radio is a paperweight | Buy at least one spare per handheld; keep them charged (a battery cycler or simple charger in the go-bag) |
| **Coax cable and connectors** | Antenna connection is the most common failure point | Keep a 10 ft run of LMR-240 with PL-259 and BNC ends in the vehicle kit |
| **Antenna (backup)** | The whip on a handheld breaks; a mobile antenna can be lost | A 1/4-wave ground-plane for 2 m ($20) as a spare is cheap insurance |
| **Power supply / regulator** | Running from a 12 V vehicle or battery requires clean power | An inline fuse (25–30 A for 50 W), a noise filter, and a proper cable |
| **Headset / speaker-mic** | Hands-free operation in the vehicle; reduces PTT fatigue | A simple coiled-cable speaker-mic is sufficient |
| **Logbook** | Required by the FCC? No. But useful for tracking contacts, repeater checks, and emergency traffic | Paper (a small notebook) or a digital log (N1MM+, LoTW) |
