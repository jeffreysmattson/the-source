# Ham Radio Operating Procedure

## Summary

Operating ham radio well comes down to a few habits: identify with your call
sign, keep transmissions short and clear, listen before you transmit, and know
the local repeater conventions. The rules are simpler than they first appear —
the FCC requires station identification and prohibits certain content (see
below) — but the community norms matter as much as the regulations for keeping
the channels usable. For emergency preparedness, the key skill is joining a
local **net** (a scheduled group check-in) so that when a real event happens,
you already know who to call and how traffic gets relayed.

## Station identification

You must identify your station with your FCC-assigned call sign:

- **At the end of each communication exchange** (i.e., when you finish talking
  to another station) ([§97.119(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.119)).
- **At least every 10 minutes** during continuous operation (e.g., a long net
  or a broadcast) ([§97.119(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.119)).
- The identification must be in **audible voice** (for phone emissions) or
  **Morse code / readable signal** (for CW/data modes). You may also use a
  secondary identifier (e.g., "KB1ABC base" or "KB1ABC mobile") as long as the
  full call sign is included.

**In practice:** At the end of each contact, say "73, KB1ABC" ("73" is ham
shorthand for "best regards"). On a repeater, identify at the end of each
exchange: "This is KB1ABC, thanks." You do not need to identify at the start
of every transmission, but it's good practice on a busy channel so others know
who is talking.

## What you may and may not transmit

The FCC rules on content are specific ([§97.111–97.113](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/part-97)):

**Permitted:**
- Any communication incidental to the purposes of the Amateur Radio Service:
  technical experimentation, self-training, inter-station communications, and
  **emergency traffic**.
- Personal, non-commercial messages between amateur stations.
- Traffic for recognized public-service organizations (ARES, RACES, Red Cross)
  during declared emergencies.

**Prohibited:**
- **Commercial communication.** You may not use ham radio for business
  dispatch, commercial messaging, or any service offered for hire. The narrow
  exception is "incidental" — e.g., telling a friend "the store is closed" is
  fine; running a taxi dispatch is not.
- **Obscene or indecent language.** The same standard as broadcast radio.
- **Intentional false or deceptive signaling**, including false distress calls.
  A false MAYDAY is a federal offense.
- **Music** transmitted for the purpose of broadcasting (you may transmit a
  brief musical note as part of a signal, e.g., a keyer sidetone, but not a
  music program).
- **Rebroadcasting** content from other sources (e.g., streaming a podcast
  over ham radio).

**Emergency traffic:** When a disaster is declared (by local, state, or federal
authority), amateur stations may handle emergency traffic on any frequency in
the amateur bands. This includes voice, data, and even relaying messages for
public-service agencies. Outside of declared emergencies, handling "emergency
traffic" is limited to genuine personal safety situations (e.g., relay a
message that someone is lost or injured).

## Repeater operations

Repeaters extend your range to the line-of-sight horizon of the repeater site.
In New England, most 2 m and 70 cm repeaters are operated by local amateur
radio clubs and are free to use by any licensed amateur.

**Basic procedure:**
1. Tune to the **repeater input frequency** (the frequency you transmit on).
   Your radio should be programmed with both the input and output frequencies
   and the required CTCSS/DCS tone.
2. Listen for a few seconds to make sure the repeater is not in use.
3. Key up and say "CQ [area]" or "Calling CQ" if you want any station to
   respond, or call a specific station: "KB1XYZ, this is KB1ABC."
4. Keep exchanges short. Repeaters have a **timeout timer** (typically 5–10
   minutes of continuous use) and a **squelch tail** (a brief click after you
   release the PTT). If you talk over someone's tail, you've "talked over"
   them — say "sorry, I'll stand by."
5. Identify at the end of each exchange: "KB1ABC, thanks."

**Repeater etiquette:**
- Do not monitor a repeater for an extended period without transmitting
  ("parking on the repeater"). If you're just listening, use a separate radio
  or move to simplex.
- If two stations start talking at once (a "collision"), both should stop and
  one should yield: "Stand by, I'll let you go first."
- Some repeaters have **access restrictions** (tone-only, specific DCS codes,
  or even closed to certain call signs). Respect the posted rules; the repeater
  owner sets them.

## Nets

A **net** is a scheduled group communication: a net control station (NCS) calls
the frequency, stations check in one by one, and the NCS coordinates traffic
or information sharing. Nets are the backbone of organized amateur emergency
communication.

**Types of nets relevant to preparedness:**

| Net | Schedule (typical) | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Local club net** | Weekly, often Tuesday or Thursday evenings, 2 m FM | Club business, practice, check-in; the entry point for a new operator |
| **County/state ARES net** | Weekly or daily during activations | American Radio Relay League Emergency Service: coordinated emergency communication within a county or state |
| **RACES net** | Varies; activated by local government | Radio Amateur Civil Emergency Service: ham operators formally tasked by a government agency (FEMA, state EM) for emergency communications |
| **National traffic nets** | Daily, various times on HF | Relaying formal traffic (messages) between stations; less common now that email and phone handle most messaging, but still used for training and during outages |

**How to join a net:**
1. Find your local club's net schedule (ask at a VE session, check the club
   website, or listen on 146.520 MHz in the evening).
2. Tune in a few minutes early. The NCS will announce the net is starting and
   call for check-ins.
3. When called (or when the NCS opens for "open check-in"), key up: "NCS,
   KB1ABC checking in, [area]." The NCS will acknowledge or give you a number.
4. Listen for the rest of the net. If you have traffic (a message to relay),
   say "NCS, KB1ABC has traffic." Otherwise, stay quiet.
5. When the NCS closes the net, you're done.

**Why this matters for preparedness:** A net is a **rehearsal.** When a real
emergency hits — a winter storm that knocks out power and cell service for
days — the operators who show up on the frequency are the ones who've been
doing nets for months. They know the procedures, they know each other's call
signs, and they know how traffic gets relayed from your house to the emergency
management coordinator. If you want ham radio as a preparedness tool, doing
nets is not optional; it's the whole point.

## Emergency operations

When an actual emergency occurs (declared or otherwise), amateur radio
operations shift:

1. **Get on the frequency.** In New England, 146.520 MHz (2 m simplex) and
   the local ARES/RACES net frequencies are the first places to check. HF:
   7.185 MHz (40 m SSB) is a common emergency calling frequency.
2. **Keep transmissions short.** In a real emergency, the channel is crowded
   with people who need to get through. Short, clear messages: "This is
   KB1ABC at [location]. Power out, no cell service. Requesting status on
   [specific need]. Out."
3. **Do not block the channel.** If you have a non-urgent message, move to a
   different frequency or wait. The channel belongs to people with genuine
   emergencies.
4. **Relay traffic if you can.** If you hear someone calling for help and
   another station acknowledges, your job is to get out of the way. If you're
   in a position to relay (you can hear both stations), offer: "KB1ABC can
   relay between KB1DEF and KB1GHI."
5. **Log everything.** Write down times, call signs, and message content. In
   the aftermath, this log may be the only record of what was communicated and
   when.

!!! note "This is not a substitute for professional emergency services"
    Amateur radio emergency communication supplements — it does not replace —
    911, local emergency management, and public safety agencies. If you are in
    immediate danger, use 911 or any available means first. Ham radio is the
    backup when those systems are unavailable.

## Digital modes (briefly)

Digital modes are growing in the NE US ham community and add capabilities that
pure voice doesn't have:

- **APRS (Automatic Position Reporting):** Your handheld transmits your GPS
  location as a data packet, visible to anyone monitoring [aprs.fi](https://aprs.fi/).
  Useful for tracking hikers, confirming a station's location during an
  emergency, or building a local awareness map. Requires a GPS receiver (built
  into most modern handhelds) and a TNC (also built in, or a $20 external
  unit).
- **D-STAR / YSF (C4FM) / DMR:** Digital voice protocols that can be routed
  through internet-linked "repeaters" (reflectors), giving worldwide coverage
  from a local antenna. Useful when local repeaters are down but the internet
  is up (or vice versa). Not yet universal in New England; check with your
  local club on what's available.
- **FT8 / FT4 (HF digital):** The dominant HF "QSO" mode today. Two stations
  exchange 15-second computer-encoded transmissions that can be decoded from
  deep noise. Extremely efficient for long-distance work; not useful for
  real-time emergency voice communication but valuable for confirming that HF
  paths are open during an outage.

For a preparedness-focused station, **APRS on 2 m** is the highest-value digital
addition: it's simple, requires no internet, and gives you a position report
that other operators can see. Everything else is a refinement.
