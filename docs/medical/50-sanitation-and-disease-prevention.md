# Sanitation and Disease Prevention

## Summary

In most disasters, the event itself is not what kills the most people
afterward. **Broken sanitation is.** When toilets, sewers, and trash
collection stop working, human waste and garbage accumulate faster than most
people expect, and the fecal-oral diseases that follow — cholera, dysentery,
hepatitis A, norovirus, and other diarrheal illness — are well documented in
humanitarian and outbreak literature as the dominant secondary threat. A CDC
review of infectious disease after hydrologic disasters puts diarrheal
illness at up to **40% of deaths after a natural disaster**, concentrated in
displaced populations; humanitarian mortality studies of refugee-camp
emergencies report similar or higher shares, with diarrheal disease
historically accounting for **more than 40% of deaths in the acute phase**
of a camp emergency, disproportionately among children under two. This page
is about breaking that chain: waste disposal, handwashing, food handling,
and pest control, done consistently, without needing a functioning sewer
system.

!!! warning "Not a substitute for professional care or real infrastructure"
    These pages are a reference for situations where professional medical
    care and normal sanitation infrastructure — municipal sewer, trash
    collection, a functioning septic system, public health services — are
    genuinely unavailable. None of the field measures below are as safe or
    as effective as that infrastructure, and they exist to reduce risk while
    it's down, not to replace it. Restore or reach real sanitation service
    as soon as you can, and seek a clinician for anyone who becomes seriously
    ill rather than treating diarrheal disease as routine.

## Why this matters more than it seems like it should

A working toilet and trash pickup are so ordinary that it's easy to
underrate what happens when they stop. The mechanism is simple and
well-established in outbreak literature: **fecal-oral transmission** — a
pathogen shed in one person's stool ends up, via contaminated hands, water,
food, or flies, in another person's mouth. Crowding, contaminated water, and
degraded sanitation are the shared precondition behind cholera outbreaks
(Bangladesh flood years, post-earthquake Haiti), hepatitis A and E outbreaks
after flooding, and norovirus outbreaks in shelters (over 1,000 evacuees
after Hurricane Katrina). This is exactly why the WASH acronym — **water,
sanitation, and hygiene** — is treated as one interdependent system in
disaster response rather than three separate problems: a failure in any one
undermines the other two.

## Human waste disposal without a working toilet

### Short-duration: bucket toilets

For a household, for days to a few weeks, a bucket toilet is simpler than
digging and doesn't require outdoor space or soil suitable for a pit.

- **Twin-bucket system** (recommended over a single bucket): one bucket for
  urine, one for feces and paper. Urine is roughly ten times the daily
  volume of feces, and separating them cuts odor, slows bacterial growth,
  and makes the solid-waste bucket last much longer between changes.
- Line the solid-waste bucket with **two heavy-duty plastic bags** (double
  bagging matters — a single bag tears and leaks).
- Add a scoop of absorbent/cover material after each use: **kitty litter,
  sawdust, wood ash, dry soil, or shredded paper** all work. This controls
  odor and makes the bag less liquid and less attractive to flies.
- When a bag is full or at the end of the day, **tie it off securely**,
  and move it to a covered outdoor container away from living and food-prep
  areas until it can be disposed of — a functioning sewer/septic cleanout,
  burial (see distances below), or, only where a local emergency declaration
  specifically authorizes it, with regular household garbage.
- Keep a lid on the bucket between uses and locate it away from where food
  is stored or prepared.

### Longer-duration: emergency latrines

Once you're dealing with weeks and a household rather than days, a dug
latrine handles more volume with less bag/litter consumption. **Siting is
the part that actually protects your water supply — don't skip it to save
digging time.**

**Distance and depth — sources roughly agree, with different margins:**

| Guidance source | From water source | From dwellings | Depth / clearance |
|---|---|---|---|
| U.S. household emergency-toilet guidance (King County WA, citing standard state/local guidance) | ≥100 ft (≈30 m) from any well, spring, or surface water, and downhill of it | 100 ft away from occupied structures | Pit ≥3 ft deep; bottom ≥1 ft above hardpan/water table |
| Sphere Handbook / UNHCR humanitarian standard (larger-scale, longer-duration) | ≥30 m where soil permeability hasn't been tested (more if fissured rock/limestone) | No more than 50 m (close enough that people, especially children and at night, will actually use it) | Bottom of pit ≥1.5 m above the groundwater table |

These converge on the same horizontal distance (~30 m / 100 ft) but the
humanitarian standard wants a deeper vertical margin above groundwater
(1.5 m) than the minimum U.S. household figure (1 ft) — treat **1.5 m of
clearance above the water table as the safer number** to design to,
especially given a northeastern U.S. high spring water table, and always
site the latrine **downhill and downgradient** from any well.

Additional guidance that holds regardless of exact numbers:

- **Never site a pit where it can drain toward a well, spring, or surface
  water** — slope direction matters as much as raw distance.
- **Cover waste with a layer of soil daily** (a small trench latrine, dug
  incrementally) or keep a lid on a single pit between uses. Uncovered
  waste is what draws flies and produces odor.
- A simple squat slab or seat over the pit, with a lid, plus basic screening
  for privacy, meaningfully increases the odds people — especially kids —
  actually use it instead of going elsewhere.
- **Not suitable with a high water table or in dense urban settings** —
  where the ground won't take a safe pit, bucket/bagging systems or a
  properly maintained composting toilet are the fallback, not a shallow
  substitute pit.
- For a household, one properly sited and covered pit or trench, well
  maintained, will outlast the emergency it's built for; humanitarian
  guidance for larger groups targets roughly 1 toilet per 20 people as the
  standard (up to 50 people per toilet accepted only as an initial, very
  short-term measure) — useful context if you're improvising for a
  multi-family or neighborhood situation, not just one household.

## Handwashing: the single highest-leverage intervention

If this page has one instruction to actually follow every time, it's this
one. Humanitarian and clinical infection-control literature consistently
rank handwashing as the most cost-effective intervention against
fecal-oral disease, ahead of any single sanitation or water-treatment
measure alone — because it's the step that closes the loop between
contaminated hands and the next meal.

**When (CDC key times):**

- After using the toilet or a bucket/latrine system
- After changing or cleaning up a child who used the toilet
- Before, during, and after preparing food, and before eating
- After handling garbage or waste bags
- After touching animals, animal waste, or animal feed
- Before and after caring for someone who is sick, or treating a wound
- After blowing your nose, coughing, or sneezing

**How:** soap and clean water, lathering all surfaces — backs of hands,
between fingers, under nails — for at least **20 seconds**, then drying with
a clean towel or air-drying. Water temperature (warm vs. cold) doesn't
change effectiveness; the soap and the friction/duration do the work.

**Hand sanitizer is a fallback, not an equivalent.** Alcohol-based sanitizer
(≥60% alcohol) reduces many bacteria and some viruses when soap and water
genuinely aren't available, but CDC guidance is explicit that it has real
gaps:

- **Norovirus:** CDC states plainly that hand sanitizer "does not work well
  against norovirus" and is not a substitute for washing — facilities that
  relied on sanitizer instead of handwashing have seen sharply higher
  norovirus outbreak rates.
- **_C. difficile_ spores:** alcohol does not reliably kill *C. diff*
  spores; soap-and-water washing, where the physical friction of rubbing
  removes the spores from skin, is the actual mechanism that works.
- Sanitizer also performs poorly on visibly dirty or greasy hands — it
  needs a relatively clean surface to work at all.

**Practical takeaway for a water-scarce situation:** keep a small dedicated
volume of water and soap for handwashing even when you're rationing water
for other uses — this is not the water use to cut first. Use sanitizer to
bridge genuine gaps (no water on hand between chores), but return to soap
and water at the key times above, especially after latrine use and before
handling food, and always after caring for anyone with vomiting or
diarrhea.

## Food safety ties directly to sanitation

Contaminated hands, surfaces, and water are also how food gets
contaminated, so food safety is really a sanitation practice:

- **Wash hands before food handling** (see above) — this is the single
  biggest link between the sanitation practices on this page and foodborne
  illness.
- **Separate raw from ready-to-eat.** Use different cutting boards/utensils
  for raw meat versus produce or cooked food, or wash thoroughly between the
  two — cross-contamination is a leading cause of foodborne illness even in
  normal conditions, and worse without reliable dishwashing water.
- **Without reliable refrigeration:** perishable food spends less safe time
  at room temperature, not more. Cook and eat perishables promptly, and
  don't taste-test food to judge spoilage — some toxins (notably from
  *Clostridium botulinum*) aren't detectable by smell or taste. For actually
  extending shelf life without power, see
  [Canning, Drying, Smoking, and Salting](../food/30-food-preservation-canning-drying-smoking-salting.md),
  which covers the methods and their real risks (including botulism risk
  from getting canning acidity or curing wrong) in depth — this page won't
  repeat that material.
- **Wash produce and utensils** in treated water when the wash water itself
  may be contaminated; see the water page below for making that water safe
  in the first place.

## Water sanitation

Everything above assumes your drinking and food-prep water is itself safe.
It frequently won't be when sewage or floodwater has mixed with a water
source — this is exactly the fecal-oral pathway this page is about, just
arriving through the tap or well rather than through hands or food. Rather
than repeat that material here, see
[Water Purification](../water/20-water-purification.md) for boiling,
chlorination, iodine, filtration, and SODIS methods and their limits (in
particular, chlorine's weaker performance against protozoan cysts like
*Cryptosporidium*).

## General waste and trash management

Garbage is a sanitation problem even when it contains no human waste,
because it feeds the same disease vectors covered below:

- **Unmanaged trash draws flies, mosquitoes, and rodents**, all of which
  are documented disease vectors — flies mechanically transfer bacteria
  from waste to food and surfaces; rodents spread leptospirosis and other
  disease through urine and droppings and can contaminate stored food.
  Public-health guidance on vector control is blunt about the mechanism:
  sanitation (removing what the vector feeds and breeds on) is the primary
  and most durable control, more so than pesticide use after the fact.
- **Keep a clean perimeter.** Store household garbage in a sealed,
  animal-proof container, away from the house and food-storage area, and
  don't let it accumulate for days in warm weather.
- **Burying** is reasonable for a household's food waste and non-recyclable
  trash where you have suitable ground: bury at least a couple of feet deep
  and away from where you'd site a latrine or well (same distance logic —
  keep it out of drainage toward any water source).
- **Burning** reduces volume for some waste but produces heavy smoke and
  odor and is not appropriate for plastics (toxic fumes) or in an actual
  wildfire-risk or air-quality-restricted setting; check what's legal and
  safe locally rather than defaulting to it. It is not a substitute for
  proper disposal of anything with sharp or hazardous content.
- **Sort out anything sharp, medical, or hazardous** (broken glass,
  needles, batteries, chemicals) separately, wrapped or contained, so it
  doesn't become a hazard to whoever handles the rest of the trash.

## Vector and pest control at the household level

This is home-scale mitigation, not professional pest control — the goal is
removing what attracts and breeds pests, since that's the intervention with
the actual evidence behind it.

- **Mosquitoes** breed in standing water, often surprisingly little of it.
  **Weekly**, empty, cover, or overturn anything holding water — buckets,
  tarps, tires, birdbaths, clogged gutters, uncovered rain barrels. Screen
  windows/doors where possible, and use repellent and permethrin-treated
  clothing in areas where you can't eliminate all standing water (a stream,
  wetland, or neighbor's property you don't control).
- **Rodents** move toward available food, water, and shelter, and disaster
  conditions (flooding, structural damage) increase both their numbers and
  their movement into human spaces. Keep food — human and pet — in sealed,
  hard containers; eliminate the clutter and gaps rodents shelter in; and
  address an active infestation promptly rather than letting it establish,
  since droppings and urine carry disease risk that increases with
  exposure and volume.
- **Flies** track sanitation almost directly — covered latrines/buckets,
  sealed trash, and prompt cleanup of food waste and spills are the
  actual fly-control measures; chemical fly control without fixing the
  waste problem underneath it is a losing, repeating battle.

## Personal hygiene with limited water

- **Bathing:** a full shower isn't required for meaningful hygiene. A
  washcloth bath focused on hands, face, underarms, groin, and feet, with a
  small amount of water and soap, covers most of the actual disease-
  prevention benefit. Baby wipes or similar are a reasonable bridge when
  water is very scarce.
- **Dental hygiene:** keep brushing with what water you have — even a dry
  brush or brushing with a small rinse matters, since oral infection is a
  real secondary risk when dental care is unavailable and small problems
  don't get treated early.
- **Wound hygiene ties directly into this page**: hands that aren't clean
  contaminate wound dressings during changes, and a compromised sanitation
  environment (standing water, unmanaged waste) raises the ambient
  bacterial load around any wound care you're doing. See
  [Wound Cleaning and Closure](10-wound-cleaning-and-closure.md) for the
  wound-care procedure itself — the sanitation-relevant point here is:
  wash hands before touching a wound or dressing, same as before handling
  food.
- **Menstrual and diaper hygiene:** treat these as waste-stream problems too
  — dispose of soiled material the same way as other solid waste (sealed,
  not left loose), and wash hands afterward.

## Isolating a sick household member

When someone in the household has a diarrheal or vomiting illness — exactly
the fecal-oral diseases this page is about — reducing their contact with
shared surfaces and other people slows or stops household spread:

- Use a **separate bathroom or bucket toilet** for the sick person if at
  all possible. If that's not possible, clean and disinfect shared surfaces
  (toilet seat, faucet handles, door handles) after each use.
- **Don't share** cups, utensils, towels, or bedding until the person has
  recovered and items have been washed.
- **Whoever is caring for them washes hands** — soap and water, not just
  sanitizer, given the norovirus/_C. diff_ limitation above — after any
  contact, and especially after handling soiled laundry, diapers, or waste.
- **Improve ventilation** in shared spaces where practical; it doesn't stop
  fecal-oral transmission by itself but is a reasonable adjunct for any
  respiratory component.
- Watch for dehydration (the actual killer in diarrheal illness) and start
  oral rehydration early; a sick person who is also dehydrated and cut off
  from care is the scenario this whole page is trying to prevent.
