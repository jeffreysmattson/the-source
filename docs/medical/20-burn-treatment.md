# Burn Treatment

## Summary

Burns are common, painful, and easy to make worse with well-meant folk
remedies. This page covers first aid for **minor to moderate thermal burns**
when a clinician genuinely cannot be reached — cooling, blister and dressing
care, and pain/fluid management. It also gives clear criteria for burns that
need professional or hospital care regardless of how remote you are, because
some burns are dangerous in ways that are not obvious by looking at them.

!!! warning "Not a substitute for professional care"
    These pages are a reference for situations where professional medical care
    is genuinely unavailable. Seek a clinician whenever one can be reached.
    Burns are deceptively easy to under-estimate — depth is often impossible
    to judge in the first hours, and several burn categories below need
    hospital-level care even when they look small.

## Burns that need professional or hospital care regardless

The American Burn Association's burn center referral criteria are the
working standard cited by EMS and hospitals. If any of the following apply,
this is a "get to care" situation, not a "manage at home" situation, even if
that means a delayed evacuation rather than no evacuation:

- **Any full-thickness (third-degree) burn**, at any size.
- **Partial-thickness burns covering more than about 10% of total body
  surface area (TBSA)** in an adult — roughly one entire arm, or the front of
  one leg. Smaller partial-thickness burns still merit a remote consult when
  one is reachable.
- **Burns to the face, hands, feet, genitals/perineum, or over a major joint**
  (knee, elbow, ankle, wrist, shoulder), regardless of size — these areas have
  a high risk of disabling scarring, contracture, or loss of function.
- **Circumferential burns** — a burn that fully encircles a limb, digit,
  neck, or the chest. Burned skin loses elasticity and can act like a
  tourniquet as the tissue underneath swells over the following hours,
  cutting off circulation or breathing. This is a surgical emergency
  (escharotomy) if it progresses, not something to wait out.
- **Chemical burns of any size.**
- **Electrical burns and lightning strikes** — see the section below; these
  are always treated as more serious than they look on the skin.
- **Any burn with suspected inhalation injury**: singed nasal/facial hair,
  soot around the mouth or nose, hoarseness, cough, or a burn sustained in an
  enclosed space or from an explosion.
- **Burns in infants, young children, adults over ~65, pregnant patients, or
  anyone immunocompromised or with significant comorbidities** (diabetes,
  heart or kidney disease) — the same burn is more dangerous at either end of
  age and in a compromised patient.
- **Pain that can't be controlled**, or a burn combined with other trauma.

If none of these apply, the burn is a reasonable candidate for the field
first-aid described below — but keep re-assessing over the next 24–48 hours,
because burns often look worse (or better-defined) than they did in the first
hour.

## Classifying burn depth

Burn depth is what drives everything downstream: healing time, infection
risk, scarring, and whether the burn belongs in this page at all. Depth is
genuinely hard to judge right after injury — a burn can **deepen over the
first 24–48 hours** as damaged tissue that looked viable dies off (this is
sometimes called burn "conversion" or "extension"). Reassess, don't just
diagnose once and move on.

| Depth | Old name | Appearance | Sensation | Typical healing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Superficial** | First-degree | Red, dry, no blisters; blanches with pressure | Painful | 3–6 days, no scarring |
| **Superficial partial-thickness** | Second-degree (superficial) | Red, moist, clear blisters that blanch | Very painful (nerve endings exposed) | 2–3 weeks, minimal scarring |
| **Deep partial-thickness** | Second-degree (deep) | Mottled pink-to-white, may blister, drier, blanches slowly or not at all | Less painful than superficial — nerve endings partly destroyed | ≥3 weeks if it heals at all; scarring common; often needs grafting |
| **Full-thickness** | Third-degree | White, waxy, leathery, or charred/black; does not blanch | Little or no pain in the burned area itself (nerve endings destroyed) — surrounding partial-thickness burn still hurts | Will not heal on its own except by scarring from the edges; needs surgical care |

The **superficial-vs-deep partial-thickness distinction matters most for
prognosis**: superficial partial-thickness burns heal well with basic wound
care, while deep partial-thickness burns behave much more like full-thickness
burns and belong in the "get to care" category above whenever that's
possible. A burn that is unexpectedly *less* painful than its size would
suggest is a bad sign, not a good one — it can mean deeper nerve damage.

## Immediate first aid

1. **Stop the burning process first.** Remove the person from the heat,
   flame, or steam source. If clothing is on fire, have them stop, drop, and
   roll, or smother the flame. Remove smoldering clothing.
2. **Remove jewelry, watches, and tight clothing** from the burned area
   immediately, before swelling makes this difficult or impossible — this
   applies especially to rings on burned fingers and anything circumferential.
   Do not remove clothing that is stuck to the burn; cut around it instead.
3. **Cool the burn with cool running water.** Current international guidance
   (British Burn Association / NHS, Australian and New Zealand Burn
   Association, European Burn Association) is **cool — not ice-cold — running
   water for 20 minutes**, roughly 8–25°C (46–77°F), started as soon as
   possible and ideally within 3 hours of the burn (benefit drops off after
   that, but cooling still helps). This guidance was extended from an older
   ~10-minute recommendation in 2019 after evidence showed the longer duration
   improves outcomes; some sources (including Mayo Clinic's consumer
   guidance) still cite roughly 10 minutes, so treat **20 minutes as the
   better-supported target** and 10 minutes as an acceptable minimum if
   water, time, or hypothermia risk is limiting. Use a tap, shower, hose, or
   a clean wet cloth continually re-wetted with cool water if running water
   isn't available.
4. **Watch for hypothermia** while cooling, especially with larger burns,
   small children, or cold ambient conditions — cool the burn itself, keep
   the rest of the person warm and dry.
5. **Cover loosely** with a clean, non-fluffy dressing or cloth once cooled
   (see Dressing below).

### What NOT to do

- **Do not put ice or ice water directly on a burn.** It feels good briefly
  but causes vasoconstriction and additional tissue damage (frostbite-like
  injury on top of the burn) — use *cool*, not cold, water.
- **Do not apply butter, oil, mayonnaise, or thick ointments.** These trap
  heat in the skin, can worsen the injury, and have to be scraped off before
  a clinician can even assess the burn.
- **Do not apply toothpaste, egg white, or other folk remedies.** Toothpaste
  in particular contains abrasives and flavoring compounds that irritate
  already-damaged skin and raise infection risk. None of these have a
  legitimate medical use on a burn.
- **Do not use raw cotton wool, fluffy or fibrous dressings** directly on a
  burn — fibers stick to the wound.
- **Do not break blisters open with a needle or fingernail yourself** as a
  matter of routine — see Blisters below.

## Blister management

Guidance here is genuinely mixed, and this page says so rather than picking
one side silently:

- **The general default, and the position supported by the most recent
  clinical consensus, is to leave blisters intact** when reasonably possible.
  Intact blister skin is a sterile biological dressing — it protects the
  wound bed, keeps it moist, and reduces infection risk compared with an
  open wound.
- **Small blisters (roughly under 6 mm) that aren't in the way** are
  essentially always left alone.
- **Drainage becomes reasonable** for blisters that are very large and likely
  to rupture on their own from normal movement, thin-walled blisters that
  keep catching on clothing or gear, or blisters that have already torn.
  Some more recent clinical literature (e.g., a 2020 *Annals of Emergency
  Medicine* analysis) argues that select large blisters should not be left
  intact and do better drained early — this is the area of real disagreement
  among sources, and outcome differences are modest either way.
- **If you do drain one:** wash hands, clean the blister with soap and water
  or a wound antiseptic, use a sterilized needle or blade (flame or alcohol,
  then let it cool/dry), make a small puncture at the edge, let the fluid
  drain, and **leave the overlying skin (the blister roof) in place** as a
  natural dressing rather than peeling it off. Cover afterward.
- **Never use a dirty or unsterilized instrument**, and don't drain a blister
  repeatedly — each opening is a new infection opportunity.

## Dressing

- Once cooled, cover the burn with a **non-stick / non-adherent dressing** —
  purpose-made non-adherent gauze, a hydrogel or silicone-faced dressing, or
  in a pinch clean plastic film (like a clean kitchen wrap) as a temporary
  cover. The goal is a material that will not bond to weeping or healing
  tissue and tear it open again at the next change.
- Avoid fluffy gauze, cotton balls, or anything with loose fibers directly
  against the wound; a non-stick layer against the burn with an absorbent
  layer over it works well.
- **Change frequency** depends on how much the wound is weeping: as a
  practical field rule, **daily** for a moderately weeping partial-thickness
  burn, more often (every 8–12 hours) if the dressing is soaked through, and
  less often (every 2–3 days) once drainage has slowed and a non-adherent
  dressing is holding up well. Always change if the dressing becomes wet,
  dirty, or foul-smelling.
- **Clean at each change**: gently rinse with clean water or saline, pat
  (don't rub) dry around the wound, reapply a fresh non-stick dressing.
- **Signs of infection to watch for**, at any dressing change: increasing
  redness spreading beyond the original burn margin, red streaking away from
  the wound, increasing pain after the first couple of days (pain should
  trend down, not up), pus or cloudy/green/foul-smelling drainage, a wound
  that stops healing or starts looking worse, swelling beyond what's
  expected, and systemic signs — fever, chills, or feeling generally unwell.
  Red streaking plus fever is a "needs care now" combination, not a
  wait-and-see one.

## Pain, fluid, and nutrition

Burns are not just a skin injury — a large burn is a whole-body fluid-loss
and metabolic event, because burned skin can no longer hold fluid in or
regulate heat.

- **Pain**: burns, especially partial-thickness ones, are very painful
  because nerve endings are exposed rather than destroyed. Basic over-the-
  counter analgesics (following label dosing) and simply keeping the wound
  covered and undisturbed help considerably; cooling itself provides real
  pain relief in the first hour. This page does not recommend specific
  prescription-strength dosing — that's exactly the kind of decision to get
  professional input on if there's any way to reach it.
- **Fluids**: even burns well under the threshold for hospital care lose
  fluid through weeping and evaporation from the damaged skin. Encourage
  normal oral fluid intake and watch for the ordinary signs of dehydration
  (dark urine, dizziness, dry mouth). For anything approaching or exceeding
  roughly 10–15% TBSA, fluid losses become clinically significant — this is
  squarely in "needs care" territory above, and formal IV fluid resuscitation
  protocols exist for exactly this reason.
- **Nutrition**: larger burns trigger a hypermetabolic, catabolic stress
  response that can persist for weeks to months, driving markedly increased
  calorie and protein needs as the body works to heal and fights off
  infection risk. In an austere setting this translates to a practical
  priority: **don't let a burn patient go without food**, favor
  higher-protein intake if available, and understand that a person recovering
  from a significant burn will eat more than usual and lose weight/muscle
  mass even while eating reasonably well. This is one more reason moderate
  and larger burns are not a "wait it out" situation if care can be reached
  at all.

## Chemical burns

- **Remove contaminated clothing and jewelry immediately** while protecting
  yourself from the chemical (gloves if available) — clothing holds the
  chemical against the skin.
- **Flush copiously with clean, low-pressure running water** — a gentle
  stream, not a hard spray that can splash chemical onto unaffected skin or
  the responder. Continue for at least 20 minutes, and longer for alkalis
  (which tend to penetrate deeper) or if stinging continues.
- **Exception — dry powders, especially dry lime (calcium oxide):** **brush
  the dry powder off the skin first**, before adding any water. Water reacts
  with calcium oxide to form calcium hydroxide, a strong caustic alkali, and
  will actively worsen the burn if applied before the powder is removed.
  Once the visible powder is brushed away, flush as above.
- If a Safety Data Sheet or product label is available, check it for a
  specific antidote or contraindication (a few chemicals have one), but do
  not delay flushing to search for one — copious water/removal is correct for
  the great majority of chemical exposures.
- **All chemical burns warrant a professional evaluation regardless of
  apparent size** — surface appearance under-represents chemical burn depth,
  and some agents (hydrofluoric acid, phenol) cause systemic poisoning
  separate from the skin injury.

## Electrical burns

- Electrical burns can look like a small, unimpressive mark on the skin at
  the entry and exit points while causing **serious, sometimes fatal, injury
  along the current's path through the body** — deep tissue and muscle
  damage, cardiac rhythm disturbances, kidney injury from muscle breakdown,
  and internal injuries that are invisible from outside.
- **Do not touch the person until the power source is confirmed off** — you
  can become a second casualty. For high-voltage or downed-line situations,
  stay well back and wait for the source to be de-energized.
- Once safe to approach: check breathing and pulse, begin CPR if needed, and
  treat visible burns with the same cooling/dressing principles above.
- **Every electrical burn or shock — even one that looks minor — should be
  treated as needing professional evaluation**, because dangerous heart
  rhythm changes and delayed complications can appear hours later in someone
  who otherwise feels fine. High-voltage injuries (roughly ≥1,000V) and any
  lightning strike are unequivocal "get to care" emergencies.
