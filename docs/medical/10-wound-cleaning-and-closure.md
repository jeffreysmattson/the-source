# Wound Cleaning and Closure

## Summary

Most cuts and scrapes need nothing more than cleaning, a dressing, and time.
The judgment call this page exists for is the harder middle ground: a wound
clean and simple enough that closing it in the field is reasonable, versus one
that should be left open and watched, versus one that genuinely needs a
clinician and there is no safe substitute for that.

The order of operations matters and does not change: **stop the bleeding,
then clean the wound, then decide about closure.** Closing a wound is
optional and sometimes wrong. Stopping severe bleeding is not optional and the
first few minutes are what matter.

!!! warning "Not a substitute for professional care"
    This page is a reference for situations where professional medical care is
    genuinely unavailable. Seek a clinician whenever one can be reached —
    especially for anything below marked as needing one. Field wound care
    trades a better long-term outcome for a survivable one; it is not
    equivalent to a clinic visit.

## When this page is not enough

Get real medical care, evacuate, or call for help rather than field-manage the
wound yourself, if any of these apply:

- **Bleeding that direct pressure and elevation don't control**, or spurting/
  pulsing (arterial) bleeding — control what you can, then get care regardless.
- **Deep puncture wounds**, especially dirty, rusty, or through footwear —
  high infection and tetanus risk, and depth is hard to judge from outside.
- **An embedded object.** Do not pull out anything large or deeply lodged; it
  may be tamponading a bleeding vessel.
- **Animal or human bites** — contaminated by nature (see
  [closure decisions](#3-decide-close-it-or-leave-it-open) below). Human
  bites carry a serious infection rate; animal bites also raise a rabies
  question this page does not cover.
- **Signs of spreading infection or sepsis** (see
  [below](#signs-of-infection-what-to-watch-for)) — fever, confusion, red
  streaking, or rapidly spreading discoloration is an emergency.
- **Wounds over a joint, on the face, or near the genitals**, or **any wound
  where a good cosmetic/functional result matters** and a clinician — even a
  delayed one — can be reached. Field closure is for when that's not an
  option, not a substitute for sutures when sutures are available.
- **Crush injuries, dead/devitalized tissue, or a wound you can't get clean**
  despite irrigation.

## Quick reference

| Situation | Action |
|---|---|
| Severe limb bleeding, direct pressure failing | Tourniquet, high and tight, note the time |
| Minor bleeding | Direct pressure + elevation, no tourniquet |
| Fresh, clean, linear cut, low contamination, <6–8 h old | Irrigate, consider field closure |
| Puncture, bite, crush, heavily contaminated, or wound clock unknown/long | Irrigate, **leave open**, pack/dress, watch for infection |
| Hydrogen peroxide / full-strength iodine *in* the wound bed | Don't — see [Antiseptics](#antiseptics-use-and-limits) |
| Deep, near a joint, embedded object, animal/human bite, spreading infection | Professional care, not field closure |

## Procedure

### 1. Stop the bleeding first

Do this before you think about cleanliness. A clean wound that someone bled
out from was cleaned for nothing.

1. **Direct pressure.** Press firmly on the wound with the cleanest available
   cloth or gauze. Maintain continuous pressure — don't lift to check every
   few seconds. Add more material on top of a soaked dressing rather than
   removing it.
2. **Elevate** the injured area above the heart if it doesn't delay pressure
   or worsen another injury (e.g., a suspected fracture). This is a helpful
   adjunct, not a substitute for pressure.
3. **Tourniquet, for severe limb bleeding pressure doesn't control**, or
   bleeding you cannot otherwise reach (e.g., under entrapment). This is the
   current Stop the Bleed / TCCC-derived civilian standard — a real change
   from older first-aid teaching that treated tourniquets as a last resort to
   avoid. Modern guidance: apply it promptly rather than losing minutes to
   pressure alone on bleeding that is clearly severe.
    - Place it directly on skin, **2–3 inches above the wound**, and never
      directly over a joint.
    - Tighten the windlass until the bleeding **stops completely** and a
      distal pulse is gone — not just "slowed."
    - **Write the time of application** clearly on the tourniquet or the
      patient's skin. Downstream care depends on knowing how long it's been on.
    - **Do not loosen or remove it in the field.** Leave it in place until the
      patient reaches definitive medical care. Prolonged-field-care protocols
      that convert a tourniquet to pressure dressings exist, but they require
      training — without it, leave it on.
    - If bleeding continues at maximum tightness, apply a **second
      tourniquet** just above (closer to the torso from) the first.
4. **Wound packing**, for severe bleeding at a junction where a tourniquet
   can't be applied (neck, groin, armpit, shoulder): pack gauze directly into
   the wound cavity while holding firm direct pressure for a full 3 minutes,
   then apply a pressure dressing over the packed gauze.

Only move on to cleaning once bleeding is controlled.

### 2. Clean the wound

**Irrigation is the single most effective step** — mechanically flushing out
bacteria and debris matters more than what you flush with.

- **Fluid:** clean potable water is an acceptable, evidence-supported
  substitute for sterile saline for ordinary, uncomplicated wounds in an
  otherwise healthy person — a 2012 Cochrane review and more recent scoping
  reviews both find no meaningful difference in infection rates. Prefer
  actual sterile saline for deep wounds, puncture wounds, or an
  immunocompromised patient if you have it; use treated/potable tap water
  otherwise rather than skipping irrigation.
- **Pressure matters more than most people expect.** A gentle rinse under-
  performs. The commonly cited effective range is roughly **5–8 psi**,
  achievable with a 30–60 mL syringe fitted with an 18- or 19-gauge
  catheter tip, or a punctured water-bottle cap held close to the wound and
  squeezed firmly. A gravity drip or squeeze bulb (under ~1 psi) is not
  enough. You do not need to reach the ≥25 psi used for grossly contaminated
  surgical washouts — that range offers no extra benefit for routine wounds
  and can drive debris deeper.
- **Volume:** roughly **500–1,000 mL for an average wound**, or a working
  rule of about **60 mL per centimeter of wound length**. More for anything
  visibly dirty; irrigate until it runs clear and you can see the wound bed.
- **Debris removal:** pick out visible foreign material (grit, splinters,
  fabric) with clean tweezers or forceps as part of irrigation.
- **Do not scrub the wound bed.** Aggressive scrubbing damages tissue and
  increases infection risk rather than lowering it; irrigation volume and
  pressure do the work that scrubbing is often reached for. Gently wiping
  visible surface debris away is fine — grinding at the wound is not.
- **No additive is the current wilderness-medicine default.** Wilderness
  Medical Society guidance is to irrigate with plain potable water or saline
  and add nothing to the irrigation fluid.

#### Antiseptics: use and limits

First-aid folklore and current evidence disagree here, and it's worth being
explicit about what changed.

- **Hydrogen peroxide poured into a wound is now discouraged as routine
  care.** It is cytotoxic to fibroblasts — the cells that rebuild tissue —
  and repeated use measurably delays healing. The fizzing is not a
  meaningful measure of antisepsis. It is particularly dangerous in **deep
  puncture wounds**, where released gas in an enclosed space has caused air
  embolism; do not use it to "flush out" a deep or narrow wound. Reserve it,
  if at all, for a single, one-time wipe on a dirty superficial scrape where
  mechanically lifting debris is the goal — never as ongoing wound care and
  never poured *into* a wound cavity.
- **Full-strength iodine (tincture) directly in a wound bed** is similarly
  discouraged — undiluted povidone-iodine is toxic to the same healing cells,
  with fibroblast growth measurably inhibited even at low concentrations.
  Diluted povidone-iodine (roughly 1% or less) retains antimicrobial effect
  with much less tissue toxicity and is reasonable on intact skin around a
  wound margin, but plain irrigation is the preferred treatment for the
  wound itself.
- **Mild soap and water** on the surrounding skin is fine and useful; avoid
  driving soap into a deep wound.
- **Net practical guidance:** irrigate generously with clean water or
  saline; skip peroxide and iodine *inside* the wound as routine practice;
  use a dilute antiseptic on surrounding skin if you have one and want extra
  margin.

### 3. Decide: close it, or leave it open

This is the highest-consequence decision on this page, because **closing a
contaminated wound traps infection inside it** — worse than leaving it open.

**Reasonable candidates for field closure:**

- Clean mechanism — a sharp, linear cut rather than a tear, crush, or bite.
- Low visible contamination, and it irrigated clean.
- Not a puncture and not a bite of any kind.
- Recent — see the timing note below.
- Not over a joint under tension, not on the face/hands/genitals if you
  want a good cosmetic or functional outcome (send those to a clinician
  instead if at all possible).
- Bleeding controlled and the wound edges come together without much
  tension.

**Should be left open, packed, and allowed to heal by secondary
intention (granulation) instead:**

- Any bite (animal or human) — contaminated with oral bacteria by definition.
- Puncture wounds — narrow, deep, and hard to clean or irrigate fully.
- Crush injuries or wounds with visibly dead/devitalized tissue.
- Heavily contaminated wounds (soil, feces, organic debris) that don't come
  clean with irrigation.
- Anything already showing early infection.
- A wound in an immunocompromised person, where the safety margin is smaller.

For an open/packed wound: irrigate, pack loosely with clean (moist-to-dry, if
available) gauze so it doesn't seal over, dress, and change the packing on a
schedule (see below). Many of these can still be closed later — **delayed
primary closure** at 3–5 days, once you're confident it has stayed clean — a
compromise clinicians use for exactly this situation.

**On the "golden period" for closure — sources disagree, so here's both
sides.** Older teaching and Wilderness Medical Society field guidance give
practical numbers: most clean wounds can be closed within roughly **4–6
hours** of injury, extended to **10–12 hours for the face and scalp** (better
blood supply, lower infection rates there regardless of timing). But a 2022
systematic review found **no strong evidence for a specific hour cutoff** —
the studies behind the old "6-hour rule" are weak, and wound characteristics
(contamination, mechanism, location, and the person's health) predict
infection better than the clock does. This wiki's practical takeaway: treat
the WMS hour ranges as a reasonable, conservative default when you have
nothing else to go on, but a clean, well-irrigated wound that's a bit older
than that is not automatically wrong to close, while a dirty or puncture
wound within the window is still wrong to close. Judge the wound, not just
the stopwatch.

### 4. Field closure methods

Only for wounds selected above. In rising order of skill required and
consequence of getting it wrong:

**Steri-strips / butterfly closures** — the safest field option.

- Dry the skin around the wound thoroughly; adhesive fails on wet or oily
  skin. Tincture of benzoin (if you have it) improves adhesion.
- Approximate the wound edges with your fingers, then lay strips across
  perpendicular to the wound line, starting at the center and working
  outward.
- Leave small gaps between strips rather than overlapping them edge-to-edge
  — the wound still needs to be able to drain, and sealing it fully invites
  infection underneath.

**Skin adhesive (cyanoacrylate, e.g., medical Dermabond-type glue)** — good
for small, low-tension, superficial lacerations, especially on the face.

- Irrigate and dry the wound first; adhesive needs a dry field and doesn't
  work on wet or mucosal surfaces.
- Hold the edges together by hand and brush adhesive **across** the closed
  edges, in a thin layer, in 2–3 coats, letting each dry.
- **Never press the applicator into the wound itself** — forced into the
  wound (rather than just across the surface), it can trigger a foreign-body
  reaction and cause the closure to fail from the inside. It bonds skin
  surface to skin surface; it is not a wound filler.
- Do not use it on contaminated or infected wounds, bites, punctures, joints
  under tension, or moist areas (mouth, groin, armpit) — moisture breaks the
  bond and traps whatever is under it.
- No topical ointment afterward (it dissolves the adhesive). It sloughs off
  on its own in about 5–10 days.
- Medical-grade cyanoacrylate is a purified, medical-use formulation — this
  is not an endorsement of substituting hardware-store cyanoacrylate
  ("super glue"), which is manufactured and purified differently and is not
  validated for use on living tissue, whatever internet folklore claims.

**Sutures or staples** — real last resort, and be honest with yourself about
this one.

- These require actual hands-on training to place safely: local anesthesia,
  correct needle handling, appropriate spacing and tension, and knowing which
  wounds are and are not appropriate to suture at all. A "just-in-case"
  suture kit bought for a preparedness stockpile does not confer that
  training.
- Improvised suturing without training carries real risk — poor tissue
  approximation, trapped dead space that becomes an abscess, nerve or vessel
  injury, and a worse cosmetic result than simply leaving the wound open and
  letting it granulate.
- If you have genuine training, use it. If you don't, prefer strips or
  adhesive on appropriate wounds, and leave everything else open rather than
  suturing blind.

### 5. Dressing, bandaging, and follow-up

- Cover a closed wound with a non-stick dressing and a secondary absorbent
  layer; secure it without cutting off circulation (check color, warmth, and
  sensation distal to the bandage).
- **Change frequency:** daily, or immediately if the dressing becomes wet,
  soiled, or loosens. A closed wound generally tolerates brief wetting after
  24–48 hours; keep an open, packed wound's dressing changed on the schedule
  you decided when you packed it (often once or twice daily) so you're
  re-checking the wound bed regularly, not sealing in a problem unseen.
- Keep the area elevated where practical to reduce swelling.

#### Signs of infection — what to watch for

Check daily for several days after any wound, closed or open:

| Sign | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Redness/warmth spreading beyond the wound margin | Local infection developing |
| Increasing pain after the first day or two (pain should trend down, not up) | Local infection developing |
| Pus, cloudy drainage, or a foul odor | Local infection |
| Swelling that keeps increasing | Local infection or poor drainage |
| **Red streaking** extending away from the wound (toward the torso) | **Spreading infection — seek care urgently** |
| **Fever, chills, or confusion** | **Possible sepsis — seek care urgently, treat as an emergency** |
| Wound area going numb, gray, or black | Tissue death or a fast-moving infection — emergency |

On darker skin, redness and streaking may be harder to see and can appear
purplish-gray or simply darker than the surrounding skin rather than red —
go by warmth, swelling, and pain trend as well as color.

## Tetanus

Worth stating even if you have no vaccine on hand, because it changes what
you do and don't rely on:

- Tetanus is caused by spores (common in soil and dust) that germinate in
  low-oxygen conditions — exactly what a deep puncture, a wound packed with
  dirt, or a wound with dead tissue provides. Puncture wounds and dirty,
  devitalized wounds are specifically flagged as tetanus-prone by CDC
  guidance.
- **No antibiotic, topical or systemic, is a substitute for tetanus
  vaccination.** Thorough cleaning and debridement of dead tissue reduces
  risk but does not eliminate it.
- The actual prevention is vaccination *before* you need it, plus a booster
  after a dirty or major wound if your last dose was more than 5 years ago
  (or more than 10 years ago for a clean, minor wound). Know your own
  vaccination history now, while you can still act on it — this is not
  something field wound care fixes after the fact.
