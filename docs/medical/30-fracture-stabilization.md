# Fracture and Joint Injury Stabilization

## Summary

This page covers **splinting to stabilize and protect** a suspected fracture,
dislocation, or serious joint injury when professional medical care is delayed
or unavailable — not how to set, reduce, or definitively treat one. Real
fracture care requires imaging and, often, surgery. What follows is field
first aid: reduce pain, prevent a closed injury from becoming an open one,
protect nerves and blood vessels, and buy time until the person reaches a
clinician.

!!! warning "Not a substitute for professional care"
    These pages are a reference for situations where professional medical care
    is genuinely unavailable. Seek a clinician whenever one can be reached.
    A splint is a way to protect an injury during a delay — it is not
    treatment, and it does not substitute for an X-ray, a reduction, or
    surgery. Evacuate to real care as soon as it is possible to do so.

You often cannot tell for certain, in the field, whether a bone is broken or
a joint is badly sprained. **When in doubt, treat it as a fracture** — splint
it, protect it, and get the person to care. Over-splinting a sprain costs
little; under-splinting a fracture can cost a limb.

## Red flags — evacuate urgently regardless of how well it's splinted

Some injuries need urgent, physical evacuation to a hospital no matter how
good the field splint is. Splinting does not resolve any of these — it only
buys time while you move toward help faster.

!!! danger "Evacuate immediately for any of these"
    - **Open (compound) fracture** — bone has broken the skin, or a wound
      overlies a suspected fracture. This is a major infection risk
      (contaminated bone and deep tissue) on top of the fracture itself.
    - **Signs of compromised circulation or nerve function** below the
      injury — a cold, pale, blue, numb, tingling, or immobile hand or foot.
      See below; don't wait for all of these to appear before acting.
    - **Suspected spine or neck injury** — significant mechanism (fall, high-
      speed impact, diving, being struck) plus neck/back pain, numbness,
      tingling, weakness, or loss of control of bladder/bowel.
    - **Suspected pelvis fracture** — high-energy mechanism (fall, crush,
      vehicle/animal collision) with pelvic pain, especially with signs of
      shock. This can bleed catastrophically internally with no external
      sign.
    - **Suspected femur (thigh bone) fracture** — the femur is surrounded by
      large muscles and blood vessels; a closed femur fracture alone can
      cause life-threatening internal blood loss.
    - **Obvious severe deformity combined with any of the above** — a limb
      that looks grossly wrong *and* has circulation or nerve signs is not a
      "splint and wait" situation.

## Is it broken? Fracture vs. sprain/strain in the field

Without an X-ray you often cannot be sure. These findings make a fracture more
likely, and any one of them is reason enough to splint and treat it as a
fracture:

| Finding | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| **Point tenderness over a bone** | Pain sharply localized to one spot on the bone itself, not diffusely around a joint, is a strong fracture indicator |
| **Inability to bear weight or use the limb** | Can't take four steps unassisted, or can't use the hand/arm for any task |
| **Visible deformity** | Angulation, shortening, or rotation that doesn't match the other limb |
| **Rapid, significant swelling or bruising** | Especially if it appeared within minutes, suggesting bleeding at a fracture site |
| **Crepitus** | A grinding or crunching sensation/sound with movement — do not deliberately move the limb to check for this |
| **Deformity plus a snap or pop felt/heard at the time of injury** | Common fracture and dislocation history |

A sprain (ligament) or strain (muscle/tendon) can still be quite painful and
swollen without any of these — but if you're unsure, splint it as a fracture.
It rarely hurts to immobilize a bad sprain the same way.

## General splinting principles

- **Immobilize the joint above and below the injury.** A fractured forearm
  bone needs the wrist and elbow immobilized; a fractured bone at a joint
  needs the bone above and below that joint immobilized. The splint should
  extend past both.
- **Splint in the position found, in most cases.** Attempting to straighten a
  deformed limb in the field risks further damage to bone, nerve, and blood
  vessel, and you cannot confirm alignment without imaging. The one broadly
  taught exception: if the limb **has no pulse or is clearly losing
  circulation** (cold, pale/blue, distal to an obvious deformity) and
  evacuation is not imminent, gentle in-line traction to try to restore
  circulation is taught in wilderness medicine curricula — see below. This is
  a judgment call with real risk, not a routine step.
- **Pad the splint.** Rigid material against skin, especially over bony
  points (wrist, ankle, elbow), causes new pressure injuries. Pad generously,
  particularly in the joints and voids of the splint.
- **Check circulation and sensation before splinting, immediately after, and
  periodically afterward** (roughly every 15–30 minutes early on, and at
  least hourly during evacuation). Check for: skin color and warmth compared
  to the uninjured side, a distal pulse if you can find one, capillary refill
  (skin should pink back up within ~2 seconds of a light press), and whether
  the person can feel light touch and move fingers/toes. Loosen and re-pad
  immediately if a limb becomes more numb, cold, or pale after splinting —
  the splint itself is a common cause.
- **Don't splint too tight.** Swelling increases in the hours after an
  injury; a splint that fit well at hour one can cut off circulation by
  hour three. Leave fingers or toes exposed at the end of the splint so you
  can watch color and warmth without unwrapping it.
- **Remove rings, watches, and tight clothing** near the injury before
  swelling makes that impossible.

## Materials

None of this requires medical equipment — rigid support, padding, and
something to secure it are the whole recipe.

- **Rigid support:** sticks/branches, trekking poles, tent poles, paddles,
  rolled-up magazines or foam pads, cardboard, a SAM-type malleable splint if
  you carry one, an inflatable splint if you carry one.
- **Padding:** clothing, a jacket, a bandana, foam pad, blanket — anything
  soft between the rigid material and the skin, and packed into gaps (like
  under the arch of the foot or the curve of the wrist).
- **Ties:** cravat bandages, torn cloth strips, tape, cordage, belts. Tie
  around the splint, not directly and tightly over the injury site itself,
  and space ties so none sits right on the fracture point.

## Procedure — building a splint

1. **Control bleeding and expose the injury.** Cut away clothing over the
   injury rather than pulling it. Control bleeding with direct pressure
   before splinting (see wound care). If bone is visible through the skin,
   do not push it back in — cover it with the cleanest material available
   and treat it as an open-fracture emergency (see red flags above).
2. **Check circulation and sensation first.** Before touching the limb
   further, note pulse, skin color, warmth, and whether the person can feel
   and move fingers or toes distal to the injury, with a time. This is your
   baseline for later comparisons.
3. **Choose and pad the splint.** Rigid material long enough to immobilize
   the joint above and below the injury, padded well, especially at the ends
   and over bony points.
4. **Apply and secure.** Support the limb (a second person holding gentle,
   steady support helps) while positioning the splint. Tie above and below
   the injury first, then in between — snug enough to prevent movement,
   loose enough to slide a finger under.
5. **Recheck circulation and sensation.** Compare against your baseline;
   loosen and re-pad immediately if it's worse.
6. **Elevate and monitor.** Elevate above heart level if possible, and keep
   rechecking circulation/sensation periodically throughout the wait for
   evacuation.

## Splinting common sites

**Forearm or wrist:** Rigid material along the underside (palm side) of the
forearm from beyond the elbow to beyond the fingertips, padded well at the
wrist. A rolled sock or bandana in the palm keeps the hand in a natural,
slightly cupped position. Secure with a sling and swathe (below) to also
support the arm against the body.

**Ankle or lower leg:** Pad heavily around the ankle and heel. Rigid material
along both sides of the leg (or a single piece bent to wrap the sole and both
sides, "sugar-tongue" style) from below the knee to beyond the foot. Leave
toes visible for circulation checks. A rolled blanket or foam pad wrapped
around the lower leg and tied, without rigid material, can work for a stable
lower-leg injury when nothing rigid is available, though it gives less
protection against rotation.

**Generic long-bone splint (forearm, upper arm, lower leg):** Two rigid
pieces padded on the inside, placed on opposite sides of the limb (e.g.,
medial and lateral), long enough to cross the joints above and below, tied
in at least three places — above the injury, below the injury, and near
each end — avoiding direct pressure on the fracture site itself.

## Special cases: femur, pelvis, and spine

These three are generally **"protect and evacuate," not "DIY splint and
continue,"** because the failure mode is internal bleeding or spinal cord
injury, not a poorly aligned bone.

**Suspected femur fracture.** The thigh's large muscles can hide substantial
internal bleeding, and thigh muscle spasm around a fracture causes the bone
ends to override, increasing pain and blood loss. A rigid **traction splint**
(commercial, e.g., Sager or Hare-type, or improvised) applies gentle
longitudinal traction and is the standard field treatment where trained
providers and equipment are available — but **traction splints are
contraindicated if there is any suspicion of hip or pelvic fracture**,
because traction can pull the femur head into an already-unstable pelvis and
worsen the injury. Without training or a traction device, a padded
non-traction splint immobilizing the whole leg (from the ankle to well past
the hip, tied to the sound leg or a rigid pole on both sides) plus urgent
evacuation is the realistic field option. Watch closely for shock (pale,
cool, rapid pulse, anxiety/confusion) from internal blood loss.

**Suspected pelvic fracture.** High-energy mechanism plus pelvic pain, or
inability to bear weight without a clear leg injury, are the field tells.
This can cause severe, invisible internal bleeding. The priority is
**minimizing movement and pelvic motion**, not building a splint:

- Do not log-roll, sit up, or have the person walk to test it.
- Keep the legs together and gently supported (padding between the knees) to
  limit pelvic motion.
- Commercial pelvic binders (a padded circumferential band tightened to a
  specific, device-guided tension) are the trained-provider standard.
  Evidence on **improvised** binders (bedsheets, tarp straps) is mixed: at
  least one comparative study found improvised sheet-wrapping *increased*
  the rate of severe pelvic bleeding compared with a purpose-built binder,
  likely from uneven, excessive, or poorly controlled tension. If you are
  not trained in pelvic binder application, prioritize minimizing movement
  and rapid evacuation over improvising a binder.
- Treat for shock and evacuate as an emergency.

**Suspected spine or neck injury.** Guidance here has shifted meaningfully
over the last decade. Older first-aid teaching emphasized rigid backboards
and cervical collars; current **Wilderness Medical Society** guidelines take
a goal-oriented approach — *protecting the spinal cord*, not mechanically
recreating a hospital backboard — and note growing evidence that aggressive
rigid immobilization can itself cause harm (pressure injury, airway
compromise, pain, and delay) without proven benefit in mild injuries. Their
guidance allows an injured person to be carefully assessed for whether
spinal precautions can be safely discontinued in the field, using criteria
similar to:

- Alert, oriented, sober (no intoxication or significant distracting pain).
- No neck or back pain or tenderness on gentle exam.
- Normal strength, sensation, and pulses in all four limbs.

If any of these fail — or you're not confident assessing them — **minimize
movement of the head, neck, and torso as a unit**, keep the person still,
and get help to come to them rather than moving them, unless there's an
immediate hazard (fire, rising water, avalanche path) that forces a move.
When a move is unavoidable, keep the head, neck, and torso aligned and
move as a single unit with as many hands as available. This is a
"stabilize in place and get trained help" situation, not a DIY splinting
job — treat it with the same urgency as the other red flags above.

## Sling and swathe (shoulder, upper arm, collarbone)

A sling and swathe supports an injured arm/shoulder against gravity and
binds it to the torso for additional stability — used for suspected
shoulder, collarbone, or upper-arm injuries, often layered over a forearm
splint.

1. Support the arm with the elbow bent roughly 90° and the hand slightly
   higher than the elbow, palm toward the chest.
2. Form a sling from a triangular bandage (or a jacket hem, shirt pinned up)
   from the wrist, under the forearm, and up over the opposite shoulder,
   so the forearm and hand are cradled against the chest. Leave the fingers
   exposed for circulation checks.
3. Add a swathe: a wider band of cloth around the torso, over the sling and
   the injured arm, pinning the upper arm against the body. This is what
   controls shoulder rotation, not the sling alone.
4. Check that the wrist doesn't extend past the sling's edge (pressure on
   the wrist/ulnar nerve) and recheck circulation and sensation in the hand.

## Ongoing care while waiting for evacuation

- **Pain:** Whatever over-the-counter or prescribed analgesics are on hand
  (see medication guidance elsewhere in this wiki), used at labeled doses.
  Positioning, padding, and immobilization themselves reduce pain
  significantly — a well-built splint often does more for comfort than
  medication alone.
- **Cold, in the first 24–48 hours, if available:** Ice or a cold pack
  wrapped in cloth (never directly on skin), 15–20 minutes at a time, helps
  limit swelling and pain. Don't apply continuously — risk of cold injury to
  already-compromised tissue.
- **Elevation:** Above heart level where the splint and injury allow,
  to reduce swelling.
- **Rest the limb** — that's largely what the splint is for.

### Compartment syndrome — watch for it, don't wait for all the signs

Compartment syndrome is dangerous swelling and bleeding inside a closed
muscle compartment (common after lower-leg and forearm fractures, and can
follow a too-tight splint) that cuts off circulation to muscle and nerve
inside that compartment. Left untreated it causes permanent muscle and nerve
damage within hours and can require surgical fasciotomy or, eventually,
amputation. It is a genuine emergency, not a routine complication to expect.

Classic teaching describes "6 P's" — **pain, pallor, paresthesia,
pulselessness, paralysis, poikilothermia** (coldness) — but clinical sources
are consistent that **these do not all appear together, and pulselessness
and paralysis are late signs, not early warnings.** Don't wait for them.

- **Earliest and most reliable sign: pain that is severe, worsening, and out
  of proportion to the apparent injury** — especially pain made
  dramatically worse by passively stretching the fingers or toes (e.g.,
  gently extending the toes increases calf pain sharply). Pain not relieved
  by rest, elevation, or the usual dose of pain medication.
- A compartment that feels **tense or "wood-like"** to the touch compared
  to the other limb.
- Early **numbness/tingling** (paresthesia) distal to the injury.
- A pulse can still be present with early compartment syndrome — a normal
  pulse does **not** rule it out.
- If any of this develops: loosen the splint and any bandaging immediately,
  elevate to heart level (not above, which some guidance suggests can
  reduce arterial flow further), and treat it as an emergency requiring
  urgent evacuation. This can develop hours *after* the original injury,
  including after splinting — it's a reason for the periodic rechecks above
  to continue for as long as the person is off the arm/leg.

## Realistic expectations for evacuation

Field splinting is a bridge, not a destination. Realistically: open
fractures, pelvis/femur injuries, spine injuries, or any injury with
circulation/nerve compromise need to move toward emergency care as fast as
safely possible — carrying, sled, ATV, boat, or calling for helicopter
evacuation if that's genuinely available, not walking out on a splinted leg.
A well-splinted, neurovascularly intact simple limb injury can often tolerate
a slower, more careful evacuation, but still needs an actual clinician and,
in most fracture cases, imaging and follow-up care that a splint cannot
provide.

## Safety

- Never test for crepitus or "see how bad it is" by moving an obviously
  injured limb through its range of motion.
- Don't force a deformed limb straight; the exception (restoring circulation
  to a pulseless limb) is a trained, judgment-based intervention with real
  risk of harm, not a default step.
- Recheck circulation and sensation on a schedule — splints loosen, swell
  into, or shift during evacuation.
- Treat any open wound over a suspected fracture as a serious infection risk
  and prioritize evacuation timing accordingly.
- Watch for and treat shock (pale, cool, clammy skin, rapid weak pulse,
  anxiety, confusion) especially with femur, pelvis, or multiple fractures.
